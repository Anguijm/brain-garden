# What is psychological safety?

This is the plain-English primer on psychological safety: a short explainer, built around an
interview with Amy Edmondson (who coined the term), of what it is and how to build it. If you
want the fuller, more argued version, see [psychological safety](topics/leadership/psychological-safety); this is
the quick on-ramp.

FACT: this is a 2023 HBR.org explainer by Amy Gallo. (Gallo, "What Is Psychological Safety?")

## The short version

Assessment: psychological safety is a *shared* belief that a team is safe for interpersonal
risk, speaking up, asking questions, admitting mistakes, disagreeing, without fear of being
punished or humiliated. Edmondson's shorthand: "felt permission for candor." It is a property
of the *group*, not a personality trait.

FACT: it matters because it drives engagement, better decisions (more views get voiced), and
learning from mistakes, and Google's Project Aristotle found it was the single biggest factor
in team effectiveness.

## How to build it

![Diagram: a four-step loop for building psychological safety. Make clear why people's voices matter. Admit your own fallibility. Actively invite input (ask, don't wait). Respond productively (appreciation and curiosity, not blame). The loop feeds back into more candor.](img/build-psych-safety.png)
*Four moves that build psychological safety. Diagram.*

- **Make clear why voices matter** — people default to silence, so tell them why you need their
  input.
- **Admit your own fallibility** — own your mistakes so others can own theirs.
- **Actively invite input** — ask open questions ("What are you seeing? Where do you stand?"),
  don't wait for volunteers.
- **Respond productively** — meet a risky idea or hard truth with appreciation and curiosity;
  replace blame with "what did you learn?"

Assessment: two myths worth killing, safety is *not* about being "nice" (nice often means not
candid), and it's *not* about always feeling comfortable (learning is uncomfortable).

## How much to trust this

Assessment: the concept itself is one of the best-supported ideas in this library, and this
primer is an accurate, useful summary. The four moves are sound.

The honest caveat is just what this is: an explainer, not evidence. FACT: all the weight is
borrowed from Edmondson's research and Project Aristotle, asserted rather than detailed, and
one cited "diversity plus safety" finding is a single study in one industry. Assessment: use it
as a fast introduction; the deeper [Edmondson note](topics/leadership/psychological-safety) is the better source.

**Evidence check (2026 review, verdict: mixed).** A large 2017 meta-analysis, [Frazier et al. in *Personnel Psychology*](https://doi.org/10.1111/peps.12183) (136 samples, over 22,000 people), confirms psychological safety is positively linked to learning and performance, but the effect is only moderate and gets noticeably weaker when performance is measured objectively rather than by self-report. Later work by [Eldor et al. (2023) in *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2023.104255) even finds the link is nonlinear: too much of it can hurt routine-task performance. Treat the "single biggest factor" line as marketing, not settled science: it comes from Google's Project Aristotle, an internal study that was never peer-reviewed.
