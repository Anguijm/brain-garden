## Waterfront Ops & Production

### Rosyth keeps the carrier drumbeat

FACT: Babcock announced on April 22, 2026 that HMS Queen Elizabeth has been returned to
operational readiness after a planned maintenance program at Rosyth, the fourth dry
docking of a Queen Elizabeth-class carrier there in seven years, with sea trials next.
FACT: Babcock describes the work as spanning thousands of engineering hours across
propulsion, stabilization, and general repair and renewal on the 65,000-tonne ship.
Assessment: the useful benchmark for U.S. yards is the cadence, not the ship. A
predictable big-deck docking rhythm at a single yard, four evolutions in seven years,
is exactly the kind of repeatable interval the U.S. carrier enterprise struggles to
protect when dry docks are the constraint.

**Bottom line:** Watch Rosyth as a cadence benchmark: the win is not one docking, it is
the same yard turning the same class on schedule, four times running.

*Sources: [Babcock via Naval News, Apr 2026](https://www.navalnews.com/)*

### New dock in the Indian Ocean, first customer: a navy

FACT: Drydock Magazine reported in April 2026 that Réunion has commissioned the floating
dock Titan, able to dry-dock vessels up to 4,600 tons and 120 meters, and that its first
commercial docking is the French Navy support ship BSAOM Champlain, a roughly two-month
evolution run by Piriou Reunion. FACT: per the same report, the dock was bought
second-hand in 2024, refurbished in Cape Town, and carried to Réunion on a
semi-submersible; the pitch is European-standard repair capability in a region that had
none. Assessment: for Pacific and Indian Ocean sustainment planners, this is the pattern
to note: modest, second-hand dock capacity, positioned forward and anchored by a naval
tenant, stood up in about two years. The same play is available to allied yards closer to
the western Pacific.

**Bottom line:** Forward dock capacity does not have to mean a decade of MILCON; a
refurbished floating dock with a committed naval customer stood up in two years.

*Sources: [Drydock Magazine, Apr 2026](https://drydockmagazine.com/)*

### SIOP's waterfront push: what the money is buying

FACT: in a NAVSEA-published interview from a May 2024 shipyard visit, Program Executive
Officer for Industrial Infrastructure Mark Edelson said the Shipyard Infrastructure
Optimization Program had more than $6 billion of construction underway, more than $500
million of industrial plant equipment in procurement, and 138 pieces of new equipment
already delivered, with early years "heavily focused on dry docks." FACT: Edelson
organizes SIOP along three lines of effort (waterfront, facilities and utilities, and
industrial plant equipment) across the four public shipyards and their roughly 37,000
workers, and the program is standardizing equipment across all four yards' Shop 31s.
Assessment: the standardization detail matters more than the dollar figures for
mid-level managers, because common machines across yards mean transferable quals,
transferable planning products, and a real basis for cross-yard workload sharing.

**Bottom line:** As SIOP equipment lands, push for the cross-yard commonality benefits
(quals, procedures, spares) rather than treating each delivery as a local upgrade.

*Sources: [NAVSEA, May 2024](https://www.navsea.navy.mil/); [NAVSEA Shipyards page](https://www.navsea.navy.mil/Home/Shipyards/)*

### Commercial pace note

FACT: Drydock Magazine reported in late 2025 that Bahrain's ASRY yard redelivered the
tanker Torm Venture in 14 days against an expected 18-day window. Assessment: the
enablers commercial Gulf yards credit for that kind of compression (pre-staged material,
fixed work windows, dense trade manning at period start) are process choices, not
capital ones.

**Bottom line:** The 14-versus-18-day redelivery is a process story worth stealing even
where the capital plant can't be copied.

*Sources: [Drydock Magazine, Nov 2025](https://drydockmagazine.com/)*

## Maintenance Engineering & Technologies

### Additive manufacturing went to sea in 2025; 2026 is about the pipeline

FACT: NAVSEA's year-in-review states that in 2025 HII installed the first additively
manufactured metal valve manifold aboard a nuclear-powered aircraft carrier (a 1.5-meter,
450-kilogram assembly), the Virginia-class program installed a metal printed component,
and the AUKUS partners completed a shipboard installation of a metal 3D-printed part.
FACT: the same NAVSEA review credits Marotta with cutting a 29-week destroyer valve lead
time by 70 percent, a Southeast Regional Maintenance Center polymer part with over
$300,000 in cost avoidance, and Forward Deployed Regional Maintenance Center Rota with
cutting some repair times by 80 percent; NAVSEA also reports a collaboration with
academia cut additive testing requirements by more than 60 percent, with three
material specifications released (MIL-PRF-32802, -32803, -32804).

*Diagram: the additive pipeline in six reported figures (NAVSEA and SWRMC, 2024-2025).*

Assessment: the strategic change is not any single part, it is that parts are now moving
through an approval system with specs, trackers, and a supply-system endpoint. The
numbers above are all as reported by NAVSEA organizations, without independent audit, so
treat them as the program's own scorecard. Speculation: if the approved-parts list keeps
compounding, the first sustained effect managers will feel is in long-lead legacy
components, where a print becomes the alternate procurement path of record.

**Bottom line:** Stop evaluating AM part by part; the management question for 2026 is
whether your shop's candidates are in the approval pipeline at all.

*Sources: [NAVSEA 2025 review](https://www.navsea.navy.mil/); [SWRMC via NAVSEA](https://www.navsea.navy.mil/); [N-RAM via NAVSEA](https://www.navsea.navy.mil/)*

### The unglamorous infrastructure: N-RAM and the approved-parts list

FACT: NAVSEA describes N-RAM (Navy Resources for Additive Manufacturing) as a SharePoint
hub centralizing additive specs, standards, and policy, with a production tracker
following roughly 140 candidate parts from requirement through print and approval,
refreshed nightly, and more than 6,000 views since launch per NAVSEA deputy director Jay
Ong. FACT: Southwest Regional Maintenance Center reported that as of October 2024 the
NAVSEA approved-parts list held 182 items with about 600 more in engineering review, that
it now prints metal parts weighing over a ton, and that printing a motor fan costs
roughly $20 and a day versus up to $20,000 to order the parent pump unit. Assessment:
this is what maturation actually looks like: a boring hub, a tracker, and a review queue.
The 600-in-review number is the one to watch; if it converts at any reasonable rate, the
approved list triples.

**Bottom line:** Have your engineering department check the N-RAM tracker before starting
any local AM effort; duplicate candidates waste review capacity the queue can't spare.

*Sources: [NAVSEA on N-RAM](https://www.navsea.navy.mil/); [SWRMC via NAVSEA, 2024](https://www.navsea.navy.mil/)*

### Discovery Team: 90,000 man-days sitting on the table

FACT: NAVSEA's spring 2026 update on its Discovery Team, the "front door" linking the
four public shipyards to headquarters engineering, says the team identified 90,000
man-days of potential savings across the yards in its first year and has taken in more
than 250 improvement ideas, working three lanes: challenging overly stringent technical
requirements, inserting new methods, and securing funding. Assessment: the most valuable
lane is the first one. Requirements relief is the cheapest capacity there is, and a
formal channel for a deckplate planner to challenge a spec, with headquarters obligated
to answer, is a genuine culture mechanism, not a suggestion box. The 90,000 man-day
figure is the program's own estimate of *potential* savings, not audited realized
savings.

**Bottom line:** If your yard has a standing irritant requirement, the Discovery Team
channel now exists precisely for it; the queue rewards specific, costed submissions.

*Sources: [NAVSEA, spring 2026](https://www.navsea.navy.mil/)*

### Commercial retrofit watch: shaft generators earning their keep

FACT: The Maritime Executive reports that Maersk and Seaspan have launched an upgrade
program covering 18 chartered vessels with commitments of at least $75 million, headlined
by shaft generators, optimized propellers, and pre-swirl devices on four 13,000 TEU
ships, with an expected slot-cost improvement of 10 to 13 percent. FACT: in a Berg
Propulsion release carried by the same outlet, operator Chemfleet reported about 10
percent fuel savings from a propulsion-control retrofit on a chemical tanker, with the
control-system swap done in three days in service; both Berg items are vendor press
releases, which The Maritime Executive explicitly does not endorse. Assessment: numbers
from vendor releases deserve a discount, but the direction is consistent across
independent programs: mid-life efficiency retrofits, especially shaft generators, are
now routine commercial drydock scope. Navy auxiliaries with similar hull lives face the
same economics.

**Bottom line:** Expect efficiency-retrofit scope to keep migrating into routine
dockings; the commercial side is normalizing it at the 10-percent-savings scale, per
vendor-reported trials.

*Sources: [The Maritime Executive](https://www.maritime-executive.com/) (incl. two Berg vendor releases, so labeled)*

### Research radar: ending the switchboard open-door inspection

FACT: an NSRP-published briefing by RSL Fiber Systems (presented at the February 2025 All
Panels Meeting, project RA 2025-04 with NAVSEA 05Z33, NSWC Philadelphia, Austal USA, and
others) argues shipboard electrical inspections are a condition-monitoring gap: it cites
roughly $3 million per year in surface-fleet open-door switchboard inspection labor, an
average of eight arc faults per year across fleet switchboards, and $6 billion in
electrical-fire costs over twelve years. FACT: the proposed fix in the briefing is
fiber-optic distributed temperature sensing, the whole fiber acting as the sensor with
0.1-degree-Celsius rise detection, now being piloted on a DDG 51 land-based facility in
Philadelphia with an in-service installation targeted next. Assessment: the interrogator
technology is mature in data centers and fire detection; the shipboard question is
installation and maintenance economics, which is exactly what the pilot should answer.
Also on the radar: two 2024 arXiv papers on multi-robot wire-arc additive manufacturing,
and a 2025 study of multi-skilled workforce scheduling calibrated on a real hull-block
shop (results paywalled, so no numbers claimed here).

**Bottom line:** Watch the DTS pilot; if the DDG 51 installation holds up, periodic
open-door switchboard inspections become the next maintenance requirement worth
challenging through the Discovery Team.

*Sources: [NSRP RA 2025-04 briefing](https://www.nsrp.org/); [arXiv 2408.04677](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.04677); [arXiv 2408.04827](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.04827); [Computers & Operations Research, 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/)*

## Operations Maintenance & Readiness

### Fire safety: GAO says the contract has no teeth

FACT: GAO's December 2025 report on fire prevention during ship maintenance
(GAO-26-107716) counts 13 fires on ships in maintenance since 2008, notes the 2020
Bonhomme Richard arson fire caused over $3 billion in damage and early decommissioning,
and finds the Navy's contract oversight tools largely toothless: Corrective Action
Requests carry no monetary penalties, none of the six reviewed ships' quality assurance
plans assessed any penalty for safety noncompliance, and the ship-repair contractor
liability limit has not been adjusted since 2003. FACT: GAO made six recommendations
(including monetary-penalty options and reassessing the liability clause); the Navy
concurred with all six, with target completion January 2027. FACT: GAO's earlier 2023
fire report (GAO-23-105481) put maintenance-fire damages at more than $4 billion from
2008 to 2022; its training-goals recommendation remained open as of May 2025.
Assessment: no major fire since 2020 is real progress, and GAO credits the post-fire
culture push, but the report's core point stands: the deterrent structure is cultural,
not contractual, and culture is what erodes first under schedule pressure.

**Bottom line:** Between now and January 2027, expect penalty language and liability
terms to start appearing in availability contracts; contractors and RMC staff should
plan for QASP enforcement with actual consequences.

*Sources: [GAO-26-107716](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-107716); [GAO-23-105481](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-23-105481)*

### Forward MRO in Korea keeps expanding

FACT: Hanwha Ocean announced that the Chief of Naval Operations, Admiral Daryl Caudle,
visited its Geoje shipyard in November 2025, where USNS Charles Drew was undergoing
maintenance as the third U.S. Navy vessel in Hanwha's repair program, and the company
stated it intends to expand from logistics vessels to combatant maintenance and
eventually new construction, under the U.S.-Korea MASGA shipbuilding initiative.
Assessment: as of this writing the program remains auxiliaries-only, and combatant MRO
in a foreign yard would need policy movement, not just yard capacity. But three ships
in two years, a CNO visit, and an automated pipe plant purpose-built for the work is a
trajectory, and it is the single most concrete relief valve in the Pacific for the
surface-maintenance backlog. Speculation: expect the combatant-MRO question to surface
formally within the next budget cycle; the capacity argument is getting harder to
answer with "no."

**Bottom line:** Forward allied MRO is becoming a planning fact, not an experiment;
Pacific schedulers should model Korean-yard availability windows as real capacity with
a policy asterisk.

*Sources: [Hanwha Ocean via Naval News, Nov 2025](https://www.navalnews.com/)*

### The backlog, honestly framed

FACT: GAO testified in May 2024 that fewer than 40 percent of Navy ships were completing
maintenance availabilities on time, and USNI News reported the USS Boxer that year was
repaired pierside by divers because both adequate San Diego dry docks were occupied.
FACT: GAO's January 2025 surface-ship report (GAO-25-106990) notes roughly $25.9 billion
appropriated for combat surface ship maintenance across FY2020-2023, with persistent
shortfalls in spare parts and qualified personnel, and 46 sustainment recommendations
unimplemented as of September 2024. FACT: the Congressional Budget Office projected back
in 2021 that public-yard labor demand would exceed supply in 25 of 30 years, a shortfall
of roughly 295,000 labor-days per year, about one submarine overhaul's worth of work
falling behind annually. Assessment: these are different years' snapshots, not one
dataset, but they agree on the shape: money is not the binding constraint; docks and
people are, and both have decade-long lead times.

**Bottom line:** Treat dock windows and journeyman headcount as the planning currency;
appropriated dollars have not been the limiting factor for five straight years of
reporting.

*Sources: [USNI News, May 2024](https://news.usni.org/); [GAO-25-106990](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-25-106990); [USNI News on CBO, 2021](https://news.usni.org/)*

## Business Practices & Strategy

### GAO to Congress: strategy before steel

FACT: GAO's April 2026 testimony (GAO-26-109068) says Navy and Coast Guard shipbuilding
programs are collectively billions over cost and years behind schedule across two
decades, notes the Navy's 2025 strategic shift away from the Constellation-class frigate
after exercising more than $3 billion in contract options, and reports DOD cannot say
how much more funding the submarine industrial base needs beyond the more than $10
billion already invested. FACT: GAO added two recommendations (a full cost and schedule
assessment of reaching the one-Columbia-plus-two-Virginia yearly goal, and documented
oversight of industrial-base investments); DOD agreed, against a backdrop of 92 Navy
shipbuilding recommendations since 2016, many unaddressed. Assessment: for the repair
enterprise the relevant thread is the submarine industrial base number: sustainment and
new construction now compete for the same welders, machinists, and suppliers, and GAO is
saying nobody has priced that competition.

**Bottom line:** When the submarine industrial-base assessment lands, read it as a
repair-side document too; it will price the labor pool your availabilities draw from.

*Sources: [GAO-26-109068](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-109068)*

### $12.6 billion for the industrial base, still no strategy

FACT: GAO's report on ship repair industrial-base investment (GAO-25-106286) finds DOD
spent more than $5.8 billion on the shipbuilding industrial base from FY2014 to FY2023
and plans another $12.6 billion through FY2028 without full return-on-investment
visibility, and that the Navy plans direct repair-base investments without having
assessed how much dry-dock capacity it actually needs beyond peacetime. FACT: per GAO's
status updates, NAVSEA demonstrated a shipyard-investment-incentive data repository in
April 2026 but has not formalized the policy behind it, and GAO judged the America's
Maritime Action Plan too unspecific to count as the required strategy as of February
2026. Assessment: the repair base's complaint for years was an unstable demand signal;
GAO is now making the same complaint about the government's investment signal. The
repository demo is the first concrete artifact, and whether it becomes policy is a
2026 decision worth tracking by name.

**Bottom line:** Private-yard partners should watch the investment-incentive repository:
if it formalizes, it becomes the scoreboard that decides whose capital projects get
government co-investment.

*Sources: [GAO-25-106286](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-25-106286)*

### Allies double down: Australia prices its yard future

FACT: Naval News reported in April 2026 that Australia's new National Defence Strategy
roughly doubles projected defence spending by FY2035, raises AUKUS submarine investment
to A$71-96 billion, lifts the Collins-class life-extension budget to A$7.8-11 billion
citing worse-than-expected hull corrosion, and commits up to A$25 billion over a decade
to the Henderson Defence Precinct, whose first tranche funds expanded submarine
maintenance facilities including a new graving dock. Assessment: for U.S. Pacific
sustainment, Henderson is the line to watch. A new allied graving dock with submarine
maintenance scope, funded in-year, is capacity that eventually shows up in combined
maintenance planning, on the same timeline as SIOP's own dock work.

**Bottom line:** Put Henderson's graving dock on the same mental map as SIOP dock
projects; by the early 2030s Pacific submarine availability planning will count both.

*Sources: [Naval News, Apr 2026](https://www.navalnews.com/)*

### SIOP's money lesson, still compounding

FACT: GAO's 2023 SIOP report (GAO-23-106067) documented Pearl Harbor's dry-dock estimate
growing from $6.1 billion in 2018 to $16 billion by 2022, and Portsmouth's first SIOP
dry-dock project growing from a $528 million baseline to $2.2 billion, partly for lack
of competition, against an original 2018 program estimate of $21 billion over 20 years.
FACT: GAO also relayed the Navy's own warning that without infrastructure improvement it
cannot support almost a third of planned carrier and submarine maintenance periods
through 2040; two of three recommendations closed in 2023, with schedule best practices
due in the SIOP concept-of-operations update. Assessment: three years on, the lesson for
managers is unchanged and local: estimates made before final design are floors, and any
availability plan that assumes a SIOP dock arrives on the original date should carry its
own margin.

**Bottom line:** Build schedule margin around every SIOP-dependent dock date in your
long-range plan; the program's own history says early estimates do not hold.

*Sources: [GAO-23-106067](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-23-106067)*
