This issue is about where a hull can actually go, and who is allowed to work on it. The Navy budgeted
USS Harry S. Truman's midlife overhaul to start in June 2026; it is August, the ship has not moved, the
carrier ahead of it at the same yard has taken two thirteen-month slips in two budget cycles, and
planning alone has been obligated about half again what the contract was announced to be worth. On the
other coast Puget Sound is handing a carrier back on schedule, through the only West Coast dock that can
take one. The Navy is betting on prediction to fix its maintenance schedules, and has quietly changed
something that matters more than the algorithm. And the shipbuilding plan wants smaller, more frequent
availabilities while the surface force spends its money on port engineers and on taking intermediate
work back from contractors.

## Waterfront Ops & Production

### Two budget cycles, two thirteen-month slips, and a planning bill half again the announced figure

FACT: Stars and Stripes reported on August 10, 2026 that USS Harry S. Truman is at Naval Station Norfolk
"awaiting a move to start a multiyear midlife overhaul at HII Newport News Shipyard." HII is Huntington
Ingalls Industries, the only American builder of nuclear aircraft carriers. A midlife overhaul, written
RCOH in budget records, is the once-in-a-lifetime refit at mid-life that replaces the fuel in both
reactors and runs years. A ship's induction is the moment it stops being an operating unit and becomes
the yard's work; until then, everyone planning around it is planning against a date rather than a fact.

FACT: Janes reported on July 18, 2025, reading the fiscal 2026 budget documents, that "the budget
supports the planned start of RCOH work at HII's Newport News Shipbuilding in Virginia in June 2026,"
and that the work "is due to be completed in January 2031."

Assessment: that budgeted start has passed and the ship has not moved. **Unverified:** no revised start
date has been published by the Navy or the builder, and neither document that would settle it was
obtainable here: a Navy schedule statement, or the fiscal 2027 shipbuilding justification book, the volume in
which the Navy explains each ship line to Congress, which would not come off the comptroller's site in
time.

**What the award record shows.** FACT: HII announced on January 26, 2024 that Newport News Shipbuilding
"was awarded an advanced planning contract" for Truman's overhaul, "which has the total potential value
of \$913 million." FACT: the federal award record for that contract, N00024-24-C-2106, described as "CVN
75 RCOH ADVANCE PLANNING," shows total obligations of \$1,319,638,971 as of its most recent modification
on May 13, 2026. An obligation is money the government has legally committed and must pay; a "total
potential value" is the most a contract could be worth if every option is taken. They are different
measures, which is why the comparison below is stated carefully.

Assessment: obligations are about 45 percent above the value announced when the contract was let, and
the ship being planned for has not entered the yard. Advance planning is the work of writing the job
before the hull arrives: inspections, drawings, material buys. **Unverified:** this is not evidence of an
overrun. A contract's ceiling can be raised by modification, and the federal award record publishes no
potential-value field for this award, so what the current ceiling is cannot be established here. What the
record does show is that planning this overhaul has already cost half again what the announcement
implied, before the hull arrives.

**The ship in front of it.** FACT: USNI News reported on June 18, 2024, reading the fiscal 2025 budget
documents, that the overhaul of the preceding carrier at that yard, USS John C. Stennis, had been due to
finish "by August 2025" before that date was "pushed to the right by more than a year to October 2026,"
"an extension of almost 14 months." FACT: the same report quotes a NAVSEA statement attributing the
delay to "mandatory growth work following ship condition assessments, as well as industrial base
challenges." Growth work is work discovered after a ship is opened up. NAVSEA is the Naval Sea Systems
Command, which owns ship design, maintenance requirements and the public shipyards.

FACT: Janes reported that the fiscal 2026 request carries "USD483.1 million for the cost to complete"
Stennis's overhaul, "due to an additional 13-month delivery delay," and that the work "is scheduled to
be completed in November 2027."

Assessment: two consecutive budget cycles recorded two slips of roughly the same size on one ship,
August 2025 to October 2026 to November 2027, about twenty-five months of movement in two years of
documents, attributed on the record to discovered work and industrial-base capacity rather than any
single event. The same fiscal 2026 document left Truman's start at June 2026, so those dates together
plan roughly seventeen months in which two carrier overhauls run at that yard at once.

FACT: HII announced on April 8, 2024 that Stennis "was successfully moved to an outfitting berth at the
shipyard," a pier where remaining work is finished with the ship afloat rather than docked, after it "completed the dry dock portion of the refueling and complex overhaul." Assessment:
the dock has been clear since April 2024, so it is not what Truman is waiting on. The causes NAVSEA put on
the record are discovered work and industrial base challenges, neither of which a free dock relieves.

FACT: USNI News reported on February 24, 2025 that Truman "entered the emergent repair availability in
Souda Bay, Greece, on Feb. 16, four days after the aircraft carrier collided with a merchant vessel near
Port Said." An emergent availability is unscheduled repair driven by damage or failure, as against the
planned periods in a ship's maintenance plan. FACT: The War Zone reported on October 10, 2025 that a
Navy official said the collision damage "will get repaired during the ship's upcoming Refueling Complex
Overhaul (RCOH) at Newport News Shipbuilding as planned," and that at Souda Bay "new bulkheads were
installed inside each of the damaged spaces to establish weathertight integrity."

Assessment: work deferred into an overhaul inherits that overhaul's schedule. The forward-base repair
made the ship watertight and was never the permanent fix. When an induction slips it is not only the
original package that waits, it is everything anyone rolled into it.

**Bottom line:** the constraint at the only yard that overhauls carriers is people and discovered work
rather than dock space, planning alone has already been obligated about 45 percent above the announced
figure before the ship arrives, and the pressure is propagating from one hull to the next, so if your schedule depends on
Truman, track Stennis, because that is the ship in front of it.

### Puget Sound is delivering carrier availabilities on time, and it is one dock deep

FACT: Stars and Stripes reported on August 10, 2026 that Catherine Norton, public affairs officer for
Puget Sound Naval Shipyard and Intermediate Maintenance Facility, said "the docking planned incremental
availability for USS Ronald Reagan is on track for its scheduled completion at the end of August 2026,"
closing a seventeen-month servicing and repair period. A docking planned incremental availability, or
DPIA, is a routine scheduled period in which a carrier is docked so work can be done on the hull,
propellers and the machinery normally underwater. It is not the midlife refuelling overhaul the lead
article is about.

FACT: Stars and Stripes reported on April 2, 2025, in dated context as the work began, that the
Nimitz-class Reagan "has begun a maintenance stint that will keep it from deployment for 17 months."

Assessment: seventeen months from that report runs to roughly September 2026, and the yard says the work
finishes at the end of August, so the availability is landing on the number it was given at the start.
**Unverified:** whether it finished its work package or made the date by deferring scope into the next
availability is not published, and that distinction is the difference between a schedule kept and a
schedule moved.

**The problem is underneath the ship.** FACT: Military Times reported on July 22, 2026, quoting the
Navy's Multi-Mission Dry Dock project website, that "the only dry dock on the West Coast able to
accommodate a current Nimitz-class aircraft carrier is Dry Dock 6 (DD6), which does not meet seismic
resiliency standards and cannot fully accommodate Ford-class carriers," and that the Navy "does not
currently have the capacity and capability to perform depot-level maintenance on a Gerald R. Ford-class
carrier in the Pacific Northwest." FACT: the same report states the replacement project is sited in
existing Dry Dock 3 and Piers 6 and 7, with the second task order covering demolition of Dry Dock 3.

Assessment: that work ran through a single dock the Navy itself says does not meet its seismic standard
and cannot fully take the class the carrier fleet is moving to. No. 3 covered the replacement programme
as construction; the operational reading is shorter and harder. West Coast carrier docking is one asset
deep today, the replacement is a multi-decade project, and the interim plan runs through the dock being
replaced.

**Where this hull goes next.** FACT: the April 2025 report states that under Navy plans Truman would be
the next carrier to undergo a refuelling and complex overhaul, "which is only done at the Newport News
facility," and that Truman "would be followed by the Reagan and the last Nimitz carrier to be built, the
USS George W. Bush."

Assessment: the ship Puget Sound is handing back on schedule is third in a published queue at the yard
this issue's lead article shows has not started the first one. Finishing on time at Bremerton does not
shorten that queue, because the constraint sits at Newport News, and a hull arriving ready simply waits
there instead.

**Bottom line:** West Coast carrier maintenance is performing, and it is one dock deep, so if you work
Pacific Northwest hulls your schedule risk is not the yard's execution but the dock's availability and
its seismic standing; and if you work carrier hulls on either coast, the queue behind Truman now
contains a ship that is ready and waiting on a yard that is not.

## Maintenance Engineering & Technologies

### The Navy is betting on prediction, and quietly changed something that matters more

FACT: the Navy Shipbuilding Plan of May 2026 states that "we must fix our maintenance schedules with
disciplined planning measured to the hour," and that "to drive this change, we are accelerating a World
Class Planning approach which will heavily leverage Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Machine Learning
(ML) to transform maintenance from a reactive to a predictive process." FACT: the same plan states the
Navy is building "digital twins" to "virtually replicate its ships and simulate decades of wear," and
that these technologies "will identify bottlenecks and streamline the supply chain by predicting which
parts will be needed months in advance." A digital twin is a model of a specific ship kept current with
its real condition, so wear and repair options can be tried on the model instead of the hull.

FACT: the same plan states this maintenance work "will be performed through PAE Industrial Operations,"
a new Portfolio Acquisition Executive, meaning a single official accountable for a whole portfolio's cost
and schedule, combining "control of the Navy Regional Maintenance Centers, NAVSEA's Industrial Operations
Directorate, the four Naval Shipyards."

**The change that is easier to copy.** FACT: an April 15, 2026 account of the surface force's
maintenance management describes the legacy method: "once every three years, a training team comes
aboard to review requirements, assess performance, and ensure Sailors understand expectations. Thirteen
weeks later, they return to verify execution," after which they depart, "often not to be seen again for
another three years." FACT: the same account states that under the replacement, 3-M 365, "maintenance is
no longer evaluated periodically. It is monitored continuously," and that "using machine learning, 3-M
365 categorizes the inputs and identifies the true drivers behind maintenance gaps, whether parts
availability, equipment condition, operational commitments, or manning." The 3-M in the name is the
Navy's Maintenance and Material Management system, the record of what maintenance a ship owes and what
it has done.

Assessment: the change that does the work here is the measurement interval, not the algorithm. A reading
every three years is a point, not a trend, and two points are not a trend either. With a single reading
you cannot separate the ordinary variation of a working process from something that actually changed, and
treating one reading as a signal is the error that reliably makes outcomes worse. Continuous monitoring
is what makes the distinction available at all; the machine learning then sorts what it collects.

FACT: the same account reports one case in which "recurring delays tied to parts availability appeared
to be isolated across multiple ships," and in which, through the new system, "these delays are
aggregated and revealed as a systemic supply shortfall, enabling action at the enterprise level."

Assessment: that single example carries the argument. The same delays read as a run of unrelated
incidents at ship level and as one supply problem at fleet level, and nothing about the underlying facts
changed, only the level at which they were added up. A cause addressed at the wrong level gets addressed
again and again and never closed, which is what a yard is doing whenever it solves the same problem
afresh on the next project.

**And the part that is about people, not software.** FACT: the same account states that surface force
3-M representatives "are now aligned directly to individual ships, replacing the legacy model of large
assessment teams that deployed episodically across the fleet," each representative assigned "a small
group of ships, typically three to four," and onboard "at a minimum" monthly, and that "rather than
arriving to grade a command, they are there to develop it."

Assessment: that is the transferable mechanic, and it costs no software. Fewer projects per process
person, present on a regular short interval, with the job defined as developing the crew rather than
scoring it. A yard can do that with the people it already employs, and it is the reverse of how most
quality and process organisations are staffed, which is a large team arriving occasionally to audit.

**What to hold them to.** Assessment: "transform maintenance from a reactive to a predictive process" is
the claim to test, and the test is not whether the digital twin exists. A work package is the priced,
scheduled list of jobs for an availability, and it is locked at a fixed point before work starts; work
counts as unplanned by arriving after that lock. So prediction can move work earlier and leave the total
unchanged, while simply locking later would cut reported unplanned work without anything improving. Ask
which happened before crediting the tooling.

**Bottom line:** the durable idea here is not AI, it is that you cannot improve what you measure once
every three years, and you cannot see a systemic cause while it sits filed as thirty separate incidents;
so if you copy one thing, copy the staffing model, put your process people on three or four projects
apiece with a monthly rhythm, and add their findings up above the project before acting on any of them.

## Operations Maintenance & Readiness

### Smaller and more frequent sounds like more work, until you read what the surface force is buying

FACT: the Navy Shipbuilding Plan of May 2026 states that the Navy "will no longer attempt to restore all
capabilities and add upgrades in a single, lengthy maintenance availability," and that it is
"integrating operations and maintenance into a maintenance continuum that will use smaller, more
frequent availabilities to do needed work while increasing operational availability." An availability is
a scheduled maintenance period.

Assessment: read from a repair yard that looks like more hulls arriving more often, and a reason to
size the shop to it. On the surface-combatant side the 2026 record does not support that reading.

**The measured problem is not the yard.** FACT: the Congressional Budget Office, in a report dated
December 10, 2025 analysing destroyer and amphibious warfare ship maintenance from October 2010 to
September 2024, "projects that DDG-51 class destroyers will spend an average of nine years, or more than
a quarter of their planned service life, out of the fleet for maintenance," which is "more than twice as
long as estimated in their 2012 class maintenance plans." FACT: the same report states that "maintenance
events often take 20 percent to 100 percent longer than estimated in the Navy's final schedules for
those events," and lists likely causes reported by the Navy, the private shipyards, or both, among them
"late inspections and contract awards," "unexpected additional work and contract negotiations," "delays
in obtaining parts and materials," and "poor integration of modernization (which is performed separately
by contractors) with maintenance work."

FACT: the same report states that although CBO had previously found that capacity constraints at Navy
shipyards led to delays in nuclear ship maintenance, "CBO did not identify capacity constraints as a
driver of delays in maintenance for conventional ships."

Assessment: that last sentence separates the surface fleet from the carrier story in this issue. On
conventional ships the constraint CBO could measure is not the yard's ability to take the work. It is
the sequence wrapped around the work: inspect, award, negotiate, get parts, integrate somebody else's
modernization. Shortening an availability does not touch any of those, and inspections and contract
awards attach to each availability, so more availabilities means more occasions for them to be late.

**What the Navy says it wants.** FACT: Janes reported that in his keynote at the Surface Navy
Association National Symposium on January 13, 2026, Vice Adm. Brendan McLane, commander of Naval Surface
Forces and Naval Surface Force Pacific, said "on-time completion remains our number one goal as we drive
towards zero days of maintenance delay," and that the Navy is "surging expertise where it matters most,"
which he described as "investing port engineers in key hubs, doubling them on large-deck amphibs, and
funnelling Advanced Engineering Instructors to surface groups." Large-deck amphibs are the assault ships
that carry aircraft and landing craft; Advanced Engineering Instructors are the Navy's senior technical
trainers.

Assessment: the goal named is predictability, not brevity. A port engineer is the government's technical
manager for a ship's maintenance, the person who builds and defends the work package before the
availability starts. Buying more of them is buying planning capacity, which is where CBO put the problem
rather than where the welding happens.

**What the Navy actually did.** FACT: the Navy re-established Shore Intermediate Maintenance Activity
commands at Norfolk on June 2, 2026 and at Naval Base San Diego on June 4, 2026, and stated that the
re-establishment "is part of an effort to increase Navy self-sufficiency by putting maintenance back in
the hands of Sailors, empowering their shore tours with hands-on experience, and reducing wait times for
intermediate-level maintenance previously performed by contractors." Intermediate-level maintenance is
the tier between what a crew does alongside and the depot work a shipyard does with a ship opened up.

Assessment: the Navy put the competitive claim in its own announcement, that the work being recaptured
is work contractors have been doing. Set against the plan's smaller-and-more-frequent language, the
shape that emerges is more maintenance events overall, with the small and frequent end of them
increasingly performed by sailors at Norfolk and San Diego rather than bought from a yard.

**The evidence that cuts the other way.** FACT: the same CBO report states that delays "have continued
to increase—especially for older ships, which have longer scheduled overhauls." FACT: the Navy reported
on May 13, 2026 that its Ship Repair Facility and Japan Regional Maintenance Center in Yokosuka "has
delivered all seven of its completed ships to the fleet on time or ahead of schedule" that year, having
returned two mine countermeasures ships, three destroyers and an amphibious transport dock between
January and April.

Assessment: CBO's own finding that the worst delays sit on old hulls with long overhauls is an argument
for breaking those overhauls up, so the policy has a defensible basis, and Yokosuka shows on-time
surface delivery happening at a real command rather than in a plan. Neither changes who performs the
small, frequent work, which is the question that decides how much of it reaches a yard at all.

**Bottom line:** do not size your yard to an expected surge of availabilities, because what the surface
force funded this year is planning capacity, parts and sailor-performed intermediate maintenance, and the
Navy describes the last of those as taking back work previously done by contractors. Put the effort into
your own work-package planning and material readiness before each availability opens, expect the small
and frequent end of the work to thin, and weight your capacity toward the depot-level jobs a shore
intermediate activity cannot do. And treat a late award as a planning problem rather than a contracting
one: the later it lands, the less time your package has to settle before it locks.
