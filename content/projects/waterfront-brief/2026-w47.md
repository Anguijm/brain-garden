This week's lead puts the Senate's complaint about canceled Navy repair money against Treasury's own books, which point to a larger closing in September 2027. The second sets the Navy's request for 2,845 more ship repair civilians against Senate appropriators' doubt that the department can hire that fast. The third traces who owns the data a yard needs to fix a ship, from a 1984 law to this year's two defense bills, and where we expect the fight to end. The watch page carries a year and a half of fuller yards in the Navy's plan.

## Business Practices & Strategy

### Senate appropriators call the Navy's canceled repair money excessive, and Treasury's books point to a larger closing in 2027

Senate appropriators say the Navy lets too much ship repair money cancel. Treasury's books show the Navy's operations account canceling \$815 million to \$1.12 billion at every closing for a decade, and in the three years we measured a smaller share than the Army or Air Force. The money that closes next, in September 2027, is carrying more unpaid than fiscal 2019 or 2020 money did at the same point. The books cannot show ship repair's part of it, and a new Senate report would show it only for money from fiscal 2024 on.

The complaint comes without a number. [Senate appropriators' explanatory statement, released Nov. 16, orders](https://web.archive.org/web/20261118010806/https://www.appropriations.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/def_explanatory_stmt.pdf) a report within 180 days of enactment on "the factors contributing to the excessive cancellation of ship depot maintenance funds." It names no amount. The same statement funds ship depot maintenance at the full \$14.29 billion requested, and it is not law.

Canceled money is money left in an account when the law closes it. [Federal law requires](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/1552) that an account close on Sept. 30 of the fifth fiscal year after its money stops being available for new obligations, and that "any remaining balance (whether obligated or unobligated) in the account shall be canceled." So money for fiscal 2020 closed on Sept. 30, 2025, and Treasury's statement for fiscal 2025 shows what was left.

The Navy's amount has barely moved. Treasury's year-end statements for [fiscal 2023](https://fiscal.treasury.gov/system/files/files/reports-statements/combined-statement/cs2023/c13.xlsx), [2024](https://fiscal.treasury.gov/system/files/files/reports-statements/combined-statement/cs2024/c13.xlsx) and [2025 show](https://fiscal.treasury.gov/system/files/files/reports-statements/combined-statement/cs2025/c13.xlsx) that the Navy's operations account canceled \$879 million, \$1.11 billion and \$877 million of its fiscal 2018, 2019 and 2020 money. [Its statements back to fiscal 2016 show](https://fiscal.treasury.gov/system/files/files/reports-statements/combined-statement/cs2016/c13.xlsx) every closing between \$815 million and \$1.12 billion.

The other services cancel more. By our arithmetic, setting those amounts against each year's appropriation in the [fiscal 2018](https://fiscal.treasury.gov/system/files/files/reports-statements/combined-statement/cs2018/c13.xlsx), [2019](https://fiscal.treasury.gov/system/files/files/reports-statements/combined-statement/cs2019/c13.xlsx) and [2020 statements](https://fiscal.treasury.gov/system/files/files/reports-statements/combined-statement/cs2020/c13.xlsx), the Navy canceled 1.7, 2.1 and 1.5 percent of each year's money for those three years, against the Army's 2.9, 2.9 and 5.0 percent, and the Air Force 2.5, 2.4 and 2.5 percent.

For money up to fiscal 2020, whatever the Senate counts as excessive is not new, and it is not out of line with the other services. It concerns the tail of five-year-old money, not this year's spending.

A late bill on closed money lands on this year's. [The next section of the law provides](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/1553) that such a bill may be charged to "any current appropriation account of the agency available for the same purpose," up to "1 percent of the total appropriations for that account." So a late bill on closed money is paid out of money meant for this year's work.

The Senate wants the money traced by ship. [The Senate statement requires](https://web.archive.org/web/20261118010806/https://www.appropriations.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/def_explanatory_stmt.pdf) a report 45 days after each quarter of fiscal 2027. For each ship with a planned availability, it shows money enacted, obligated and spent, the share of work completed, deferred, canceled or rescheduled, and "an explanation for any schedule variance exceeding 30 days."

Its by-ship funding table covers the current year and the three before it, so it would show ship repair's money from fiscal 2024 on, but not the fiscal 2022 money that closes next.

The next closing is running high. [Treasury's fiscal 2025 statement shows](https://fiscal.treasury.gov/system/files/files/reports-statements/combined-statement/cs2025/c13.xlsx) fiscal 2022 Navy operations money, which closes Sept. 30, 2027, still holding \$2.55 billion unpaid. At the same point, two years before closing, fiscal 2019 money held \$1.66 billion and fiscal 2020 money \$1.56 billion, [the fiscal 2022](https://fiscal.treasury.gov/system/files/files/reports-statements/combined-statement/cs2022/c13.xlsx) and [2023 statements show](https://fiscal.treasury.gov/system/files/files/reports-statements/combined-statement/cs2023/c13.xlsx). About two-thirds and a little over half of those balances went on to cancel. At those rates, fiscal 2022 money would cancel \$1.4 billion to \$1.7 billion, more dollars than any closing in the decade's record. Part of the rise is a larger account: the fiscal 2022 appropriation was \$64.5 billion, so its unpaid balance is about 4 percent of it, against about 3 percent for fiscal 2019 and 2020. We expect Treasury's fiscal 2027 statement to show more than \$1.2 billion of fiscal 2022 Navy operations money canceled. We're wrong if it shows less.

**Bottom line:** Do not take the Navy's steady past cancellations as the rate for the fiscal 2022 money now closing. It carries about 60 percent more unpaid dollars than fiscal 2019 or 2020 money did at the same point, and 30 to 50 percent more as a share of its year's money, and any late bill on it after Sept. 30, 2027 is paid from current money.

### The Navy wants 2,845 more ship repair civilians next year, while Senate appropriators doubt the department can hire that fast

The Navy's 2027 budget asks for 2,845 more civilians in ship maintenance, a year after cutting positions there under an executive order. Senate appropriators say the department's planned civilian growth cannot be hired on current timelines, and would pay the Navy for less. The Navy's own record before the order says it might: in 2025 the line came within 312 of its request.

The ask is large. The Navy asks for 46,773 full-time equivalents, a year of paid work each, on its ship maintenance line in 2027, [its 2027 budget book shows](https://web.archive.org/web/20260927025227/https://www.secnav.navy.mil/fmc/fmb/Documents/27pres/OMN_Book.pdf) (p. 154). The line pays the four naval shipyards, the regional maintenance centers and Kings Bay's Trident refit facility. That is 2,845 above its 2026 estimate of 43,928, and 442 above the 46,331 it actually paid in 2025. It includes 618 at Portsmouth for USS California and USS Columbia and 518 at Pearl Harbor for USS Indiana and USS Minnesota (p. 131).

Part of the 2026 low was planned. [The Navy's 2026 book](https://web.archive.org/web/20250709201504/https://www.secnav.navy.mil/fmc/fmb/Documents/26pres/OMN_Book.pdf) cut the line's civilian positions by 1,177 under Executive Order 14210, in three entries of 19, 175 and 983 (p. 156), and Congress then took \$223.6 million from its civilian pay, both reported here Nov. 6. The 2027 book's estimate still falls 1,465 short of that reduced 45,393 request.

Before the order, the line was catching up. By our count, the Navy's books for [2024](https://web.archive.org/web/20230328150351/https://www.secnav.navy.mil/fmc/fmb/Documents/24pres/OMN_Book.pdf) through [2027](https://web.archive.org/web/20260927025227/https://www.secnav.navy.mil/fmc/fmb/Documents/27pres/OMN_Book.pdf) put its actual counts for fiscal 2022 to 2025 short of the requests in [the 2022](https://web.archive.org/web/20210529143705/https://www.secnav.navy.mil/fmc/fmb/Documents/22pres/OMN_Book.pdf) to [2025 books](https://web.archive.org/web/20240329012805/https://www.secnav.navy.mil/fmc/fmb/Documents/25pres/OMN_Book.pdf) by 1,811, then 1,506, 453 and 312.

The Senate bets against the hiring. [Senate appropriators' explanatory statement, released Nov. 16,](https://web.archive.org/web/20261118010806/https://www.appropriations.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/def_explanatory_stmt.pdf) takes \$413.6 million from Navy operations (p. 56), saying the department's budgeted civilian growth "is not executable under current hiring timelines and processes" (p. 48). [The House Appropriations Committee's report](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CRPT-119hrpt715/html/CRPT-119hrpt715.htm) takes \$400 million from the same account in its Navy operations table. Neither is law, and [the stopgap](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/6500/text) runs to Dec. 11.

The rolls are refilling. As we reported Nov. 6 from [Office of Personnel Management records](https://data.opm.gov/get-data/api-access), the Navy's repair trades ended September within 100 of their 2024 size.

That does not contradict the budget's low estimate. The count is trades workers on the rolls in September. The budget is a year of paid time across every civilian on the line, estimated in the spring, so a slow first half holds the year down after the rolls refill. The 2027 actual count, printed in the Navy's 2029 book, is the test of who is right. The 2025 record shows the line can come within 1 percent of a plan, but this one starts 2,845 behind with both bills paying for less. We put the 2027 actual below 46,773 at about 80 percent. We're wrong if it reaches 46,773.

**Bottom line:** Build 2027 labor plans at the public yards, maintenance centers and Kings Bay on no more than the 46,331 civilians the line paid in 2025, and nearer the 2026 estimate of 43,928 until the count recovers, not on the 46,773 requested. Count the 2,845 added positions, 1,136 of them for the Portsmouth and Pearl Harbor submarine work, as people still to be hired.

### Congress would loosen makers' hold on the drawings the Navy needs to fix ships, but leaves a back door

Today a company that paid to develop a part can bar the Navy from passing its manufacturing drawings to the other firms that repair Navy ships. The House and Senate versions of the fiscal 2027 defense authorization bill would loosen that hold in two ways and tighten one thing the Navy already has. Our read: the final law trades a gain for a loss, drawings for wartime repair against a guaranteed right to maintenance data.

The restriction is on drawings, not manuals. Under [the technical data law, 10 U.S.C. 3771](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2024-title10/html/USCODE-2024-title10-subtitleA-partV-subpartD-chap275-subchapI-sec3771.htm), a contractor may restrict data on items developed "exclusively at private expense," a split dating to a 1984 act. Data "necessary for operation, maintenance, installation, or training" cannot be restricted; [the Government Accountability Office (GAO) calls these](https://files.gao.gov/reports/GAO-25-107468/index.html) "unlimited rights." But that carve-out excludes "detailed manufacturing or process data," the drawings and process steps another firm needs to make or rebuild a part. The law already allows release "necessary for emergency repair and overhaul."

So the Navy can usually get the manuals. It often cannot get the drawings.

The cost shows up at the pier. A private repair contractor would not replace a broken crane motor on a Littoral Combat Ship without the original maker present, and maintainers waited two and a half weeks, [GAO reported in September 2025](https://files.gao.gov/reports/GAO-25-107468/index.html). Shipyard officials called that "a quick turnaround." GAO asked Congress to clarify how manufacturing data needed for maintenance is treated.

Congress has tried workarounds. The fiscal 2025 act ordered a process to make parts "through reverse engineering or re-engineering" when their data is missing or restricted, [the law's notes show](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2024-title10/html/USCODE-2024-title10-subtitleA-partV-subpartD-chap275-subchapI-sec3771.htm). Unverified: our search on Nov. 20 found no report on it.

Last year's negotiators held back. They dropped a House rule on "fair and reasonable access to all repair materials," writing that the problems "are not rooted in an insufficiency in the law," [their joint explanatory statement says](https://armedservices.house.gov/uploadedfiles/fy26_ndaa_joint_explanatory_statement.pdf). They kept only a system to track technical data and find programs short of it.

The first change loosens the hold on drawings in wartime. [Section 861 of the House-passed bill](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-119hr8800eh/html/BILLS-119hr8800eh.htm) and [section 804 of the Senate committee's bill](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-119s4784rs/html/BILLS-119s4784rs.htm) would let the government release manufacturing data for maintenance "supporting wartime operations or contingency operations." The release needs a finding that the maker "will be unable to satisfy" readiness needs.

The second shifts the burden to the maker. Both bills say data delivered to the government carries government purpose rights unless the contractor proves more "through clear and convincing evidence"; the Senate limits this to noncommercial items. Government purpose rights let the government share data with other firms for government work, such as a repair yard.

The third tightens what the Navy gets today. Both bills restate the maintenance carve-out as government purpose rights "unless the Government and the contractor negotiate different license rights." [House section 824](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-119hr8800eh/html/BILLS-119hr8800eh.htm) goes further. It rewrites the same paragraphs so that all four of today's carve-outs, and the emergency release, apply "unless otherwise negotiated," and it narrows the form, fit and function carve-out to exclude manufacturing data. A contract could then trade away data the Navy gets by law today.

Timing differs by chamber. The House applies all of its section 861 changes to competitive solicitations issued after enactment and to sole-source awards made after it, [its applicability clause says](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-119hr8800eh/html/BILLS-119hr8800eh.htm). The Senate text and House section 824 set no start date.

Industry says the Pentagon is cool on the burden shift. Department officials are not saying "we don't like this at all," [the Aerospace Industries Association's Matt Murphy told Breaking Defense](https://breakingdefense.com/2026/11/defense-industry-takes-aim-at-right-to-repair-as-ndaa-decision-looms/). But "they are recognizing that default government purpose rights is a very broad stroke approach to this problem," he said.

As written, the bills loosen more than they tighten. As we expect them to pass, they trade even. The wartime release and the negotiable carve-out read the same in Senate section 804 and House section 861, so we expect both in the final act. The House text also argues with itself: its section 824 rewrites the same paragraphs without the wartime release, so negotiators must pick one version. The burden shift is what industry is fighting and last year's negotiators passed on, so we expect it dropped or narrowed. We're wrong if the final act keeps the default as either chamber wrote it, or drops the wartime release.

**Bottom line:** Plan maker-held parts under today's rules until a final act sets its start date, and count on rights only to data a contract delivers. For a part stopping a ship now, use the emergency repair release.

## On the watch

### One thing to watch

**The Navy's plan holds a step more ships in repair at once for a year and a half than in the last three years.** By our count from [the Navy's April 2026 operations budget book](https://web.archive.org/web/20260927025227/https://www.secnav.navy.mil/fmc/fmb/Documents/27pres/OMN_Book.pdf), 50 to 55 ships sit in a scheduled repair period at the middle of each quarter for six straight quarters, from late 2026 into early 2028. The same count from the [fiscal 2024](https://web.archive.org/web/20230328150351/https://www.secnav.navy.mil/fmc/fmb/Documents/24pres/OMN_Book.pdf), [2025](https://web.archive.org/web/20240329012805/https://www.secnav.navy.mil/fmc/fmb/Documents/25pres/OMN_Book.pdf) and [2026 books](https://web.archive.org/web/20250709201504/https://www.secnav.navy.mil/fmc/fmb/Documents/26pres/OMN_Book.pdf) gives 43 to 51 in every quarter from late 2023 to mid-2026, an average of 47.7 against the plan's 52.5. The rise comes in one step in late 2026 and holds for those six quarters. Past early 2028 the count thins, as every year's list does beyond its own money.

**Bottom line:** Plan 2027 crews, piers and supervisors around about five more ships in repair at any moment than the last three years' average, held for six quarters, not a surge to ride out.
