This issue covers four stories and two things to watch. The first is Norfolk Naval Shipyard
reorganising itself around single points of accountability, read against what the research
says a reorganisation can and cannot do. The second is the promise made under that structure,
the Ford back in six months with the fire run as a second availability. The third is two
decisions that moved a date by moving scope, at San Diego and at Newport News. The fourth is a destroyer that lost power in
the South China Sea and the Philippine yard that took it, which is now getting a longer pier.

## Business Practices & Strategy

### Norfolk gave every shop one owner, and the research says that is the easy half

Norfolk Naval Shipyard has redrawn its command structure so that one person answers for
production, one for nuclear work, and one each for support, business and the command itself. It
is the first public yard to reorganise around the sustainment programme it has been running for
five years, and it has put a date on the wall to be judged by.

FACT: [Norfolk announced on September 2 that](https://www.dvidshub.net/news/573775/norfolk-naval-shipyard-launches-command-and-control-c2-reorganization) it "has recently launched" a Command and Control
reorganisation under a five-person Executive Steering Committee that "provides single points of
accountability across the shipyard's focal areas," per the shipyard's release on DVIDS. FACT: the
same release puts Codes 200, 300C, 300S, 700 and 900 under an Executive Director of Production
Operations, Capt. Bart Sievenpiper, as "the single accountable person for production execution
across the shipyard," puts nuclear engineering, radiological controls and nuclear production
(Codes 105, 300N and 2309) under a separate director, Jeremy Largey, and groups industrial
support, business and command support under three more. FACT: the shipyard commander, Rear Adm.
Kavon Hakimzadeh, said the structure was designed "to establish clear, unified chains of command
so that the right 'supported and supporting' relationships are set," and Largey said "in this
initial pilot over the last year, we saw key areas of improvement that needed to be made," per
the same release. Supported and supporting is the military's way of saying who a task belongs to
and who serves it.

The structure serves an execution model the yard has been building for a year. FACT: [Norfolk's
July 8 release on the Ford's arrival](https://www.navy.mil/Press-Office/News-Stories/display-news/Article/4537840/norfolk-naval-shipyard-welcomes-uss-gerald-r-ford-for-its-first-planned-increme/) described its Focus and Finish model as "starting
availability jobs and urgently working them to completion to minimize interruptions and delays,"
and quoted a project official on using it "to minimize multitasking, drive scheduling discipline,
and rapidly close out work areas." FACT: [the yard said on July 1 that](https://www.dvidshub.net/news/569156/focus-and-finish-mechanic-workshops-small-changes-lead-time-saving-results) its transformation office
runs Focus and Finish mechanic workshops "where cross-functional stakeholders work together to
streamline small-scope processes," about one every two weeks, developed after a visit to
Portsmouth Naval Shipyard, with the first sessions joined by Portsmouth, Puget Sound and Pearl
Harbor and aimed at jobs the size of a freeze seal or a valve repack, per DVIDS. FACT: the yard
launched a monthly State of the Shipyard forum on August 26, streamed to the workforce, at which
the reorganisation was briefed, [per its September 2 release](https://www.dvidshub.net/news/573808/norfolk-naval-shipyards-new-state-shipyard-forum-works-align-workforce-and-support-mission).

The results it points to are carrier dates. FACT: [Norfolk said on April 24 that](https://www.dvidshub.net/news/563538/uss-dwight-d-eisenhower-completes-planned-incremental-availability-norfolk-naval-shipyard) USS Dwight D.
Eisenhower's planned incremental availability was "the SECOND consecutive early finish of an
aircraft carrier availability" at the yard, after Bush in November 2024, and that the team
executed "more than 25,000 resource days of new work" while "saving 2,000 resource days," per
DVIDS. A resource day is one person working one day.

**What the research says a structure can do.** FACT: Casciaro, Edmondson and Jang wrote in
[Harvard Business Review in 2019](https://hbr.org/2019/05/cross-silo-leadership) that "one way to break down silos is to redesign the formal
organizational structure. But that approach has limits: It's costly, confusing, and slow. Worse,
every new structure solves some problems but creates others." FACT: the same article reports that
in studies of more than 2,000 teams, diverse teams that contained a "cultural broker," a person
who works both sides of a boundary, significantly outperformed teams without one, and names four
practices that cross silos: brokers, asking the right questions, taking the other side's
perspective, and knowing where the expertise is.

Assessment: the reorganisation fixes the vertical problem, who owns production, and the research
says the interfaces are horizontal. The seam between production and nuclear did not go away on
September 2; it now runs between two directors instead of through a matrix, and a valve repack
still needs the machinist, the nuclear engineer and the tester in the same week. Norfolk's
workshops are the broker mechanism the research describes, small, cross-functional and frequent,
and they predate the boxes, which is the right order. The prediction is that the structure shows
up in the numbers the yard already publishes, resource days saved on the next carrier and the
next submarine, and that the other three public yards adopt the directorate model within a
year, because the Naval Sustainment System programme they all sit in rewards what Norfolk can
show. The Ford's date is the wrong test of the structure, for the reasons the next article
gives. The risk is the one the research names: a structure that solves
accountability creates a new problem at the seams it draws, and the seams here are nuclear.

**Bottom line:** before redrawing your own chart, find the people your yard already uses to get a
job across a code boundary and give them a two-week cadence and a small scope; give production
one owner only when the seams it creates, nuclear first, have a named broker on each side; and
watch Norfolk's resource-day numbers, not its org chart, for whether this worked.

## Operations Maintenance & Readiness

### The Ford in six months: the promise, the mechanism and the arithmetic against it

Norfolk's commander has promised the fleet a carrier back in six months after the longest
deployment since Vietnam and a fire, and the way he intends to keep it is to run the fire as a
second availability on the same ship. The date was set before either happened, and the plan has
not said where the growth goes or who integrates two availabilities on one hull.

FACT: USS Gerald R. Ford arrived at Norfolk on July 7 for its first planned incremental
availability, the first time a Ford-class carrier has been serviced at the yard, and "a
concurrent availability will be conducted to restore spaces damaged during a fire aboard the
then-deployed carrier in March," [per the Navy's July 8 release](https://www.navy.mil/Press-Office/News-Stories/display-news/Article/4537840/norfolk-naval-shipyard-welcomes-uss-gerald-r-ford-for-its-first-planned-increme/).
A planned incremental availability is a carrier's scheduled maintenance period between
deployments. FACT: [USNI News reported on July 14](https://news.usni.org/2026/07/14/carrier-uss-gerald-r-ford-starts-first-major-post-deployment-maintenance-period)
that such availabilities "are typically scheduled for six to eight months" and that the fire
started in the aft laundry and spread to berthing.

FACT: [Breaking Defense reported on September 14](https://breakingdefense.com/2026/09/despite-fire-lengthy-deployment-navy-expects-only-six-month-maintenance-for-uss-ford/)
that Rear Adm. Hakimzadeh said "it was 10 months, I promised the Fleet commander we could do it
in six months. So we set that in place while she was on deployment before she had a fire... I'm
still going to deliver that ship back in six months, but we've set up a second availability
that is overseen by the regional maintenance center with a commercial partner, with a
commercial vendor, that is running that availability." FACT: the same report says he allowed
for "follow up" and "clean up" at the end, that the ship spent 326 days at sea, and that the
acting Secretary of the Navy, Hung Cao, told House appropriators in May that "for every 30 days
that the ship is extended on deployment, it adds 6 percent of maintenance, so five months extra
would add 30 percent for maintenance."

Assessment: the six-month figure was set before the deployment ended and before the fire, and
nothing announced since has added time to it, while the Navy's own rule of thumb adds 30 percent
of maintenance for the extension alone, growth that lands in the planned package, not the fire
one. A vendor's people speed work only where the work is separable, and a laundry and berthing
rebuild is not separable from the ship: its power, ventilation and firemain run through systems
the yard is working in the same months, under the same tag-outs and the same test programme.
Two availabilities on one hull is two work-authorisation and test streams on shared systems,
and nothing announced says who integrates them. The prediction is that
the planned availability closes near its date, that what does not fit moves into the fire
availability and the "clean up," and that the ship is not back with the fleet in six months in
the sense the fleet commander was promised. If it is, the desk will say so.

**Bottom line:** do not copy this yet. The questions for Norfolk, and for anyone proposing the
same on your pier: who integrates the two work-authorisation and test streams at the systems
they share, where the 30 percent from the extension is provisioned, and which of the two dates
the fleet was promised. Watch the Ford for two dates, not one.

*Sources: [U.S. Navy, Jul 8, 2026](https://www.navy.mil/Press-Office/News-Stories/display-news/Article/4537840/norfolk-naval-shipyard-welcomes-uss-gerald-r-ford-for-its-first-planned-increme/); [USNI News, Jul 14, 2026](https://news.usni.org/2026/07/14/carrier-uss-gerald-r-ford-starts-first-major-post-deployment-maintenance-period); [Breaking Defense, Sep 14, 2026](https://breakingdefense.com/2026/09/despite-fire-lengthy-deployment-navy-expects-only-six-month-maintenance-for-uss-ford/)*

### Two decisions that moved a date by moving scope

An amphibious ship undocked on time in San Diego because the yard contracted for growth work
before it found any, and a new submarine skipped its post-delivery repair period because its
trials were clean. Both are scope decisions, and both defer something.

FACT: [Southwest Regional Maintenance Center said on September 15 that](https://www.navsea.navy.mil/Media/News/Article-View/Article/4601275/swrmc-undocks-uss-america-on-time/) USS America (LHA 6)
undocked on time on August 11 in its Chief of Naval Operations availability, and quoted the
project manager, Jorge Mota, crediting "proactive risk mitigation, disciplined planning, and an
agile contracting strategy designed to address potential growth work early," per NAVSEA; the
same release defines growth work as "additional repairs identified once systems and structural
compartments are opened" and says the availability completes in 2027. FACT: [NASSCO announced in
May 2025 that](https://nassco.com/featured/general-dynamics-nassco-san-diego-awarded-fy26-docking-selected-restricted-availability-contract/) it was awarded a \$198,955,270 firm-fixed-price contract for the availability,
with options to \$209,233,949 and completion expected by July 2027. FACT: [GAO reported in May
2020 that](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-20-370) the Navy's multiple-award contracting strategy for ship repair relies on fixed-price
awards, and described a congressional pilot meant to "mitigate schedule delays typically
associated with growth work," per GAO-20-370.

FACT: [the Navy said on August 17 that](https://www.navy.mil/Press-Office/Press-Releases/display-pressreleases/Article/4574915/uss-massachusetts-ssn-798-ready-for-fleet-t) it waived the post-shakedown availability of USS
Massachusetts (SSN 798), delivered by Newport News in November 2025 and commissioned in March,
with the Chief of Naval Operations saying the decision "allows our shipbuilding workforce to
focus its talent and capacity where we need it most, building the next generation of
submarines," and Vice Adm. Rob Gaucher calling it "a calculated risk." A post-shakedown
availability is the yard period after delivery in which deficiencies found in a new ship's
first months are corrected. FACT: [GAO reported in 2017 that](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-17-418) the Navy "regularly accepts ships
with numerous deficiencies," that it "pays for the vast majority of shipbuilder-responsible
deficiencies discovered after the Navy takes delivery," and that across six ships the count of
significant construction deficiencies fell from 363 at delivery to 45 by the time the ships
were provided to the fleet, per GAO-17-418.

Assessment: SWRMC has named a method and not described it, and under a fixed-price award growth
work is priced as it is found, which is the delay GAO describes, so "address growth early" most
likely means growth capacity priced or optioned before the ship opened; that is this desk's
inference, and an undocking date is not a completion. The Massachusetts decision is clearer: a
post-shakedown period is where a new ship's deficiencies get fixed at the builder's expense, and
waiving it on a boat with clean trials moves whatever surfaces later into the fleet's first
depot availability, paid from the maintenance account. The prediction is that Massachusetts's
first availability carries work that would have been the shipbuilder's, and that more Block IV
boats with clean trials get the same waiver, because the capacity argument the CNO made does
not change from hull to hull.

**Bottom line:** ask SWRMC's planners how growth was provided for in the America order before
your next task order is written, and when a post-shakedown availability is waived on a hull
headed for your pier, get the list of what it would have corrected, because that list is now
yours.

*Sources: [NAVSEA, Sep 15, 2026](https://www.navsea.navy.mil/Media/News/Article-View/Article/4601275/swrmc-undocks-uss-america-on-time/); [General Dynamics NASSCO, May 21, 2025](https://nassco.com/featured/general-dynamics-nassco-san-diego-awarded-fy26-docking-selected-restricted-availability-contract/); [GAO-20-370](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-20-370); [U.S. Navy, Aug 17, 2026](https://www.navy.mil/Press-Office/Press-Releases/display-pressreleases/Article/4574915/uss-massachusetts-ssn-798-ready-for-fleet-t); [GAO-17-418](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-17-418)*

## Waterfront Ops & Production

### The pier the Benfold went to is getting longer

A destroyer that lost power in the South China Sea was towed to Subic Bay and met there by
people from Yokosuka, and the American-owned yard that took it has a US-funded study to extend
its pier to a berth a destroyer fits.

FACT: [USNI News reported on August 15 that](https://news.usni.org/2026/08/14/uss-benfold-was-stalled-in-the-south-china-sea-for-four-days-after-engineering-casualty) USS Benfold (DDG 65) reported a generator casualty
with loss of power on July 24 in the Indo-Pacific, that sailors were "without galley services,
toilets and air conditioning," that contractor tugs towed the ship to Subic Bay "where it was
met by personnel from the Ship Repair Facility – Japan Regional Maintenance Center on July 28,"
that power was restored on July 30, and that repairs were completed by August 7. FACT: [USNI News
reported on September 14 that](https://news.usni.org/2026/09/14/u-s-funding-subic-bay-shipyard-repair-expansion) the Subic Drydock Corporation, "an American-owned firm operating
out of the former U.S. naval station," has a \$1.4 million feasibility study from the US Trade
and Development Agency to extend its approach pier by 169 metres, which the firm says would
create a usable berth of more than 270 metres and "allow us to accommodate larger vessels and
undertake repairs that are currently not possible due to draft and berth limitations," and that
under its Master Ship Repair Agreement the yard has serviced expeditionary fast transports and
littoral combat ships. A Master Ship Repair Agreement is NAVSEA's certification that a private
yard may bid on Navy repair work.

Assessment: the Benfold job is what forward emergent repair looks like in practice: the forward
yard's people travel to where the hull is, and the hull goes to the nearest pier that can take
it. That pier today takes fast transports and small combatants under its agreement; a destroyer
alongside for two weeks was a casualty response, not a capability. The extension is a study, so
the earliest a longer berth exists is years out, and the study's own words say the constraint
is draft as much as length. The prediction is that Subic's next Navy work after the study is a
destroyer's planned pier-side period rather than another tow, because the yard now has both the
agreement and the proof of demand, and that the draft limit, not the pier, decides which hulls.

**Bottom line:** if you plan emergent work for 7th Fleet, put Subic on the list now as the
destroyer-capable pier it is about to become, and ask the Yokosuka team who went what the
Benfold job needed that the yard could not provide; that gap is the requirement the study
should be written to.

*Sources: [USNI News, Aug 15, 2026](https://news.usni.org/2026/08/14/uss-benfold-was-stalled-in-the-south-china-sea-for-four-days-after-engineering-casualty); [USNI News, Sep 14, 2026](https://news.usni.org/2026/09/14/u-s-funding-subic-bay-shipyard-repair-expansion)*

### Two things to watch

Two developments that are not yet stories.

FACT: [the department's contract list for September 3](https://www.war.gov/News/Contracts/Contract/Article/4590748/) records a \$47,768,691 firm-fixed-price
award to Integrated Solutions for Systems of Huntsville "for expeditionary maintenance and
repair facility (EMRF) production," for "five fully outfitted EMRFs, consisting of 90 individual
expeditionary maintenance and repair container (EMRC) prototype units and four additional fully
outfitted EMRC variants," to be "deployed globally to diverse environments where the government
will use them as data collection platforms to validate the EMRF concept," complete by September
2027. Expeditionary here means forward: these are the pre-staged repair packages No. 9
described, loaded by SIMA sailors for a repair availability outside the maintenance plan, with
a 72-hour arrival objective.

FACT: [the commanding officers of SIMA San Diego and SIMA Norfolk said on September 3 that](https://www.dvidshub.net/news/574309/sima-commanding-officers-highlight-technical-proficiency-and-warfighting-readiness-surface-na) their
commands, re-established in June, are rotating sailors "through multiple shops" instead of one
specialty, adding a mandatory four-week onboarding assessment, and will conduct shipboard
maintenance "in direct coordination with Forward Deployed Regional Maintenance Center personnel,"
per DVIDS.

Assessment: these are the two ends of one intermediate level. The SIMAs sit at the homeports
and take intermediate-level work back from the depots and the contractors; the containers carry a
repair package forward to a ship that cannot get home, packed by the same sailors. What the
enterprise gains is sailor-manned repair at both ends of a deployment. The prediction is that
once the test reports, the emergent and intermediate-level work that today accumulates until a
hull reaches a depot pier arrives there less often, and the packages that do arrive carry less
of it.

**Bottom line:** September 2027 is when the container test reports. Until then, go through the
emergent and intermediate-level items in your last three packages and mark the ones that only
came to you because there was nowhere else to do them, forward or at the homeport; that is the
work the containers and the SIMAs are for.

*Sources: [DoW contracts, Sep 3, 2026](https://www.war.gov/News/Contracts/Contract/Article/4590748/); [SIMA via DVIDS, Sep 10, 2026](https://www.dvidshub.net/news/574309/sima-commanding-officers-highlight-technical-proficiency-and-warfighting-readiness-surface-na)*
