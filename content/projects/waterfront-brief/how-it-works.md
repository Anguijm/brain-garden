# How The Waterfront Brief is made

This explains the whole method behind the weekly brief in plain language: where the stories come
from, what gets thrown out and why, and what is kept on file so any claim can be checked later.
It is written so that a reader who has never seen the machinery can decide how much to trust
what the brief prints, and can argue with it on the merits.

## The short version

Every Friday morning, before anyone is awake, the brief goes looking through a fixed list of
subjects, at a fixed list of publications, for anything published recently. It saves a full copy
of the text of everything it finds worth keeping. Then a person, working from those saved copies
and nothing else, writes the issue: four pages of articles and a page of upcoming events. Along
the way a series of checks throw out anything too old, anything from a source that cannot be
followed back, and anything stated more strongly than the source actually supports.

## How it developed

The brief was not designed in one sitting. It started as four sections and a list of events, and
almost everything else in it exists because something went wrong and had to be fixed. That
history is worth stating plainly, because it is the reason for most of the rules below.

The first issues taught the hardest lessons. Source links pointed at the front page of a
publication rather than the article itself, so a reader who followed one landed nowhere useful,
and now every link has to go to the specific piece. A claim about a new capability was printed
as though the thing had already been delivered, which produced the rule that a source saying a
yard "can" do something is never reported as a yard having done it. Articles were anchored on
material years old, which produced the freshness rule. One well-covered subject filled an entire
issue and crowded out the rest, which produced the rule that every subject area gets its turn
before any of them gets a second story.

Later corrections were about honesty of presentation rather than sourcing. An issue shipped
without the standing note explaining how it is compiled, so that note is now mandatory and the
issue will not print without it. A corrected issue was published on the web while the printable
version still carried the old text, so the two are now checked against each other before
anything goes out.

The most recent round was the sharpest. An issue was assembled that technically followed every
rule and was still not worth reading: it named things without explaining them, it had no
discussion of policy or legislation at all, it had nothing on the leadership and workforce side,
and one article existed for no better reason than that there was space left on the page. The
fixes were to require that jargon be explained rather than named, to add subjects that were
missing, and to state a length target so that pages get filled with analysis instead of an extra
item. Filling space is now explicitly not a reason to run a story.

## What it covers

The brief watches eighteen subject areas through forty-one standing searches. In plain terms:

**The Navy's own announcements.** Anything the Naval Sea Systems Command or the Navy publishes
about shipyards, infrastructure, or repair work, including the fleet side of advanced
manufacturing such as printing parts aboard ship or at exercises.

**Public shipyard infrastructure.** The recapitalization of the four public yards: dry dock
projects, utilities, and the awards that pay for them, watched by yard name so that Norfolk,
Portsmouth, Puget Sound and Pearl Harbor each get caught, and watched at the engineering command
that executes the work rather than only where the news is repeated.

**Policy and legislation.** Defense authorization and appropriations as they move, the House and
Senate committees that write them, and maritime law affecting shipbuilding, cargo, and the
industrial base. This was added after a week in which Congress passed the largest rewrite of
federal maritime law in decades and the brief did not notice.

**The trade press.** Naval news outlets covering shipbuilding, ship repair, maintenance
backlogs, and Pacific sustainment, plus commercial maritime coverage of repair yards.

**Oversight and research.** Government audits of shipyard performance and business practices,
congressional research reports, peer-reviewed and preprint engineering work on things like
welding robotics and maintenance scheduling, and the shipbuilding research programs and
professional societies where yard techniques actually get shared.

**Workforce.** Studies, testimony, and named initiatives about the shipyard trades: hiring,
apprenticeships, retention, and supervision. This is the material behind the leadership item.

**Additive manufacturing.** The Navy's center of excellence in Danville, its operator, the
training pipeline next to it, and the trade press that covers metal printing for defense.

**Allies and partners.** Japan, South Korea, Australia, the Philippines and India, where the
subject is shipbuilding, repair, or sustainment cooperation.

**Events.** Conferences, panels and symposia a yard leader could actually attend, tracked
separately because they need dates and registration deadlines rather than a story.

## What it searches for

Each search names both a subject and, usually, a specific publication, and asks for the current
month and year. So the brief is not asking the open internet "what is new in shipbuilding." It
is asking a defined set of publications a defined set of questions, every week, in the same
words. That consistency is the point: it makes the coverage repeatable and makes gaps visible,
because a subject that never returns anything is a subject nobody is publishing about, or a
search pointed at the wrong place.

Each search returns a handful of candidates. Everything found is set beside everything else, and
then the screening starts.

## What it screens against

**Who published it.** Every candidate is scored against a list of fifty-eight publications
ranked by how much weight they carry. A command's own announcement outranks a trade report of
the same event, which outranks a contractor's press release about itself. A program's own
website is treated as what the program says, never as proof that a thing happened. Anything from
a publication not on the list is set aside unless it is a government or military source.

**How old it is.** An article can only anchor a story if it was published within the last twelve
months. Anything older is moved to a separate pile and can appear only as clearly dated
background inside a current story. Anything whose date cannot be determined is flagged for a
person to check by hand before it is used, which is where most stale material actually gets
caught.

**Whether we have seen it already.** Anything already judged too old in a previous week is
remembered and skipped, so the same outdated article does not get fetched, re-examined and
discarded every single week.

**Whether it is a story at all.** A section index or a homepage, a page listing an
organization's symposia for instance, carries no news, so it loses to a real article. The
exception is events, where the organizer's front page is often the only place the dates are
printed.

**Whether everything gets a turn.** Selection goes around the subject areas one at a time, so
that a heavily-covered subject cannot take the whole issue before a quieter one gets a single
story.

Then come the checks on the writing itself.

**Every stated fact names its source in the same sentence.** Not in a footnote, not at the
bottom. If a sentence claims something and does not say who said it, the issue does not print.

**Claims are labelled.** FACT means a named source says it. Assessment means it is the desk's
own analysis, and analysis is only allowed to rest on facts stated above it in the same article,
so conclusions cannot smuggle in new information. Speculation is labelled as such.

**Every source link goes to the actual article.** A link to a publication's front page will stop
the issue printing.

**A second pass reads the sources back.** Before anything is published, each claim is checked
against the saved text of the source it cites and graded: supported, overstated, unsupported, or
not verifiable from what the source actually says. Anything unsupported blocks publication until
it is fixed or removed. This is the check that has caught the most real errors.

**The printed edition has to be right too.** Exactly four pages of articles plus the events
page, the standing explanation of method has to be present, and the web and print versions are
compared so that a correction made in one cannot be missing from the other.

## What the library looks like

Nothing the brief uses is a link and a memory. Every source is saved as its own file containing
the full text as published, its address, its publication date, who captured it and when. Three
weeks in, that is ninety-two source files kept and twenty-nine set aside as too old.

Alongside those sit four working records. A register of nineteen events with dates exactly as
the organizers print them, including the ones with no dates yet, which are tracked but never
printed. A running record of what each week's search turned up, what was chosen, and how each
choice turned out, which exists to answer whether the selection method is actually any good. A
written ledger of every mistake caught, thirty-four entries so far across the whole vault, each
with what went wrong, what was changed, and what now prevents it. And the issues themselves,
each one kept as both a web page and a printable file.

The reason for keeping all of it is simple. Any number in any issue can be traced back to the
saved copy of the source it came from, on the date it was captured, and checked. If the brief is
wrong, it should be possible to prove that it is wrong.
