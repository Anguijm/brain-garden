Written this week for the executive steering committees at the four public shipyards and
SRF-JRMC, which means the test for every item is whether it changes a strategic or business
planning assumption rather than whether it is interesting. Four things clear that bar. The Navy
opened the market for a dry dock program at Puget Sound with a ceiling of \$30 billion, which
concentrates the heavy-civil contractor pool every yard's projects draw on. The submarine
industrial base got \$76.6 billion, which is a labor-market and future-workload event before it
is a procurement one. Cold spray became an organic Navy capability, which turns a scrap
decision into a routing decision. And a distributed manufacturing network ran a full
request-to-delivery cycle at RIMPAC, which is a watch item for forward repair rather than
anything to act on yet. Labels: FACT is attributed to the named source in the same sentence;

## Waterfront Ops & Production

### A \$30 billion ceiling at Puget Sound, and what it does to everyone else's program

FACT: the Navy announced on June 15, 2026 that NAVFAC Northwest had posted a Sources Sought
announcement on SAM.gov for the Multi-Mission Dry Dock at Puget Sound Naval Shipyard and
Intermediate Maintenance Facility, and launched multimissiondrydock.com as the project's public
repository, describing the dock under the Shipyard Infrastructure Optimization Program as "the
largest single infrastructure modernization project in Navy history." FACT: in that release
Capt. Troy Brown, the accountable project officer, called it "day one of a multi-decade,
critical infrastructure project for the Navy," and the Navy listed the proposed scope as dry
dock drainage, dewatering and flooding systems, a caisson and caisson gate, roadways and rail
networks, portal crane track, access and egress elements, mechanical, electrical and
communications systems, and utility routing tunnels. FACT: the same release states the Navy is
still in the National Environmental Policy Act process, with the Final Environmental Impact
Statement and Record of Decision expected later this year.

FACT: Military Times reported on July 22, 2026 that the resulting multiple-award construction
contract is worth up to \$30 billion and runs five years plus three one-year options. FACT: the
notice, quoted in that report, describes the dock as "a fully pressure-relieved dry
dock capable of accommodating shipyard repair services for the newest Ford-class aircraft
carrier, Nimitz-class carriers, and other surface and subsurface ships served by PSNS & IMF."
FACT: the same report quotes the Navy's Multi-Mission Dry Dock website stating that "the Navy
does not currently have the capacity and capability to perform depot-level maintenance on a
Gerald R. Ford-class carrier in the Pacific Northwest," and that "the only dry dock on the West
Coast able to accommodate a current Nimitz-class aircraft carrier is Dry Dock 6 (DD6), which
does not meet seismic resiliency standards and cannot fully accommodate Ford-class carriers."
FACT: Military Times reports the work sits within existing Dry Dock 3 and Piers 6 and 7, that
task orders typically range between \$2 billion and \$6 billion each, and that the first task
order is expected to cover dredging plus work on piers, buildings and utilities, the second the
demolition of Dry Dock 3 and construction of the new dock, and the third infrastructure such as
electrical substations, utility tunnels and buildings.

FACT: as dated context, GAO reported on June 28, 2023 (GAO-23-106067) that the estimated cost of
the Navy's dry dock project at Portsmouth Naval Shipyard rose from \$528 million to \$2.2 billion
between 2019 and 2021, that the Navy had trouble estimating project costs partly because
estimates were based on preliminary rather than mature designs, and recommended the Navy update
its estimates throughout the design process.

Assessment: three planning consequences follow, and only the first belongs to Puget Sound. For
PSNS & IMF this is major construction inside a working yard: demolishing Dry Dock 3 and
reworking Piers 6 and 7 removes docking and berthing capacity for the duration, and dredging and
utility work constrains the waterfront around it, so the availability plan for the execution
window is a steering committee problem now rather than a NAVFAC one later. For the other three
yards, a \$30 billion ceiling with task orders of \$2 billion to \$6 billion concentrates the
heavy-civil contractor pool and the NAVFAC execution capacity that your own projects bid
against; scarcity of qualified constructors, not scarcity of authorization, is the near-term
risk to your schedules. And the GAO finding is the precedent to hold this against: a project
scoped from preliminary design at Portsmouth grew roughly fourfold, so the question to put to
each task order is what design maturity its estimate rests on.

For SRF-JRMC the stated gap is the one to track: if there is no Pacific Northwest depot
capability for Ford-class carriers until this dock exists, then Ford-class presence in the
Western Pacific is supported by East Coast docking and by whatever intermediate-level work can
be done forward, and that assumption should be visible in your capacity plan rather than
implicit in it.

Assessment: note also what has not happened yet. The Record of Decision is still pending, so
the environmental clearance for the largest infrastructure project in the program is an open
item on a project the Navy is already sounding the market for. That sequence is normal, but it
means the schedule risk in the first task orders is regulatory as much as constructional.

**Bottom line:** Ask three questions early. What design maturity backs each task order estimate,
which regional constructors will be consumed by this program in the years your own SIOP projects
need them, and what the Record of Decision does to the first task order's start.

## Business Practices & Strategy

### \$76.6 billion in submarines is a labor and workload forecast, not a procurement story

FACT: USNI News reported on July 29, 2026 that the Navy awarded General Dynamics Electric Boat
and HII \$76.6 billion for the next batch of attack and ballistic missile submarines after a
delay of nearly three years, split into \$42.1 billion for nine Block VI Virginia-class boats
plus material for a tenth, \$29.5 billion for five Build II Columbia-class boats, and \$5 billion
for workforce improvements between Electric Boat and HII. FACT: USNI reports the July 29
Pentagon announcement states the Block VI award includes previously announced material awards,
including long-lead-time and economic ordering quantity material, and previously awarded
investments in shipbuilder productivity efforts. FACT: USNI reports the delay traces to a 2022
indemnification dispute that stalled negotiations for 18 months, after which the Navy and the
builders struggled for years over workforce wages that rose during the pandemic inflation spike,
and that a major factor in rising prices is workforce shortage, with many experienced
shipbuilders having retired and the builders competing for labor against other industries.
FACT: Electric Boat president Mark Rayha is quoted saying the modifications give the company and
its suppliers "the demand certainty we need to continue investing in capacity and hiring the
workforce necessary to ensure we deliver these important national security assets on schedule."

FACT: as dated context, GAO reported in March 2025 that private companies augment the repair
work conducted at the Navy's public shipyards, that the shipbuilding industrial base has not
historically met the Navy's goals, and that while ship repair has improved since 2019,
representatives from some companies told GAO they often had more capacity than the Navy used.

Assessment: read the award as three forecasts. On labor, \$5 billion aimed explicitly at
workforce lands at two builders who recruit from regional trades markets that public yards also
recruit from, and a company president saying the money buys hiring is a statement that wage and
hiring pressure in those markets is about to increase; your retention assumptions for the next
two hiring cycles should be tested against that rather than against your own attrition history.
On workload, nine attack boats and five ballistic missile boats are hulls that enter a depot
cycle years from now, so the planning artifact that matters to a public yard is the delivery
schedule behind this award, not its dollar value. On supply, the award explicitly consumes
long-lead and economic ordering quantity material, which is vendor capacity your availabilities
draw on from the same base. The GAO context cuts the other way and is worth holding: if private
repair capacity has at times exceeded what the Navy used, then the constraint in the system is
not uniformly capacity, and a workload argument built only on scarcity will not survive
scrutiny.

**Bottom line:** Get the delivery schedule, not the award value, into your long-range workload
model, and open the planning-yard and builder relationships for these classes now, because the
engineering and technical data you will need arrive on their timeline rather than yours.

*Sources: [USNI News, Jul 29, 2026](https://news.usni.org/2026/07/29/navy-awards-76-6b-for-9-virginia-5-columbia-subs-to-general-dynamics-hii); [GAO report on naval shipbuilding and ship repair, via USNI News, Mar 4, 2025](https://news.usni.org/2025/03/04/gao-report-on-naval-shipbuilding-ship-repair)*

## Maintenance Engineering & Technologies

### Cold spray goes organic, and an obsolete part gets printed

FACT: NAVSEA reported on July 16, 2026 that Naval Surface Warfare Center, Crane Division opened
its Metal Additive Manufacturing (MADMAN) Repair Center, using low-pressure and high-pressure
cold spray to extend the life of naval components. FACT: NSWC Crane Commanding Officer Capt. Rex
Boonyobhas is quoted calling it "the first organic industrial base facility for non-structural
cold spray within the Navy" and saying it "allows us to shift our focus from 'replace' to
'repair'." FACT: the release states cold spray is a solid-state process that projects metal
powder at high speed using compressed gas, and that because it does not rely on thermal melting
it avoids the cracking and loss of strength that fusion welding can introduce, and that the
low-pressure systems are portable enough to project repair capability to the component.

FACT: NAVSEA's Philadelphia division reported on June 11, 2026 that its engineers are using
additive manufacturing to redesign and produce the wallbox for the Boiler Combustion Monitoring
System, because the existing part is obsolete and no longer available through traditional supply
channels. FACT: the release states the system is installed on the Navy's six Wasp-class
amphibious assault ships and on USS Blue Ridge (LCC 19), the Seventh Fleet flagship, and
describes its safety function: the wallbox gives the furnace camera and inspection light a
protected path into the boiler furnace, and boiler operators use that view to check the furnace
deck for unburned fuel before lighting the burners, because if a burner flame goes out, fuel oil
can build on the hot furnace floor, vaporize, and create a potentially explosive situation.

Assessment: both items convert an engineering fact into a business decision. Crane turns a
condemnation into a routing question: components your yards currently scrap or send to a vendor
because fusion welding would risk them now have an organic path, and the committee-level choice
is whether to route candidate parts there, negotiate throughput, or pursue the capability
locally. The Philadelphia item is the model for obsolescence: the path to a printed replacement
ran through the in-service engineering agent, not a print shop, which means the constraint on
your own obsolescence list is engineering authority and qualification rather than machine
availability. For SRF-JRMC the hull list is the direct read, because the flagship it lives with
is on it.

**Bottom line:** Two asks. Send NSWC Crane a candidate parts list from your condemnation data
and find out what their intake and throughput actually look like, and route your top
obsolescence drivers to the cognizant in-service engineering agent rather than to a printer.

*Sources: [NAVSEA, Jul 16, 2026](https://www.navsea.navy.mil/Media/News/Article-View/Article/4547982/nswc-crane-opens-advanced-repair-center-forging-new-era-of-fleet-readiness/); [NAVSEA, Jun 11, 2026](https://www.navsea.navy.mil/Media/News/Article-View/Article/4515044/nswcpd-engineers-use-additive-manufacturing-to-modernize-boiler-monitoring-syst/)*

## Operations Maintenance & Readiness

### A full request-to-delivery cycle ran at RIMPAC, on exercise equipment

FACT: the Navy reported on July 21, 2026 that the Naval Postgraduate School's Consortium for
Advanced Manufacturing Research and Education (CAMRE) and FLEETWERX demonstrated a chain during
RIMPAC 2026 in which Marines manufactured replacement parts aboard ship using expeditionary
advanced manufacturing equipment, a digitally crewed surface vessel carried those components to
a US Navy ship, and the delivery completed what the release calls the Department of the Navy's
first digitally crewed resupply, after which the same vessel carried drone components to the
25th Infantry Division's Lightning Lab for assembly and testing at Makua Range. FACT: the Navy
credits the workflow to the Joint Advanced Manufacturing System, a digital command and control
platform developed through CAMRE with the Marine Innovation Unit, which tracks manufacturing
requests and assigns production across distributed nodes while cataloging equipment, expertise
and production capacity across the enterprise. FACT: in a July 7, 2026 release the Navy said
CAMRE was preparing what organizers describe as the largest advanced manufacturing demonstration
ever conducted by the Department of War, and that RIMPAC 2026 involves 35 nations, roughly 40
surface ships, five submarines, more than 140 aircraft and 25,000 personnel.

Assessment: this is a watch item, not an action item, and the distinction matters because the
equipment was expeditionary and the setting was an exercise, so nothing here has been through a
yard's production or qualification system. What makes it worth a committee's attention is the
catalog: a network that inventories production capacity and assigns work across nodes is, if it
matures, an allocation mechanism that could route work to or around a repair activity. The
questions that decide whether a forward yard becomes a node are qualification authority and who
owns the technical data package, and neither is answered by a successful demonstration.

**Bottom line:** Track JAMS rather than the demonstration, and specifically whether its catalog
ever extends to organic industrial-base shops, because that is the point at which this becomes a
workload question instead of a technology story.

*Sources: [Navy.mil, Jul 21, 2026](https://www.navy.mil/Press-Office/News-Stories/display-news/Article/4551621/camre-fleetwerx-conduct-first-of-its-kind-digitally-crewed-surface-vessel-resup/); [Navy.mil, Jul 7, 2026](https://www.navy.mil/Press-Office/News-Stories/display-news/Article/4534537/largest-advanced-manufacturing-demonstration-in-department-of-war-history-set-f/)*
