This week: the Congressional Budget Office (CBO) finds the Navy's submarine and destroyer fleets shrink before they grow. A Navy officer says tagouts add days to yard periods, but no record we read counts them. Sen. Roger Wicker calls steam catapults for the carrier Doris Miller a non-starter, and Senate appropriators would stretch the carrier Harry S. Truman's overhaul money over another year. ## Operations Maintenance & Readiness

### Sub and destroyer fleets shrink before they grow

The Navy's plan would grow the fleet to 398 ships by 2056, up from 286 today. But the Congressional Budget Office (CBO) says the submarine and destroyer fleets shrink first: attack and guided missile submarines drop from 50 to 45 in 2030 and 2031, and cruisers and destroyers from 84 to 71 in 2035. Meanwhile, the four public shipyards already have more attack submarines in or waiting for maintenance than the Navy wants.

The plan costs far more than the Navy gets now. [CBO counts](https://s3.documentcloud.org/documents/28926774/cbo-shipbuilding-analysis.pdf) 286 battle force ships on Nov. 1, 2026 and 398 in 2056. It puts the cost at \$53.8 billion a year, "41 percent more than the \$38.1 billion the Navy has received in annual appropriations, on average, over the past five years."

The submarine count falls before it climbs. [CBO counts](https://s3.documentcloud.org/documents/28926774/cbo-shipbuilding-analysis.pdf) 47 attack submarines and 3 Ohio class guided missile submarines today, and those three Ohios retire within three years. Together they fall to 45 in 2030 and 2031, then reach 68 by 2056. The Navy also plans 7 large payload submarines from the late 2030s, and 12 Columbia class boats to replace its 14 Ohio class ballistic missile submarines.

Destroyers bottom out later. [CBO reports](https://s3.documentcloud.org/documents/28926774/cbo-shipbuilding-analysis.pdf) that the 84 cruisers and destroyers "would decline to a low of 71 ships in 2035 before steadily increasing to 95 ships by 2056."

New submarines come slower than the Navy buys them. [CBO finds](https://s3.documentcloud.org/documents/28926774/cbo-shipbuilding-analysis.pdf) that attack submarine production over the past five years "has averaged only 1.2 submarines per year," though the Navy has bought them at 2.0 a year for most of the decade.

The public yards already have too many submarines in the queue. [CBO writes](https://s3.documentcloud.org/documents/28926774/cbo-shipbuilding-analysis.pdf) that the four public yards do nearly all carrier and submarine maintenance. The Navy wants no more than 20 percent of its attack submarines in or waiting for maintenance, but 35 percent are, CBO says, citing a January report from the Congressional Research Service.

Delays are falling. [Breaking Defense reported on Nov. 13](https://breakingdefense.com/2026/11/submarine-maintenance-delays-saw-significant-improvements-in-fy26-sub-czar-says/) that, by Vice Adm. Rob Gaucher's count, the Navy faced about 1,400 days of submarine maintenance delay in each of fiscal 2024 and 2025, and 780 days in fiscal 2026.

The dip is time to clear the backlog, not spare capacity. The count drops by 5 boats, but about 16 attack submarines are in or waiting for maintenance (35 percent of 47), against a goal of about 9. With delays already falling, the dip is a chance to shrink that line. And because new boats arrive at 1.2 a year while the Navy buys 2.0, the dip is more likely to run deeper or longer than the plan shows. We see a 55 percent chance that the Navy's count of attack and guided missile submarines falls below 45 before Oct. 1, 2031. We're wrong if it stays at 45 or more.

After the dip, submarine work grows. Counting ballistic missile boats, the submarine fleet grows from 64 today (47 + 3 + 14) to 80 in 2056 (68 + 12), about a quarter more, and the public yards do nearly all its maintenance. That leaves out the 7 large payload boats, bought one every three years from 2038, so some of them would add to the 2056 count.

**Bottom line:** Use the 2030 and 2031 dip to get attack submarines through maintenance faster, not as spare capacity. Size destroyer work to a force that shrinks until 2035, not to the 398-ship plan.

## Waterfront Ops & Production

### Tagouts stall yard work, and no record we read counts the days

A Navy officer wrote in October that one tagout can take a week to clear, and that standdowns over bad tagouts add days to yard periods. He says artificial intelligence (AI) could save that time. But no Government Accountability Office (GAO) report or Navy delay record we read counts those days, so there is no number yet for what AI would save.

His complaint is the wait. [A former public shipyard department head wrote in October's Proceedings](https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/2026/october/ai-can-shorten-shipyard-periods) that three people trace each shipyard-proposed tagout through the drawings: the first checker, the second checker and the assistant authorizing officer (AAO). Each fault sends it back, "making a single tagout take a week." During safety standdowns over inadequate tagouts, "no work controls can be processed," "which adds days to availabilities." He writes that "the AAO should be replaced by an assistant authorizing AI." A week is his worst case, and he gives no count of standdowns or days lost.

Officers lose hours to it. [Lt. William Parker wrote in Proceedings in 2022](https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/2022/october/fix-submarine-shipyard-tagout-program) that when USS California (SSN-781) was down to nine officers, six served as AAOs, and it was "typical to spend eight to ten hours per officer per week." "On average, there are four to six tagouts waiting on authorization at the daily project meeting," and "inevitably, shipyard work was delayed." That is 48 to 60 officer hours a week (six officers at eight to ten hours).

Sailors' checks cost hours too. [Parker wrote](https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/2022/october/fix-submarine-shipyard-tagout-program) that giving the shipyard full authority over its own tagouts would "remove the first and second check and save the divisions more than 6,000 man hours during an 18-month availability." Of the queue, he wrote: "This is not a big problem for the enlisted personnel in the divisions. While it is still a lot of work, there are at least three sailors to one officer per tagout." That is about 77 sailor hours a week (6,000 over about 78 weeks), more than the six short-handed officers spent.

And the human check catches real problems. [Parker reported](https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/2022/october/fix-submarine-shipyard-tagout-program) that in his boat's last availability, the AAO found safety problems in 3 of 5,000 tagouts. Two more passed every check and still had voltage at initial verification, because nobody tagged out a secondary power source.

Pearl Harbor pitched tagout-checking software in 2018. [Pearl Harbor Naval Shipyard's 2018 Maintenance Innovation Challenge entry](https://jteg.ncms.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/P18287343_DOD_18_mic_brochure_11718.pdf) said its software checks isolations "within seconds." It was "in the proof-of-concept" stage.

Newer Navy AI work skips tagouts. [Lt. Alana Davis wrote for the Center for International Maritime Security (CIMSEC) in October](https://cimsec.org/tag-youre-it-modernizing-naval-maintenance-starts-with-the-tagout-process/) that the Navy's July 2026 AI maintenance work "does not tackle" tagouts. She writes that Congress's February 2026 requirement for 3D models covers vessel design certification.

The delay records we read do not break out tagouts. [GAO's 2020 report on the public shipyards](https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-20-588.pdf) found 38 of 51 carrier and submarine periods finished late. Unplanned work and workforce led the 10 factors it examined. [A 2017 Naval Postgraduate School study of submarine work at Pearl Harbor](https://dair.nps.edu/bitstream/123456789/2256/1/NPS-AM-17-205.pdf) said the late-day codes "fail to provide any insight into the root causes." The GAO text we have never mentions tagouts, though we could not read its factor chart.

An AI in the assistant authorizing officer's seat would go after Parker's queue, and a digital trace might catch his missed power source. But it would remove a human check that caught 3 safety problems in 5,000 tagouts on his one boat, and nothing we read counts what the sailors' checks catch. The 2026 AI and 3D-model work makes a pilot more plausible, but neither names tagouts, and the Pearl Harbor software was a proof of concept in 2018. We see about a 70 percent chance that the Navy will not announce a pilot of AI-written tagouts at a public shipyard by Dec. 31, 2027.

**Bottom line:** Time every tagout your project proposes, from hand-off to approval, and note why each one comes back. Schedule rework and retests on that wait, not on how long the work takes, and fix the mistakes that keep sending tagouts back.

## Business Practices & Strategy

### Senate's Wicker calls steam catapults a "non-starter"

President Trump ordered a plan to put steam catapults on the carrier Doris Miller (CVN-81) in place of its Electromagnetic Aircraft Launch System (EMALS). On Nov. 17, Sen. Roger Wicker, who chairs the Senate Armed Services Committee, told acting Navy Secretary Hung Cao that steam is a non-starter. No law or budget settles it yet. Separately, Senate appropriators' draft bill would take \$1.6 billion out of this year's carrier refueling overhaul money and spread the USS Harry S. Truman (CVN-75) overhaul's funding over one more year.

Wicker said it plainly. [USNI News reported](https://news.usni.org/2026/11/17/reverting-ford-class-carriers-to-steam-is-non-starter-says-sasc-chair-wicker) that Wicker cut in: "Secretary Cao, returning to steam is a non-starter, and everybody needs to understand that." [A White House memo of Aug. 13](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2026/08/rebuilding-the-united-states-navy-and-americas-shipbuilding-industrial-base/) had asked for a plan replacing EMALS and the Advanced Weapons Elevators "with steam and hydraulic systems for the construction of CVN-81."

The Navy's plan is in, and Cao may back it. [USNI News reported](https://news.usni.org/2026/11/17/reverting-ford-class-carriers-to-steam-is-non-starter-says-sasc-chair-wicker) that Cao delivered the plan last month, and that early estimates put the design work to bring back steam at around \$10 billion, though USNI named no source. [The Associated Press wrote](https://abcnews.com/US/wireStory/trump-faces-gop-pushback-order-steam-launch-jets-138230538) that he "appeared to stand behind Trump's directive."

The appropriators objected in words, not dollars. [Their Nov. 16 explanatory statement](https://web.archive.org/web/20261118010806/https://www.appropriations.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/def_explanatory_stmt.pdf) warns of "many billions of dollars" and "many years in program schedule delays." It orders a Navy report within 90 days of the bill becoming law, including the maintenance manpower steam would need.

The memo orders only a plan, and it waits on Congress for money. [The memo itself](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2026/08/rebuilding-the-united-states-navy-and-americas-shipbuilding-industrial-base/) says it "shall be implemented consistent with applicable law and subject to the availability of appropriations." [The Associated Press noted](https://abcnews.com/US/wireStory/trump-faces-gop-pushback-order-steam-launch-jets-138230538) that "Democrats will take control of the House and Senate on Jan. 3."

Truman is running late, so Senate appropriators would stretch its money too. [The Senate statement](https://web.archive.org/web/20261118010806/https://www.appropriations.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/def_explanatory_stmt.pdf) cuts carrier refueling overhaul funding from \$4.4 billion to \$2.8 billion on "a one-time basis," because Truman's estimated redelivery "has increased by 10 months to November 2031." It says the cut "functionally" extends Truman's incremental funding by "an additional year."

Meanwhile, the gear already at sea on Ford still needs support. [The Pentagon's Director, Operational Test and Evaluation, reported](https://www.dote.osd.mil/Portals/97/pub/reports/FY2025/navy/2025cvn78.pdf) that on the Gerald R. Ford (CVN-78) class, "Reliance on off-ship technical support remains a challenge." The Navy has fiscal 2026 money for a fourth arresting gear engine option, "with installation of the new system targeted for FY29." In August, [a defense trade site reported](https://defence-blog.com/?p=99877) a \$42.9 million order for EMALS and arresting gear spares for the carriers in service, with work running through April 2034. It said the memo "does not affect Ford" or its two sister ships.

This brief's Oct. 16 forecast still stands: switching back to steam needs money, and we found no public request for the reported \$10 billion.

The fiscal 2028 budget request will show which way this goes. For steam: the President ordered the plan, USNI News reports an initial design estimate of about \$10 billion, and Cao may back it. Against: the memo waits on appropriations, a Democratic Congress would receive the request, and Wicker and the Senate appropriators have warned against it. We see a 65 percent chance that the request has no money for steam catapults on Doris Miller. We will know by June 30, 2027.

**Bottom line:** Plan off-ship technical support and spare parts as regular work in every Ford-class availability through 2034, not as growth. Leave room around fiscal 2029 for a fourth arresting gear engine. Keep steam maintenance crews out of plans until the Navy gives Congress a count.

### House defense chairman: no right-to-repair change soon

Mike Rogers, who chairs the House Armed Services Committee, does not expect right-to-repair policy to change soon. He says the Pentagon could not name a single case where contracts failed to get it what it wanted from companies. This year's defense policy bill is the fiscal 2027 National Defense Authorization Act (NDAA). Both versions would by default give the government purpose rights to the technical data it buys, unless the maker proves it has a right to tighter limits. We expect the final law to drop or water down that default, so for now, assume makers still control the data on parts they paid to develop.

Rogers is blunt. [Breaking Defense reported on Nov. 18](https://breakingdefense.com/2026/11/350-billion-reconciliation-bill-not-going-to-happen-hasc-chair/) that Rogers said the Pentagon could not give a single example of failing to get what it wanted through contracts. "I don't see us changing our policy anytime in the near future," he said.

His own committee outvoted him. It adopted the change by voice vote at its markup, "over the objections of the committee's chairman," [Federal News Network reported](https://federalnewsnetwork.com/congress/2026/06/hasc-advances-right-to-repair-proposal-industry-pushes-back/).

Industry agrees. Fifteen associations asked committee leaders to remove House section 861 and Senate section 804, in [a Sept. 1 letter](https://ipo.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Multi-Association-Letter-on-FY27-NDAA-IP-Provisions-Final.pdf).

The bills flip the default. Today [10 U.S.C. 3771](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2024-title10/html/USCODE-2024-title10-subtitleA-partV-subpartD-chap275-subchapI-sec3771.htm) lets a contractor restrict data on an item developed "exclusively at private expense." [House section 861](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-119hr8800eh/html/BILLS-119hr8800eh.htm) and [Senate section 804](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-119s4784rs/html/BILLS-119s4784rs.htm) would give the government purpose rights instead, unless the contractor makes its case by "clear and convincing evidence." The House applies 861 only to new contracts. Both would also let the government release manufacturing data for maintenance in wartime, when an agency head finds the original supplier "will be unable to satisfy military readiness or operational requirements." But another House section, 824, rewrites the same paragraph and leaves that release out.

Maintenance data turns into a bargaining point. Both also change the rule for maintenance data ([House text](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-119hr8800eh/html/BILLS-119hr8800eh.htm), [Senate text](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-119s4784rs/html/BILLS-119s4784rs.htm)). Data that contractors cannot restrict today would then get government purpose rights "unless the Government and the contractor negotiate different license rights." Today the government gets that data with unlimited rights, [industry's letter says](https://ipo.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Multi-Association-Letter-on-FY27-NDAA-IP-Provisions-Final.pdf).

The House also wants gaps named. [Section 867 of the House bill](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-119hr8800eh/html/BILLS-119hr8800eh.htm) makes each service check every major weapon system and critical readiness item each year. Where one falls short and its sustainment plan cannot close the gap at an affordable cost, a root cause analysis must name "the specific parts, services, software, and corresponding intellectual property" required. Reports go to the defense committees every year, with a public summary unless security concerns make one not feasible. [The Senate committee's bill](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-119s4784rs/html/BILLS-119s4784rs.htm) has no such section.

Waiting on makers costs time. A repair contractor would not fix a Littoral Combat Ship's crane motor without the original maker present, so maintainers waited two and a half weeks, [the Government Accountability Office reported in 2025](https://files.gao.gov/reports/GAO-25-107468/index.html).

Time is short. Rogers is the outgoing chairman, Democrats are "poised to take over both chambers on Jan. 3," and the NDAA had not passed, [Breaking Defense reported](https://breakingdefense.com/2026/11/350-billion-reconciliation-bill-not-going-to-happen-hasc-chair/).

Section 867 could start to produce the kind of examples Rogers says are missing, but only the House has it, and its public summary can be withheld. The bills also cut both ways: makers would lose some control of manufacturing data, but maintenance data the government holds outright today, by industry's account, would become something to negotiate.

We see about a four in five chance that the final law drops or waters down the default, because the chairman has opposed it since the markup and industry wants both sections out. The wartime release is a coin flip: both chambers wrote it the same way, but section 824 drops it. That pushes this brief's Nov. 20 forecast (the final law keeps the wartime release and drops or waters down the default) below even, because striking sections 861 and 804 whole would remove the wartime release along with the default. It settles on the final text, and lapses if no law passes by Jan. 3.

**Bottom line:** For every part whose data the maker holds, book the maker's representative when the work package locks, not when the job starts.

## On the watch

### Three things to watch

**USS North Dakota (SSN 784) finished 10 months past its first plan, and two more submarines are late.** Its commander called the Portsmouth Naval Shipyard job "the shortest availability in Portsmouth Naval Shipyard history," [the Navy announced](https://www.navy.mil/Press-Office/News-Stories/display-news/Article/4648979/uss-north-dakota-ssn-784-changes-homeport-to-pearl-harbor/). [The Navy's fiscal 2023 budget book](https://web.archive.org/web/20260315200043/https://www.secnav.navy.mil/fmc/fmb/Documents/23pres/OMN_Book.pdf) planned the job to end Oct. 17, 2025, but [the yard finished Aug. 29, 2026](https://www.navy.mil/Press-Office/News-Stories/display-news/Article/4586562/pnsy-returns-north-dakota-to-the-fleet/), "ahead of the rebaselined schedule." That is about 10 months past the first plan (316 days). The same book planned USS John Warner (SSN 785) at Norfolk to finish Sept. 25, 2025, and USS Illinois (SSN 786) at Pearl Harbor June 4, 2026. Vice Adm. Rob Gaucher said Nov. 12 that both will deliver in a "few weeks," late against their original schedules but "several months early to their re-baseline schedules," [Breaking Defense reported](https://breakingdefense.com/2026/11/submarine-maintenance-delays-saw-significant-improvements-in-fy26-sub-czar-says/).

**The Navy gives two finish dates for Portsmouth's new submarine docks, 17 months apart.** Only two of Portsmouth Naval Shipyard's three dry docks can take a Virginia-class submarine, and the new docking bays are due "Dec/2027," [the Navy's fiscal 2027 military construction book says](https://www.secnav.navy.mil/fmc/fmb/Documents/27pres/MCON_Book.pdf). The Navy "expects to complete the dry docks by May 2029," [the Government Accountability Office (GAO) reported in September](https://files.gao.gov/reports/GAO-26-107830/index.html). Plan on two docks until a boat docks in the new bays.

**The carrier USS George H.W. Bush (CVN 77) is booked for three yard periods starting in 2027, the first close to its return.** [The Navy's April 2026 operations budget book](https://web.archive.org/web/20260927025227/https://www.secnav.navy.mil/fmc/fmb/Documents/27pres/OMN_Book.pdf) schedules Bush at Norfolk Naval Shipyard Feb. 11 to March 12, 2027, for \$17 million. It then schedules a private period in Portsmouth, Va., June 1 to Dec. 21 for \$64 million, and a Norfolk period June 27 to Dec. 25 for \$145 million. Bush left Norfolk on deployment March 31, [the Navy announced](https://www.navy.mil/Press-Office/Press-Releases/display-pressreleases/Article/4451254/george-h-w-bush-carrier-strike-group-departs-for-deployment/), and carrier group sailors are told to expect 10 to 11 months away, [USNI News reported Oct. 29](https://news.usni.org/2026/10/29/long-deployments-to-be-navy-standard-for-next-couple-years-mcpon-cno-say). At that length Bush gets home between late January and late February 2027, so the Feb. 11 period could start up to 17 days before it gets home, or 11 days after. The June periods start three to five months after it gets home.

**Bottom line:** Plan for Virginia-class dockings to finish at least six months after the first end date in the Navy's budget books, and only on the two Portsmouth docks that can take them. Start Bush's 2027 crew and material plans from the day it gets back to Norfolk, not from Feb. 11.
