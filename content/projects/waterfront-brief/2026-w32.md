Four changes are moving through the system this week, and none of them is a contract award. The
Navy has written down that it is giving up on the long, everything-at-once availability. A House
committee has reported a defense bill that would hand whole categories of surface combatant to
single yards for five-year terms. The federal award records for
Navy shipboard air conditioning show one supplier, two standing contracts titled for new
construction, and the government's own written finding that no alternative exists. And the Navy's
largest overseas repair facility finished a carrier availability on time and then lost its entire
command team for the second time in twenty months.

Three of the four are documents rather than events: a plan, a bill and two contract records. Labels: FACT is attributed to the named source in
the same sentence; Assessment is this desk's analysis, built only on facts stated above it;
Speculation is flagged as such.

## Waterfront Ops & Production

### A carrier came out of a Japanese yard on time, and then the command team was relieved

FACT: the Defense Visual Information Distribution Service reported on June 28, 2026 that the Naval
Ship Repair Facility and Japan Regional Maintenance Center at Yokosuka completed Chief of Naval
Operations maintenance availabilities for the destroyer USS *Shoup* and the aircraft carrier USS
*George Washington*, returning both to the fleet on time on May 11 and May 13, 2026. A CNO
availability is a maintenance period large enough that the Navy's own headquarters, rather than a
local command, owns the schedule and the money.

FACT: Stars and Stripes reported on June 3, 2026 that the command provides intermediate- and
depot-level maintenance for Navy surface ships assigned to the 7th Fleet, supports operations
throughout Japan and Southeast Asia, employs approximately 120 military personnel, 325 civilians and
2,500 Japanese workers, and operates facilities at Yokosuka, at Sasebo Naval Base and in Singapore.
FACT: the same report states that the commanding officer had highlighted the command's 2025
accomplishments at a New Year's event in January, including more than 714,000 man-days of ship
repair and modernization work, support for maintenance operations in Singapore, and emergency
response after a fire aboard the amphibious transport dock USS *New Orleans*.

FACT: USNI News reported on June 4, 2026 that Navy Regional Maintenance Center commander Rear Adm.
Dan Lannamann had removed the commanding officer, the executive officer and the command master chief
"due to a loss of confidence in their ability to command," and that the Navy statement said "there
is no impact to the command's mission as a result of these reliefs." FACT: the same USNI report
states that a source familiar with the decision said the reliefs were due to conduct, not
performance, but was unable to provide further details.

FACT: Stars and Stripes reported that this was the second such removal in twenty months, that the
previous commanding officer and executive officer were relieved in October 2024, that Rear Adm.
Lannamann had himself temporarily commanded the facility after those removals until the relieved
commanding officer took over in February 2025, and that the supervisor of salvage and diving, the Navy office responsible for wreck removal, ship
salvage and diving rather than for scheduled repair, has now assumed the commander's duties pending
a permanent replacement.

Assessment: the only sourced statement about cause points away from delivery performance, and the
timing invites the opposite reading. Resist it. A yard that finished a nuclear carrier availability
on time in May and lost its top three in June is not evidence that the two are connected, and the
one person quoted on the question says they are not.

Assessment: what the facts above do establish is a continuity problem. By the dates given, the
relieved commanding officer held the job from February 2025 to June 2026, roughly sixteen months;
the officer who removed him had himself been the stopgap after the previous pair went; and the
current stopgap is a salvage and diving officer. Two complete turnovers in twenty months means the
people who carry the command's method are its 325 civilians and 2,500 Japanese employees, because
they are the ones who were there for all of it.

Assessment: the figure to write down is 714,000 man-days in calendar 2025, across three sites in two
countries plus Singapore, on 7th Fleet surface ships at both intermediate and depot level. That is
the scale of the forward node, and it is stated by the command about its own output rather than
audited. The open question this issue cannot answer is whether that level holds through a second
leadership reset, and the place it would show first is delivery dates on 7th Fleet availabilities.

**Bottom line:** the forward node did 714,000 man-days in 2025 across Yokosuka, Sasebo and
Singapore, it has turned over its entire command team twice in twenty months, and the only sourced
account says the reliefs were about conduct rather than output.

### Deckplate: the grievance process went away, and the supervisor's day changed with it

FACT: Government Executive reported on April 16, 2026 that an April 9, 2026 memorandum from the
Secretary of Defense directed termination of all collective bargaining agreements to which the
department is a party within twenty-four hours, citing national security, and that the exemption
list covered unions already protected by court order, agency police, guards and firefighters, and
Federal Wage System workers at four named installations: Letterkenny Munitions Center, the Air
Force Test Center, the Air Force Sustainment Center and Fleet Readiness Center Southeast. No naval
shipyard appears on that list. The Federal Wage System is the pay schedule covering trades and
craft workers, as distinct from the salaried General Schedule.

FACT: Maine Public reported on April 20, 2026 that at Portsmouth Naval Shipyard agreements covering
several hundred workers were terminated, that the Portsmouth Metal Trades Council's agreement
survives only because of a court injunction, and quoted the American Federation of Government
Employees' David Gonzalez saying "when you take away collective bargaining, there's no grievance
process."

FACT: Government Executive reported on July 22, 2026 that the American Federation of Government
Employees and the National Federation of Federal Employees had moved for a preliminary injunction
to restore the agreements, documenting management telling members the union "does not exist any
longer," employees denied representation in disciplinary proceedings, and schedules changed
unilaterally with little notice.

Assessment: strip out the politics and what is left is a change to the mechanics of a first-line
supervisor's day. Three routine things that used to have a defined path now do not: how a discipline
case proceeds, how a schedule change is communicated, and where a complaint goes. Those are the
three the union official quoted above named, and they are the three a first-line supervisor touches
most often.

Assessment: the planning problem is that this may reverse on a court's timetable rather than an
operational one. One agreement at Portsmouth already survives only by injunction and two unions are
asking a court to restore the rest, so a supervisor who rebuilds their practice around having no
grievance process may have to rebuild it again. Practices that do not depend on which way that goes
are the safer investment: writing down why a decision was made, and telling people about a schedule
change before it happens, are practices no agreement required and no termination removed.

**Bottom line:** the contractual machinery around discipline, scheduling and complaints has been
removed at a comparable yard and is being litigated, so the durable move is the practice that works
under either regime.

*Sources: [Hegseth orders termination of union contracts](https://www.govexec.com/workforce/2026/04/hegseth-orders-termination-union-contracts/412899/); [Trump admin ends collective bargaining agreements with 2 Portsmouth Naval Shipyard unions](https://www.mainepublic.org/business-and-economy/2026-04-20/trump-admin-ends-collective-bargaining-agreements-with-2-portsmouth-naval-shipyard-unions); [Unions ask for injunction restoring collective bargaining](https://www.govexec.com/workforce/2026/07/unions-ask-injunction-restoring-collective-bargaining-defense/414943/)* {.sources}

## Maintenance Engineering & Technologies

### The Navy's shipboard air conditioning has one supplier, and the contract says so out loud

FACT: the federal award record for contract N6449825D4018 shows Naval Surface Warfare Center
Philadelphia Division holding an indefinite-delivery, indefinite-quantity vehicle with Johnson
Controls Navy Systems LLC, signed September 18, 2025 and running to September 17, 2028, with a base
and all options value of \$202,336,475, coded "SINGLE AWARD," "ONLY ONE SOURCE," "NOT COMPETED" and
"ONLY ONE SOURCE-OTHER (FAR 6.302-1 OTHER)." FACT: the record for N6449825D4036 shows a second
vehicle with the same company and the same office, signed September 12, 2025 and running to
September 14, 2028, at \$197,225,022, carrying the identical competition coding. An
indefinite-delivery vehicle is a standing contract with a ceiling; the actual work arrives later as
delivery orders placed against it.

FACT: both USASpending records give the same description, "NEW CONSTRUCTION UNIT MARINEPAK R-134A,"
and classify Johnson Controls Navy Systems under air-conditioning and commercial and industrial
refrigeration equipment manufacturing. FACT: both USASpending records also show a total obligation
of zero.

Assessment: so the roughly \$399.6 million those two ceilings add up to is contract capacity that has
been set up, not money that has been spent. Treat it as the size of the channel rather than the size
of the order book.

A MarinePak is the packaged chiller unit that makes a warship's air conditioning and its cooling
water for combat systems; R-134a is the refrigerant such a unit runs on.

Assessment: one company holds both vehicles, the government has recorded in each that no other
source exists, and the description field on both says new construction.

Open question, and this issue does not answer it: through what vehicle the in-service fleet buys a
replacement plant. These two records are titled for new construction and are the ones read here.
Whether backfit work runs through them, through separate contracts, or through the same production
line under a different label is not established by these records, and it is the question that
decides whether a repair availability actually queues behind new hulls.

Assessment: the more useful signal is the competition coding, and it is worth reading literally.
"Only one source" under FAR 6.302-1 is not a market opinion or an industry study. It is a
contracting officer's formal written determination that no alternative supplier exists, recorded in
the award. Where that code appears on a component your availability depends on, the schedule risk is
not price and it is not competition; it is that there is exactly one queue.

**Bottom line:** read the "only one source" code as a schedule input, not a procurement detail,
because it is the government stating in writing that the item your availability waits on has exactly
one queue.

*Sources: [NSWC Philadelphia / Johnson Controls IDV N6449825D4018](https://api.usaspending.gov/api/v2/awards/CONT_IDV_N6449825D4018_9700/); [NSWC Philadelphia / Johnson Controls IDV N6449825D4036](https://api.usaspending.gov/api/v2/awards/CONT_IDV_N6449825D4036_9700/)* {.sources}

## Operations Maintenance & Readiness

### The Navy has written down that it is done with the long availability

FACT: the Navy Shipbuilding Plan of May 2026 states that maintenance "will be performed through PAE
Industrial Operations," a single organisation that "combines control of the Navy Regional Maintenance Centers, NAVSEA's
Industrial Operations Directorate, the four Naval Shipyards and all Navy Regional Maintenance
Centers." FACT: the same plan states that "the Navy will no longer attempt to restore all
capabilities and add upgrades in a single, lengthy maintenance availability," moving instead to
"smaller, more frequent availabilities." FACT: it also commits to reestablishing Shore Intermediate
Maintenance Activities so sailors can perform "more advanced maintenance," and to a right-to-repair
push against "proprietary lock-in on technical data packages." A Shore Intermediate Maintenance
Activity is a shore command that does repair work above the ship's own crew but below a depot.

Assessment: read plainly, "smaller, more frequent" means more availabilities per year for the same
fleet, and the plan as quoted does not say how many more or how much smaller. Those two figures are
what a yard would need to size itself, because annual man-days and annual availability count can
move in opposite directions under this change. Asking for them is the concrete next step.

A technical data package is the drawings, specifications and procedures needed to make or repair a
part.

Assessment: the right-to-repair line is worth watching because the plan names it as an objective
alongside the organisational change and the availability change, rather than as an aspiration. What
the plan as quoted does not state is which systems are locked, or what the Navy would do differently
once they were not.

Assessment: and the reestablishment of intermediate activities cuts both ways. The plan's stated
purpose is to let sailors perform "more advanced maintenance," which by its own terms moves work
that a crew or shore activity can do away from a depot. What that leaves behind is the work they
cannot do. Whether that is a net relief or a harder mix is not something the plan states, and it is
worth watching rather than assuming.

**Bottom line:** the Navy has committed in writing to more and shorter availabilities under one new
maintenance organisation, and the two figures it has not published, how many more and how much
shorter, are the ones to ask for.

*Sources: [Navy Shipbuilding Plan, May 2026](https://media.defense.gov/2026/May/11/2003928909/-1/-1/1/NAVY%20SHIPBUILDING%20PLAN%20MAY%202026.PDF)* {.sources}

## Business Practices & Strategy

### A bill that would assign whole ship classes to single yards for five years

FACT: section 1018 of H.R. 8800, as reported in the House on June 15, 2026, directs the Navy to
enter a single indefinite-delivery, indefinite-quantity contract for maintenance, repair and
overhaul of surface combatants and to issue task orders "across availability periods" to private
yards, with a separate task order for each category of vessel — a task order being the same
mechanism as the delivery orders described earlier, an individual job placed against a standing
contract — makes the yard holding a task order
responsible for all routine, scheduled or corrective life-cycle maintenance on every vessel assigned
to it, and provides that no contractor's term may be shorter than five years. FACT: the same section
requires the Navy, before awarding and within 180 days of enactment, to report vessel categories,
parts and supply-chain forecasts, standardized work-item sequencing and bundling, and an assessment
of workforce capacity and dry dock and pier availability, and directs the Secretary in setting
categories to consider "the need to ensure adequate competition, and prevent the over-concentration
of workload, among private shipyards."

Assessment: this replaces competing for availabilities one at a time with being assigned a class of
ship for a term of years. Whether a yard sits inside or outside a category would decide five years
of workload in a single award, and the mandated pre-award report is the Navy formally writing down
whose docks it plans to rely on. That report is the document to read when it appears, because it is
a published statement of who the government thinks the industrial base is.

FACT: the Government Publishing Office's own printing of that text labels it "H.R. 8800 Reported in
House (RH)" and carries it on Union Calendar No. 606 under Report No. 119-698, which is the printed bill as a
committee reported it rather than as either chamber passed it.

Assessment: so the honest frame is that none of this is law, and the stage matters more than usual.
A section in reported text is evidence of what a committee wanted, not of what will govern a
contract, and every word of section 1018 could be amended or struck on the floor or in conference.
The planning value is in the direction rather than the date. This desk did not verify the current
floor status of either chamber's bill for this issue, and no date should be inferred from the
above.

Assessment: two of the section's own requirements are worth putting together. It instructs the
Secretary to consider preventing over-concentration of workload, and it requires an assessment of
workforce capacity and dry dock and pier availability before any award. Both sit inside a structure
that assigns a whole vessel category to one holder for at least five years. So a yard's own capacity
figures would become an input to who gets a category, which is a different exercise from bidding a
single availability on price and schedule.

**Bottom line:** the direction of travel is multi-year assignment of whole ship categories to single
yards, it exists so far only as committee-reported text, and the document that would matter first is
the pre-award capacity report rather than the award itself.

*Sources: [H.R. 8800, section 1018, as reported in the House](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-119hr8800rh/html/BILLS-119hr8800rh.htm)* {.sources}
