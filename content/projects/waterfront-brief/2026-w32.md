The Navy's May shipbuilding plan says three things about maintenance that belong together, and this
issue takes them one at a time. It is abandoning the long, everything-at-once availability. It says
its own handling of intellectual property has produced "excessive non-competitive sole-source
awards" that degrade organic repair capability, and there is a \$399.6 million contract pair showing
exactly what that looks like on one destroyer class. And a House committee has reported a bill that
would assign whole categories of surface combatant to single yards for five-year terms, which is
still text on a page and cannot be awarded until a report reaches Congress.

Running underneath is the forward yard at Yokosuka, which delivered seven ships on or ahead of
schedule between January and April and then lost its entire command team in June, for the second
time in twenty months. Labels: FACT is attributed to the named source in the same sentence;
Assessment is this desk's analysis, built only on facts stated above it; Speculation is flagged as
such.

## Waterfront Ops & Production

### Two command teams gone in twenty months, and the delivery record never moved

FACT: the Defense Visual Information Distribution Service reported on March 27, 2026 that the Naval
Ship Repair Facility and Japan Regional Maintenance Center at Yokosuka completed maintenance periods
on three warships in January, returning the destroyers USS *Howard* and USS *Higgins* and the mine
countermeasures ship USS *Chief* to the fleet on or ahead of schedule, and quoted commanding officer
Capt. Wendel Penetrante saying "completing CNO availabilities on time is a tremendous effort;
accomplishing them ahead of schedule is even more challenging." A Chief of Naval Operations
availability is a maintenance period large enough that Navy headquarters, not a local command, owns
its schedule and its money.

FACT: the Navy reported on May 13, 2026 that the same command returned the destroyer USS *Ralph
Johnson* to the fleet on time on April 6, and that "this year, SRF-JRMC has delivered all seven of
its completed ships to the fleet on time or ahead of schedule," being two mine countermeasures
ships, three destroyers and an amphibious transport dock between January and April. FACT: the
Defense Visual Information Distribution Service reported on June 28, 2026 that the command then
returned the destroyer USS *Shoup* and the aircraft carrier USS *George Washington* on time on May
11 and May 13.

FACT: the Navy announced on June 3, 2026 that Rear Adm. Dan Lannamann, commander of Navy Regional
Maintenance Center, had relieved the commanding officer, the executive officer and the command
master chief "due to a loss of confidence in their ability to command," that the Supervisor of
Salvage and Diving had temporarily assumed the commanding officer's duties, and that "there is no
impact to the command's mission as a result of these reliefs." FACT: USNI News reported on June 4,
2026 that a source familiar with the decision said the reliefs were due to conduct, not performance,
but was unable to provide further details. FACT: Stars and Stripes reported on June 3, 2026 that
this was the second such removal in twenty months, that the previous commanding officer and
executive officer were relieved in October 2024, and that Rear Adm. Lannamann had himself
temporarily commanded the facility after those removals until Capt. Penetrante took over in February
2025.

Assessment: the sequence is seven ships on or ahead of schedule from January to April, two more on
time in May, and in June the entire command team removed, for the second time since October 2024.
The deliveries were completed before the removals, and the only sourced account of cause says
conduct rather than performance, so nothing in this record suggests the output was a leadership
product that left with the leaders.

FACT: the Navy's and the Defense Visual Information Distribution Service's releases both credit a
specific group. In the March and the May releases Capt. Penetrante attributed the results to "the dedication and professionalism
of the ships' crews, the ship superintendents and their teams, and the entire SRF-JRMC
organization," and both releases describe the availabilities as work bringing together the ship's
crew, the command's Japanese master labor contractors, local Japanese contractors and US-based
companies. FACT: Stars and Stripes reported that the organisation employs approximately 120 military
personnel, 325 civilians and 2,500 Japanese workers across Yokosuka, Sasebo and Singapore, and that
Capt. Penetrante had cited more than 714,000 man-days of repair and modernization work for calendar
2025.

Assessment: of roughly 2,945 people, 120 are military. The command replaced its three most senior
uniformed positions twice in twenty months; the other 2,825 were there for all of it, and so was the
delivery record. Master labor contractors are the Japanese employees hired under the labour
agreement between the two governments, which is a workforce a US yard cannot replicate, but the
superintendent layer named alongside them is one every yard has.

FACT: on recognition from above, the most recent this desk found is dated: the Navy announced in
September 2023 that the command had won the fiscal 2022 Chief of Naval Operations Shore Safety Award
in the Large Industrial Activity category and the fiscal 2022 Secretary of the Navy Safety
Excellence Award, having also won the CNO award in 2021, and quoted the command's civilian Executive
Director, John Lund, saying "I am very proud of our Japanese and American workers who watch out for
each other."

Assessment: that a civilian Executive Director sits alongside the rotating military triad is the
transferable point. A command that absorbs two leadership removals without a delivery slip has its
continuity somewhere, and the two candidates named in its own releases are the senior civilian
executive and the ship superintendents. The bench that carried this one is not the one at the top of
the organisation chart.

Open question, and it is the one that would settle this: whether anything has slipped since June.
No delivery announcement from the command after June 28, 2026 was found for this issue, which is not
evidence either way — a quiet two months is what a normal summer looks like. The next delivery
release is the test.

**Bottom line:** seven on-time deliveries, then the whole command team removed, twice in twenty
months, with 2,825 of about 2,945 people unchanged throughout — a case for treating the
superintendent bench and the senior civilian layer as the thing that holds a schedule, pending the
first post-relief delivery.

## Operations Maintenance & Readiness

### Smaller, more frequent availabilities: what that does to a schedule, and to a hull

FACT: the Navy Shipbuilding Plan of May 2026 states that "increased operational tempo continues to
strain execution of established class maintenance plans," that "the Navy will no longer attempt to
restore all capabilities and add upgrades in a single, lengthy maintenance availability," and that
it is "integrating operations and maintenance into a maintenance continuum that will use smaller,
more frequent availabilities to do needed work while increasing operational availability." A class
maintenance plan is the engineering schedule saying which inspections and renewals a class of ship
needs and when, and it is the document that protects material condition across a service life.

FACT: the same plan states "we must fix our maintenance schedules with disciplined planning measured
to the hour, because every day a ship sits in dry dock beyond plan is a day we lend initiative to an
adversary," and describes a "World Class Planning" approach using artificial intelligence and
machine learning, "digital twins" that "virtually replicate its ships and simulate decades of wear,"
and prediction of "which parts will be needed months in advance." FACT: it also states the Navy is
"implementing an innovative set of contracting strategies like multi-ship sustained partnerships in
private shipyards" and "awarding contracts earlier for complex maintenance to increase planning time
and material procurement."

Assessment: the plan states the chain itself. Operational tempo is straining the class maintenance
plan; rather than defend the plan, the Navy is changing the delivery model so ships come back
faster. The objective it names is
increasing operational availability, which means days at sea, and the mechanism is doing less per
visit and visiting more often.

Assessment: for a yard executing this, the first effect is churn rather than volume. Annual man-days
can hold flat while availability starts and finishes rise, and those are different planning
problems. Each start needs a work package, a crew assignment and a material call; each finish needs
a test program and a turnover. A yard sized on man-days can be correctly sized and still late,
because the binding constraint moves to how many simultaneous starts its planning and superintendent
capacity can carry.

Assessment, and this is the part the plan does not address: what happens to work that only fits in a
long availability. Some renewals are not divisible. Tank and void preservation, shaft and stern tube
work, main reduction gear access, and hull structure behind installed equipment all need the ship
open, dry and torn down, and the cost of opening is largely fixed however much work you do once you
are in. Splitting one long availability into several short ones either pays that opening cost
repeatedly or defers the work that requires it. The plan states the tempo problem and states the
delivery-model answer. It does not say which of those two outcomes it expects.

**Speculation, flagged as such:** if deferral is what happens, the signature would be a widening gap
between planned and actual growth work — the additional work discovered once a ship is opened up —
appearing as more of it found late in shorter availabilities. This desk has no data showing that. It is offered as the thing to watch, not as a
finding.

**Bottom line:** plan for more starts and finishes rather than more hours, and treat the indivisible
docking work as the risk this change carries, because the plan names the tempo problem and the
delivery answer without saying what becomes of work that cannot be split.

*Sources: [Navy Shipbuilding Plan, May 2026](https://media.defense.gov/2026/May/11/2003928909/-1/-1/1/NAVY%20SHIPBUILDING%20PLAN%20MAY%202026.PDF)* {.sources}

## Maintenance Engineering & Technologies

### The Navy says its own IP practice creates sole-source lock-in. Here is what one looks like.

FACT: the Navy Shipbuilding Plan of May 2026 states that "the DON has systematically undervalued its
IP rights during contract formulation, resulting in proprietary lock-in on technical data packages,
software, and sustainment data," and that "this has led to excessive non-competitive sole-source
awards in follow-on procurement and sustainment, driving up total ownership costs and degrading the
Department's organic maintenance capability." A technical data package is the drawings,
specifications and procedures needed to make or repair a part; without rights to it, only the
original manufacturer can lawfully supply or overhaul the item.

FACT: federal award records at USASpending show Naval Surface Warfare Center Philadelphia Division
holding two indefinite-delivery vehicles with Johnson Controls Navy Systems LLC, one signed
September 18, 2025 and running to September 17, 2028 at \$202,336,475 and one signed September 12,
2025 and running to September 14, 2028 at \$197,225,022, both coded "SINGLE AWARD," "ONLY ONE
SOURCE," "NOT COMPETED" and "ONLY ONE SOURCE-OTHER (FAR 6.302-1 OTHER)," and both described as "NEW
CONSTRUCTION UNIT MARINEPAK R-134A." FACT: those same records show a total obligation of zero on
both vehicles, so that \$399.6 million is contract capacity established rather than money spent.

FACT: the USASpending record for an April 23, 2026 order carrying the same "only one source"
coding, worth \$8,022,276, describes it as "DDG 51 FLT II JOHNSON CONTROLS NAVY SYSTEMS (JCNS) HIGH EFFICIENCY
SUPER CAPACITY (HESC) NEW CONSTRUCTION AIR CONDITIONING PLANTS AND BACKFIT KITS," a backfit kit
being the version fitted to a ship already in service rather than one under construction.

Assessment: the scope is a destroyer programme, not the fleet. These records name the DDG 51 class,
and the April order covers backfit kits as well as new-construction plants, so
this is not a case of in-service work queuing behind new hulls. It is a case of one class's air
conditioning plants, new and backfit alike, having exactly one lawful supplier, recorded as such by
a contracting officer under FAR 6.302-1.

Assessment, with its limit stated first: nothing read here establishes that this particular
arrangement is an intellectual property case. A determination under FAR 6.302-1 can rest on data
rights, but it can equally rest on qualification, tooling or a design nobody else has been approved
to build. The document that would settle it is the Justification and Approval behind the award, and
this desk has not read it. So treat the plan's sentence and this record as two separate facts that
describe the same condition, not as cause and instance.

Assessment: what the record does establish is worth the reading time anyway. "Only one source" is not
a market opinion, it is a formal written determination that no alternative supplier exists. Where it
appears against a component in your work package, the exposure is not price and not competition; it
is that there is one queue and no second one to join. The cheap habit is to check how a long-lead
item's last award was coded before assuming a market exists behind it.

**Bottom line:** the Navy has written down that its own intellectual property practice manufactures
sole-source dependence, and separately there is a documented one-source arrangement covering a
destroyer class's chiller plants new and backfit — read the competition coding on your long-lead
items as a schedule input, and read the Justification and Approval before assuming you know why.

*Sources: [Navy Shipbuilding Plan, May 2026](https://media.defense.gov/2026/May/11/2003928909/-1/-1/1/NAVY%20SHIPBUILDING%20PLAN%20MAY%202026.PDF); [NSWC Philadelphia / Johnson Controls IDV N6449825D4018](https://api.usaspending.gov/api/v2/awards/CONT_IDV_N6449825D4018_9700/); [NSWC Philadelphia / Johnson Controls IDV N6449825D4036](https://api.usaspending.gov/api/v2/awards/CONT_IDV_N6449825D4036_9700/); [DDG 51 Flt II HESC chiller plants and backfit kits, order N6449826FX105](https://api.usaspending.gov/api/v2/awards/CONT_AWD_N6449826FX105_9700_N6449825D4036_9700/)* {.sources}

## Business Practices & Strategy

### The five-year, one-yard-per-category bill, and how far it is from real

FACT: section 1018 of H.R. 8800, as reported in the House, directs the Secretary of the Navy to
enter a single indefinite-delivery, indefinite-quantity contract for the maintenance, repair and
overhaul of "covered vessels" and to issue task orders "across availability periods" by private
shipyards, "with a separate task order for each category of covered vessel," makes the yard holding
a task order responsible for "any routine, scheduled, or corrective life-cycle maintenance or repair
activity of such vessels assigned to such shipyard," and requires that no contractor's term be "less
than five years." FACT: the section defines a covered vessel as "a surface combatant vessel, as such
term is defined in section 8227(e) of title 10, United States Code."

Assessment: two things that structure does not do are worth saying plainly. It is organised by
category of ship rather than by port, so it is not a homeport-based assignment. And it is one
contract with task orders beneath it rather than a set of separate awards, so the categories get
decided once and then govern for at least five years.

FACT: the section bars entering the contract or issuing any task order until the Secretary submits a
report to the congressional defense committees, due not later than 180 days after enactment,
containing the categories with "the number, classes, and anticipated availability schedules of
vessels" in each, parts and supply-chain forecasting, "standardized work-item sequencing, bundling,
and planning requirements," an assessment of "the adequacy of facilities... including with respect
to workforce capacity, dry dock and pier availability," a cost baseline, and an identification of
risks from "schedule disruptions, supply chain delays, or industrial base shortfalls."

FACT: the section also requires metrics for contractor performance, schedule adherence, cost against
baseline and "effects on the industrial base and workforce sustainment," annual reports for five
years, and provides at subsection (e) that the foreign shipyard restrictions of 10 U.S.C. § 8680
apply to any task order issued under it.

Assessment: so nothing has been awarded on this authority and nothing can be for some time. The
sequence is enactment, then a report inside 180 days, then a contract, then task orders. The bill it
sits in is committee-reported text, and this desk did not verify the floor status of either
chamber's bill for this issue, so no date should be inferred from any of the above.

Assessment: the document worth wanting is the report, not the award. A published statement of which
classes group into which category, with anticipated availability schedules and an assessment of dry
dock and pier adequacy, is the Navy writing down whose capacity it is counting on. That is useful
whether or not the contract ever exists, and it arrives first.

**Bottom line:** a real structural proposal that is nowhere near real yet, and the thing to watch
for is the 180-day report on categories and facility adequacy rather than a contract announcement.

*Sources: [H.R. 8800, section 1018, as reported in the House](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-119hr8800rh/html/BILLS-119hr8800rh.htm)* {.sources}

### Deckplate: the grievance process went away, and the supervisor's day changed with it

FACT: Government Executive reported in April 2026 that an April 9, 2026 memorandum from the
Secretary of Defense directed termination of all collective bargaining agreements to which the
department is a party within twenty-four hours, citing national security, that agreements already subject to a
court order enjoining implementation were left alone, and that the memo's exemptions covered agency
police, guards and firefighters, and Federal Wage System workers at four named installations: Letterkenny Munitions Center, the Air Force
Test Center, the Air Force Sustainment Center and Fleet Readiness Center Southeast. No naval
shipyard appears on that list. The Federal Wage System is the pay schedule covering trades and craft
workers, as distinct from the salaried General Schedule.

FACT: Maine Public reported on April 20, 2026 that at Portsmouth Naval Shipyard agreements covering
several hundred workers were terminated, that the Portsmouth Metal Trades Council's agreement
survives only because of a court injunction, and quoted the American Federation of Government
Employees' David Gonzalez saying "when you take away collective bargaining, there's no grievance
process." FACT: Government Executive reported in July 2026 that two federal unions had moved for a
preliminary injunction to restore the agreements, documenting management telling members the union
"does not exist any longer," employees denied representation in disciplinary proceedings, and
schedules changed unilaterally with little notice.

Assessment: set the politics aside and what the reported facts describe is procedural. On the
account above, three things that had a defined path now do not: how a discipline case proceeds, how
a schedule change is communicated, and where a complaint goes.

Assessment: the planning problem is that this may reverse on a court's timetable rather than an
operational one. One Portsmouth agreement already survives only by injunction and two unions are
asking a court to restore the rest, so practices that do not depend on the outcome are the safer
investment: writing down why a decision was made, and telling people about a schedule change before
it happens, are things no agreement required and no termination removed.

Open question this issue does not answer: the position at Norfolk, Puget Sound and Pearl Harbor. The
exemption list names no shipyard and Portsmouth is documented, but a yard-by-yard picture was not
established.

**Bottom line:** the contractual machinery around discipline, scheduling and complaints has been
removed at a comparable yard and is being litigated, so the durable move is the practice that works
under either outcome.

*Sources: [Hegseth orders termination of union contracts](https://www.govexec.com/workforce/2026/04/hegseth-orders-termination-union-contracts/412899/); [Trump admin ends collective bargaining agreements with 2 Portsmouth Naval Shipyard unions](https://www.mainepublic.org/business-and-economy/2026-04-20/trump-admin-ends-collective-bargaining-agreements-with-2-portsmouth-naval-shipyard-unions); [Unions ask for injunction restoring collective bargaining](https://www.govexec.com/workforce/2026/07/unions-ask-injunction-restoring-collective-bargaining-defense/414943/)* {.sources}
