Five items. The Department tore up its collective bargaining agreements on twenty-four hours' notice,
including at Portsmouth, and is now defending that in court. The Navy has decided that sailors should
not be painting and insulating during a carrier's midlife overhaul, and has put 690,000 contracted
labour hours behind that view with five more contracts due. Then: the \$448 million data programme
arriving at the nuclear yards, a maintenance centre printing a part for \$131 that the supply system
prices at \$300,000, and the fifth public shipyard, rejected in 2022, now pushed by the budget office.

## Waterfront Ops & Production

### Deckplate: the grievance process went away in twenty-four hours

FACT: a memorandum signed by the Secretary on April 9, 2026 directs "the termination of all
collective bargaining agreements to which the Department is a party, not subject to a court order
enjoining implementation of Executive Order (EO) 14251… within 24 hours of the date of this
memorandum," excepting a population certified in the Federal Register in April 2025 and "the local
employing offices of any agency police officers, security guards, or firefighters." A collective
bargaining agreement is the contract between an agency and a union that fixes working conditions and,
crucially for a shipyard, the grievance procedure — the route by which a disputed discipline,
assignment or safety call gets adjudicated.

FACT: Maine Public reported on April 20, 2026 that the terminations covered two unions representing
"several hundred workers at the Portsmouth Naval Shipyard," quoting American Federation of Government
Employees national vice president David Gonzalez: "When you take away collective bargaining, there's
no grievance process." FACT: the same report quotes Portsmouth Metal Trades Council president Alana
Schaeffer saying her union's agreement "is shielded for now by a court injunction."

FACT: Government Executive reported on July 22, 2026 that two federal employee unions asked a judge
for a preliminary injunction restoring their agreements, arguing not that the executive order is
unlawful but that the Secretary failed the "reasoned decision-making" standard of the Administrative
Procedure Act and "did not point to any facts showing that any of plaintiffs' CBAs, much less all of
them, were inconsistent with national security."

Assessment: the shape of that legal argument is what makes this a scheduling matter and not only a
labour one. As reported, the unions are not asking a court to rule the executive order unlawful; they
are challenging how the termination was carried out. A challenge aimed at the manner of a decision
leaves the decision itself available to the government, so a win for the unions need not be permanent.
For a production planner that means the grievance route's availability is a variable rather than a
constant, and it already differs between bargaining units at one yard: Portsmouth has a council
shielded by an injunction alongside two whose agreements were terminated.

**Bottom line:** find out, unit by unit, which of your yard's bargaining agreements are terminated and
which sit behind an injunction, because the answer differs inside one shipyard and a
terminated agreement removes the negotiated route without this desk having established what statutory
process replaces it.

## Operations Maintenance & Readiness

### The Navy just moved 690,000 hours of painting and preservation off the crew

FACT: a Navy release of June 15, 2026 states that the Navy Quality of Service Cross-Functional Team
and PAE Maritime's In-Service Aircraft Carrier Program Office "are changing how the Navy does business
for the execution of aircraft carrier Refueling and Complex Overhaul (RCOH), beginning with USS Harry
S. Truman (CVN 75)"; that sailors on a carrier in that overhaul traditionally performed "supplementary,
non-rate-specific duties such as painting, insulating, transportation services, and maintenance"; and
that using commercial contracts for that work "will potentially return up to one million man-hours to
the crew." A refuelling and complex overhaul is the once-in-a-lifetime refit at the midpoint of a
carrier's fifty-year service life, which is why the crew is alongside for years rather than weeks.

FACT: the same release states that "to date, five Navy contracts have returned approximately 690,000
labor hours to the crew, with plans to award five additional contracts over the next five years," and
that the approach "allows for optimized, smaller crew sizes." FACT: USNI News reported on June 12, 2026
that "as of Thursday, the switch to contractors has cost about \$32 million," and that HII's Newport News
Shipbuilding is performing the overhaul.

Assessment: this is the part worth acting on, and it is not the quality-of-life framing. A service has
decided in writing that ship's force is not the labour pool for preservation, insulation and
transportation during a shipyard period, and has put contracts behind that decision with five more
scheduled over five years.

That work is going to firms other than the prime, and the award record shows it.

FACT: federal award records at USASpending show contract N0002424C2101, worth \$17.6 million and held
by Mills Marine & Ship Repair LLC, described as "THIRD PARTY FIRE WATCH SUPPORT FOR CVN 74 AND, IF
OPTIONS ARE EXERCISED, CVN 75 RCOH," running from October 2023 to October 2026. FACT: the same record
codes that award as having received five offers. Fire watch is the standing watch kept on a compartment
while hot work is done in it, and it is a classic ship's-force duty. FACT: the same records show contract N0002424C2106 with Huntington Ingalls Inc, described as
"CVN 75 RCOH ADVANCE PLANNING," at \$1.22 billion.

Assessment: those two records are the argument. One is the prime holding the billion-dollar planning
scope; the other is a small marine repair company holding a \$17.6 million watch-standing contract on
the same class of availability, with the Truman overhaul written into it as an option — and five firms
bid for it. That is a concrete demonstration that this workstream reaches beyond the prime, and that
there is a field competing for it, rather than an inference about what primes want.

Assessment, on the two numbers, because the release carries both and they are not the same claim.
"Approximately 690,000 labor hours" is what five awarded contracts have delivered; "up to one million
man-hours" is what the approach might reach. One is a count, the other a ceiling, and quoting the
larger figure as achieved would be the ordinary way to get this wrong.

Assessment on price, offered as arithmetic rather than a rate: \$32 million against 690,000 hours works
out near \$46 an hour. The release does not say the money and the hours cover the same scope or period,
so treat that as indicative and not a billing rate — but it is the only public number that puts a price
on this work.

FACT: USNI News reported in January 2024 that HII's Newport News Shipbuilding was awarded a contract
with "total potential value of \$913 million" for advanced procurement for Truman's overhaul, and that
overhauls of this kind "account for about a third of the Newport News' business."

Assessment: set against \$1.22 billion of advance planning, a \$17.6 million watch-standing contract is a
rounding error on the overhaul and the whole point for anyone who is not Newport News. The counter-
evidence angle came back empty — this desk found no source arguing the shift is a bad idea or that it
will not hold — so nothing here has been tested against a critic. **Speculation, flagged as
such:** the obvious risk is that contracted preservation in an occupied shipyard is harder to schedule
than crew labour you already own. This desk has no evidence either way.

**Bottom line:** a named, recurring, non-nuclear workstream now exists at carrier overhauls, with five
more contracts due over five years and at least one already held by a small repair firm rather than the
prime — so the vehicle to watch is the third-party support contract, not the overhaul award.

*Sources: [Navy release, "Rethinking Sailors and CVN RCOH," Jun 15, 2026](https://www.navy.mil/Press-Office/News-Stories/display-news/Article/4516872/rethinking-sailors-and-cvn-rcoh-navy-leverages-industry-contracts-to-reclaim-up/); [USNI News, Jun 12, 2026](https://news.usni.org/2026/06/12/navy-wants-sailors-to-do-less-supplemental-work-on-uss-harry-s-truman-overhaul); [Federal News Network, Jul 2, 2026](https://federalnewsnetwork.com/navy/2026/07/navy-says-carrier-overhaul-shift-will-help-sailors/); [USNI News, Jan 26, 2024](https://news.usni.org/2024/01/26/hii-awarded-uss-harry-s-truman-913m-mid-life-overhaul-contract); [Award N0002424C2101, Mills Marine & Ship Repair](https://api.usaspending.gov/api/v2/awards/CONT_AWD_N0002424C2101_9700_-NONE-_-NONE-/); [Award N0002424C2106, Huntington Ingalls](https://api.usaspending.gov/api/v2/awards/CONT_AWD_N0002424C2106_9700_-NONE-_-NONE-/)* {.sources}

## Maintenance Engineering & Technologies

### A \$448 million data programme, and the two numbers being used to sell it

FACT: a Navy release of December 9, 2025 states the Navy is investing \$448 million in a programme
called Ship OS, built on software from the company Palantir, announced by Navy Secretary John Phelan
at the first Department of the Navy Rapid Capabilities Office industry day; that it is "managed by the
Maritime Industrial Base (MIB) Program in collaboration with Naval Sea Systems Command"; and that it
"will aggregate data from enterprise resource planning systems, legacy databases, and operational
sources to identify bottlenecks." An enterprise resource planning system is the business software a
yard runs its labour, material and scheduling records in, and the programme reads across those systems
rather than replacing them.

FACT: the same release states that during pilot deployments "at General Dynamics Electric Boat,
submarine schedule planning was reduced from 160 manual hours to under 10 minutes, while Portsmouth
Naval Shipyard cut material review times from weeks to under one hour," and that the initial
investment "will focus on Submarine Industrial Base shipbuilders, shipyards, and critical suppliers."

Assessment, on those two numbers. Each is a single reading of a single task at a single site, reported
by the parties announcing the programme. Neither comes with the spread it was drawn from: how long
that task ran at its best and worst before, how often it ran, what share of a planner's year it took.
A number with no run of data behind it cannot tell you whether a process changed or a good week got
written down. That is not a reason to disbelieve them; it is a reason not to plan against them.

FACT: Breaking Defense reported on December 10, 2025 that officials said the system "would be
proliferated through three of the public shipyards and some private shipyards," and quoted a Navy
spokesman: "The Navy intends to start with ShipOS at our nuclear shipyards and our nuclear repair
yards." FACT: DefenseScoop reported on December 23, 2025 that the work "involves a shared-savings
contract mechanism, where Palantir's payout is tied to the products' impact and metrics."

Assessment: a shared-savings arrangement means the vendor is paid out of the improvement it is measured
as having produced. That makes the baseline — what the yard's performance is deemed to have been before
the software arrived — a commercial term, not just an analytical one. **Unverified:** this desk found no public description of how that baseline is
set or who audits it, having read only the announcement release and three trade reports of it. For a
yard whose own records are the input, it is the term to read first.

**Bottom line:** treat the 160-hours-to-10-minutes figure as a demonstration rather than a rate, work
out whether you are inside the nuclear-first wave or the undated expansion after it, and read the
baseline definition in any shared-savings arrangement that touches your records.

*Sources: [Navy release, Dec 9, 2025](https://www.navy.mil/Press-Office/Press-Releases/display-pressreleases/Article/4355823/navy-invests-448-million-in-ai-and-autonomy-to-accelerate-shipbuilding/); [USNI News, Dec 9, 2025](https://news.usni.org/2025/12/09/navy-palantir-announce-448m-ship-os-ai-tool-for-shipbuilding-and-repair); [Breaking Defense, Dec 10, 2025](https://breakingdefense.com/2025/12/navy-palantir-unveil-shipos-in-a-bid-to-boost-nuclear-sub-production/); [DefenseScoop, Dec 23, 2025](https://defensescoop.com/2025/12/23/palantir-navy-shipos-partnership-informed-by-project-maven/)* {.sources}

### Printing the part nobody will sell you, and the line the Navy drew around it

FACT: a NAVSEA release of August 11, 2025 states that when a chilled water pump cooling rotor used on
an Arleigh Burke-class destroyer needed replacement, Southeast Regional Maintenance Center at Mayport
found the six-blade part "not available to purchase separately in the Navy supply system," so that
correcting the deficiency "typically requires the purchase of an entirely new pump," and that the team
instead "manufacture[d] four progressively refined prototypes over two weeks." FACT: the trade outlet
3DPrint.com reported on August 13, 2025 that the motor otherwise replaced "costs just over \$300,000,"
that the polymer prototype cost "approximately \$18," and that the final part "cost \$131.21," was
delivered by Fleet Readiness Center Southeast Detachment Mayport, and "was also certified in-house."

Assessment: the transferable part is the sequence — reverse-engineer the original, print a cheap
polymer copy to prove the fit, bring in the co-located fleet readiness detachment to choose the
production material, certify in house.

Assessment on the money: \$131.21 against \$300,000 is a real saving on one part, but it measures a
catalogue problem rather than a manufacturing one. The release says the rotor cannot be bought on its
own, so the \$300,000 is the price of the smallest thing the supply system would sell. One part is also
one part: nothing here establishes a rate.

FACT: Military Times reported on June 20, 2025 that "recent NAVSEA guidance allows waterfront
engineers to use additive manufacturing… to produce vessel parts and components classified as
low-risk — meaning parts that are not vital to the safety and function of the ship," and that the
Forward Deployed Regional Maintenance Center Detachment Rota, working with Spanish allies, printed
replacement eductors, or jet pumps, for the vacuum collection holding and transfer system — the
shipboard sewage system — aboard the destroyer Arleigh Burke.

Assessment: that low-risk boundary is both the permission and the ceiling. A chilled water rotor and a
sewage-system eductor are auxiliary items, and nothing in these releases describes a printed part
entering a propulsion, weapons or pressure-boundary system. A limit on that read: the guidance is
cited here only through Military Times' paraphrase, and this desk has not identified or read the
NAVSEA instruction that sets the classification, so what counts as low-risk is described rather than
defined.

**Bottom line:** the adoption path is demonstrated at maintenance-centre level and it is cheap, but it
runs only to parts the Navy classifies as low-risk, so the useful move is to find the auxiliary items
on your own supply-blocked list and the technical data package — the drawings and specifications needed to make a part — that would let you print them.

*Sources: [NAVSEA, Aug 11, 2025](https://www.navsea.navy.mil/Media/News/Article-View/Article/4271434/sermc-delivers-cost-and-schedule-savings-with-3d-printing/); [3DPrint.com, Aug 13, 2025](https://3dprint.com/319932/a-3d-printed-chilled-water-pump-cooling-rotor-the-u-s-navy-keeps-using-am-to-cut-costs/); [Military Times, Jun 20, 2025](https://www.militarytimes.com/news/your-military/2025/06/20/navy-uses-3-d-printing-to-manufacture-destroyer-parts/); [USNI News, Jun 23, 2026](https://news.usni.org/2026/06/23/the-navys-big-3-d-printing-bet)* {.sources}

## Business Practices & Strategy

### The fifth public shipyard is back, four years after the Navy said no

Week 29 covered the FY2027 shipbuilding request and week 30 the \$1.85 billion study of foreign yards.
This is the line that has moved since.

FACT: Breaking Defense reported on June 25, 2026 that Office of Management and Budget Director Russell
Vought said the administration is "pushing hard for an additional public shipyard" and that "to do it
at scale is something that is absolutely vital." FACT: the same report states the four public shipyards
are Norfolk, Portsmouth, Puget Sound and Pearl Harbor, and that Vought pointed to the April decision to
mothball the attack submarine Boise, "which hadn't operated at sea in over a decade."

The idea has been examined before, and the reasons it stalled are on the record.

FACT: Breaking Defense reported in May 2022 that a proposal to buy and build a fifth public shipyard
in Ohio had been pitched to the Navy twice and rejected the first time "due to cost and policy
concerns," with the service still seeing issues with a revised submission, and that any Great Lakes
plan faced "technical and legal hurdles." FACT: the same report describes the Shipyard Infrastructure
Optimization Plan, first pitched in 2018, as an investment "the service expects to take two decades to
complete," with lawmakers annoyed that the Navy "low-balled its initial cost estimates" and that
twenty years "is seen as just too long to wait."

Assessment: the advocate has changed, not the obstacle. In 2022 the case came from outside the Navy and
was refused on cost and policy; in 2026 it comes from the office that writes the budget, which moves
who owns the cost objection without answering it. Nothing in the current reporting names a site, a
design, a dry dock class or a date, and those are what a fifth yard actually consists of.

FACT: the Government Accountability Office reported in August 2020 that at the existing public
shipyards it can take up to four years for an apprentice to reach journeyman and five to seven years to
become fully trained.

Assessment: that training time is why a fifth yard matters long before any concrete is poured. On those
figures a new public shipyard cannot grow its own journeymen inside its first several years, so it must
hire trades that already exist, from the yards and private repair firms holding them today. Its opening decade
therefore draws on the same journeymen everyone else employs, which is a competition for people before
it is ever a change in workload.

**Bottom line:** watch for a named site rather than a named advocate, because the budget office wanting
a fifth yard changes nothing on a waterfront until a location exists — and when one does, the first
effect is on the wage bill for trades, not on the workload.

*Sources: [Breaking Defense, Jun 25, 2026](https://breakingdefense.com/2026/06/white-house-budget-director-calls-for-fifth-public-shipyard-amid-push-to-expand-fleet/); [Breaking Defense, May 9, 2022](https://breakingdefense.com/2022/05/a-tall-order-why-a-fifth-public-shipyard-might-not-solve-the-navys-problems/)* {.sources}
