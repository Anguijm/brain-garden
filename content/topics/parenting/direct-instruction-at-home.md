# Direct Instruction at home: teaching a child to read in 20 minutes a day

Yes, a parent can do this at home in 15 to 20 minutes a day. The home version of Siegfried Engelmann's method, the
book *Teach Your Child to Read in 100 Easy Lessons*, was written for one parent and one child at about 20 minutes a
lesson, and the small studies that exist found parents could follow the script and children learned to decode. For
a six-year-old who is starting English reading while going to school in Japanese, that book, five days a week, is
the plan; a three-year-old is better served by daily interactive picture-book reading and sound and letter games
than by formal lessons. Links to everything worth buying, for reading and for math, are in
[What to buy](#what-to-buy-with-links).

This note was prompted by the Sold a Story video "What is Direct Instruction?" (APM Reports, 2026-10-15).
FACT marks a claim checked against the source linked beside it; Assessment is the desk's judgment; Unverified marks a
source that could not be opened, with the reason. Where only an abstract was read, the label says so.

## 1. What Direct Instruction is, and how it differs from "direct instruction"

Direct Instruction with capital letters (DI) is a specific family of scripted programs built
by Siegfried "Zig" Engelmann from the 1960s on; lowercase direct or explicit instruction is a
general teaching style that many curricula share.

**Origins.** FACT: Engelmann had no education background; he worked in a steel mill and in
advertising, taught his own twin sons math from age 3, filmed them, and was hired by the
University of Illinois in 1964 ([S3](https://www.apmreports.org/episode/2026/10/15/sold-a-story-s2-e1-its-not-just-reading)). FACT: his 1964 experimental preschool ran two hours a
day, one hour of play, art and music and one hour of small-group lessons in math, reading and
language ([S4](https://www.apmreports.org/episode/2026/10/15/sold-a-story-s2-e2-the-remedy-is-always-teaching)). FACT: scripts were added a couple of years in, because teachers struggled to
deliver the lessons without them ([S4](https://www.apmreports.org/episode/2026/10/15/sold-a-story-s2-e2-the-remedy-is-always-teaching)). The school programs that grew out of this include
DISTAR, Reading Mastery, Corrective Reading, Connecting Math Concepts, Spelling Mastery and
Language for Learning ([S10](https://www.mheducation.com/prek-12/resources/research/library/literacy/direct-instruction-methodology.html), [S38](https://mheducation.co.uk/schools/elementary/direct-instruction/connecting-math-concepts), [S39](https://info.mheducation.com/rs/128-SJW-347/images/2025-EURO-DI-Catalogue.pdf)).

**Core features.** FACT, from APM Reports' companion article to the video ([S1](https://www.apmreports.org/story/2026/10/15/what-is-direct-instruction)):
lessons are fully scripted; every student answers aloud together after the teacher's signal
(choral response), with individual turns mixed in; errors are corrected immediately, and the
teacher returns to the student until the answer is right; each lesson is about 85 to 90
percent review and 10 to 15 percent new material; students are grouped by skill, not age; and
nobody moves on until the core content is mastered. The motto is "If the student hasn't
learned, the teacher hasn't taught" ([S1](https://www.apmreports.org/story/2026/10/15/what-is-direct-instruction), [S4](https://www.apmreports.org/episode/2026/10/15/sold-a-story-s2-e2-the-remedy-is-always-teaching)).

FACT: "faultless communication" is Engelmann's idea that the wording and the order of examples
should allow only one interpretation. It is implemented through five rules for placing
examples and non-examples side by side (same wording, minimal differences, varied examples
that share a label, then an unpredictable test) ([S9](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8458552/)). FACT: Engelmann called a child's wrong
generalization a "misrule" and treated it as a teaching flaw; his team taught "b" well before
"d" so the two would not be confused ([S1](https://www.apmreports.org/story/2026/10/15/what-is-direct-instruction), [S4](https://www.apmreports.org/episode/2026/10/15/sold-a-story-s2-e2-the-remedy-is-always-teaching)). FACT: the design premise is generalization from
a small engineered set, for example 600 word parts and 3 rules letting students spell 12,000
words ([S8](https://darkwing.uoregon.edu/~bgrossen/pubs/aftdi.htm)).

**Lowercase explicit instruction.** FACT: Rosenshine's widely cited summary lists ten
principles: start with a short review, present new material in small steps with practice
after each, ask many questions and check every student, model, guide practice, check
understanding, get a high success rate (about 80 percent is the level he reports for the more
effective teachers), scaffold, require independent practice, and review weekly and monthly
([S11](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/periodicals/Rosenshine.pdf)). Assessment: DI is the fully engineered, scripted end of that same family; lowercase
explicit instruction is the principles without the script.

## 2. The evidence, the critics, and Sold a Story

### What Sold a Story is

FACT: *Sold a Story* is an APM Reports podcast hosted by Emily Hanford. Season 1, from October
2022, argued that an idea about how children read, which cognitive scientists had disproved,
shaped classroom reading for a generation through Marie Clay, Lucy Calkins, Fountas and
Pinnell, and the publisher Heinemann ([S5](https://features.apmreports.org/sold-a-story/season-1/)). FACT: Season 2, "What Happened to Teaching," asks
why DI, which a large government study found outperformed the other approaches tested, never
caught on; its first two episodes and the "What is Direct Instruction?" piece were published
2026-10-15 ([S1](https://www.apmreports.org/story/2026/10/15/what-is-direct-instruction), [S2](https://features.apmreports.org/sold-a-story/season-2/)). FACT: the Season 2 trailer came out 2026-10-01 ([S7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sold_a_Story), secondary).

### Project Follow Through (1968 to 1977)

FACT (secondary, Wikipedia citing the Abt Associates report, Stebbins et al. 1977): Follow
Through compared about 20 sponsored teaching models across roughly 352,000 children in 178
projects at its peak; districts chose their model, so assignment was not random. The Abt report
said basic-skills models did better on basic skills and, unexpectedly, on self-concept, and no
model type did better on "cognitive conceptual" skills. It also said effectiveness "varied
substantially from site group to site group" ([S14](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Project_Follow_Through)). FACT (pro-DI source): DI ranked first in
reading, math, spelling and language and was the only model positive on basic skills,
cognitive skills and self-esteem ([S15](https://psych.athabascau.ca/open/engelmann/direct-evid.php)). FACT: APM's Season 2 says the final report found one
program "stood out above the rest" ([S3](https://www.apmreports.org/episode/2026/10/15/sold-a-story-s2-e1-its-not-just-reading)).

**What the critics found.** FACT: the main published critique is House, Glass, McLean and
Walker (1978) in the *Harvard Educational Review*; its abstract, which I read, only describes
the experiment ([S16](https://doi.org/10.17763/haer.48.2.j2167r4594027x87)). FACT (secondary): Wikipedia reports that the critique argued the tests
favored basic-skills models, that "basic skills" implied rote methods, that there was more
variation within a model than between models, and that some sites had dropped their model
([S14](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Project_Follow_Through)). FACT: Gene Glass, one of those authors, wrote that he found "no evidence" the program
had anything special or worth distributing widely ([S3](https://www.apmreports.org/episode/2026/10/15/sold-a-story-s2-e1-its-not-just-reading)). Assessment: the critics mainly
disputed what the comparison could prove (non-random sites, narrow tests, site variation),
not that DI sites scored well on reading and math.

### Stockard et al. 2018 meta-analysis

FACT: Stockard, Wood, Coughlin and Rasplica Khoury (2018) in the *Review of Educational
Research* pooled 328 studies, 413 designs and almost 4,000 effects from 1966 to 2016; every
estimated effect was positive ([S12](https://scholarsbank.uoregon.edu/items/9c5db747-52be-4bd8-8771-b05d0f7c0b01/full)). FACT, from the paper's tables ([S13](https://scholarsbank.uoregon.edu/bitstreams/e2c9095f-b061-4d56-a3a4-b8f4d76970d1/download)): the unadjusted
average effect was d = 0.54 overall, 0.51 for reading, 0.55 for math, 0.66 for spelling; the
adjusted estimates were 0.60 overall, 0.74 reading, 0.75 math, 1.07 spelling. Affective
outcomes were 0.33 unadjusted and not significant after adjustment. Starting in
kindergarten added to the effect (for example 0.15 for reading, 0.43 for math), and longer
exposure helped. Teacher training, the paper's only proxy for fidelity, was significant only
for language, which the authors attribute to the measure being crude ([S13](https://scholarsbank.uoregon.edu/bitstreams/e2c9095f-b061-4d56-a3a4-b8f4d76970d1/download)). Assessment:
"d = 0.5 to 0.75" means a typical DI student ended up roughly 20 to 27 percentile points ahead
of a comparable non-DI student on academic tests, which is large by education-research
standards. Two cautions: almost all the studies were of schools, not parents; and
**Unverified** (2026-10-17): the lead author is widely described as working with the
National Institute for Direct Instruction, a DI advocacy body, but I did not open an
affiliation page, so check the paper's author notes ([S13](https://scholarsbank.uoregon.edu/bitstreams/e2c9095f-b061-4d56-a3a4-b8f4d76970d1/download)).

### Longer-term worry: the High/Scope preschool comparison

FACT: in the High/Scope curriculum comparison, 68 low-income children were randomly assigned
at ages 3 and 4 to a traditional nursery school, a fast-paced DI preschool, or the High/Scope
program. All three groups gained about 27 IQ points in the first year. By age 23, 47 percent of
the DI group versus 6 percent of the other two had been treated for emotional impairment, and
39 percent of the DI group versus 10 percent of the High/Scope group had felony arrests ([S17](https://ecrp.illinois.edu/v6n1/weikart.html)).
FACT: that account is by High/Scope's own president, who notes DI developers challenged the
method ([S17](https://ecrp.illinois.edu/v6n1/weikart.html)). **Unverified** (2026-10-17): the primary paper, Schweinhart and Weikart (1997),
[doi:10.1016/s0885-2006(97)90009-0](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0885-2006(97)90009-0), is
paywalled and no abstract was retrievable. Assessment: one small study, about a full-day
preschool program for children at 3 and 4, run by the rival program's team. It is not evidence
about 20 minutes a day with a parent at 6, but it is a reason to keep a 3-year-old's day mostly
play and talk, which is what I recommend anyway.

### The science of reading in brief

FACT: the National Reading Panel's phonics meta-analysis found systematic phonics beat
non-systematic or no phonics, d = 0.41 overall, d = 0.55 when begun in kindergarten or first
grade versus 0.27 later, and it beat whole language; small groups were not less effective than
tutoring ([S20](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543071003393), abstract). FACT: its phonemic awareness meta-analysis found d = 0.86 on
awareness itself and 0.53 on reading; it worked for preschoolers; it worked better taught
with letters, one or two skills at a time, and over 5 to 18 total hours rather than longer
([S21](https://doi.org/10.1598/rrq.36.3.2), abstract). FACT, the critics: Camilli and colleagues re-estimated phonics at d = 0.24,
with systematic language activities 0.29 and tutoring 0.40, and argued the three combined do
more than phonics alone ([S22](https://doi.org/10.14507/epaa.v11n15.2003), abstract); Bowers (2020) argued 12 meta-analyses do not justify
the phonics consensus, while saying this is not an argument for whole language ([S23](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-019-09515-y),
abstract). Assessment: even the critics' numbers favor teaching letter-sound decoding
explicitly; their dispute is about how big the edge is and what else belongs alongside it
(talk, vocabulary, one-to-one time), which a parent at home is supplying anyway.

## 3. Home use: does it work one-to-one, and what to buy

### Teach Your Child to Read in 100 Easy Lessons (Engelmann, Haddox, Bruner, 1983; revised 2022)

FACT: co-author Phyllis Haddox's site describes "100 twenty-minute lessons" that are scripted
and color-coded, for ages 3 to 6, ending at about a second-grade level, with free training
videos and printable sound cards, word cards and a progress chart ([S25](https://www.startreading.com/)). FACT: its FAQ says the
program suits children from about age 4, has been used with "some precocious three-year-olds,"
and lists readiness signs: interest, following simple spoken instructions, speaking in
complete sentences, and staying on task for more than a few minutes ([S26](https://www.startreading.com/faq)). FACT: it uses a
special print (the FAQ calls it "funny" print) with a deliberate letter design and sequence,
starts with letter sounds rather than names, and from Lesson 73 transitions to ordinary print
([S26](https://www.startreading.com/faq)). FACT: the book is an adaptation for parents of the DISTAR "Fast Cycle" program, and a DI
tutor quoted by Fordham called its pace "quite challenging for some" ([S19](https://fordhaminstitute.org/national/commentary/addressing-learning-loss-one-easy-lesson)). FACT: the
Australian listing gives 420 pages ([S29](https://avidreader.com.au/p/teach-your-child-to-read-in-100-easy-lessons)).

**Studies of parents using it.** FACT: McConnell and Kubina (2016) had parents of three at-risk
kindergartners teach the first 30 lessons at 15 minutes a night, five nights a week. All three
children's words-read-correctly rose with the lessons, stayed up 30 days after, and parents
completed lesson steps accurately 88 percent of the time; the common parent error was skipping
word repetitions ([S27](https://centralreach.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/04/2016-McConnell-and-Kubina.pdf)). FACT: that paper cites Ebey et al. (1999): seven preschool parent-child
pairs, a gain of 0.9 grade equivalent and 17 standard-score points, lessons averaging 23
minutes ([S27](https://centralreach.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/04/2016-McConnell-and-Kubina.pdf)). **Unverified** (2026-10-17): I read only McConnell's description of Ebey et al.;
the original was not retrievable. Assessment: this is thin evidence (10 children across two
small studies, no control group), but it is what one would expect given the school-based DI
evidence, and the studies directly answer "can an ordinary parent run the script": yes.

**What parents report.** FACT: linguist John McWhorter taught his daughter with it and called
it simple and effective ([S19](https://fordhaminstitute.org/national/commentary/addressing-learning-loss-one-easy-lesson)). FACT: a Funnix reviewer found the lessons ran long for a wiggly
kindergartner and split each in two ([S34](https://thecurriculumchoice.com/funnix-reading-program-review)). Speculation: the likely friction points are
lesson length and repetitive drill; splitting lessons, which the authors explicitly allow
([S26](https://www.startreading.com/faq)), addresses the first. I did not survey parent reviews systematically.

### Funnix (same authors, narrated on screen)

FACT: Funnix is a web subscription for kindergarten to second grade, 220 reading lessons
(Funnix Beginning Reading plus Funnix 2, ending at beginning third-grade level), 25 to 30
minutes a day ([S31](https://www.funnix.com/)). FACT: the family plan is \$24.99 a month, cancel any time, two devices,
three users, all reading lessons plus Funnix Math and printable worksheets ([S32](https://www.funnix.com/pricing)). FACT: it runs
in a browser with nothing to install, has a free guest trial, and expects the parent to sit with
the child for the whole lesson ([S33](https://www.funnix.com/faq)). The FAQ does not address use outside the US ([S33](https://www.funnix.com/faq)).
FACT: a 2012 version of Funnix Beginning Math had 100 narrated lessons of about 35 minutes on
counting and early operations ([S35](https://hip2save.com/2012/12/12/free-funnix-beginning-math-download-100-narrated-lessons-teach-children-counting/)). FACT: a 2004 master's thesis on Funnix Math with
pre-kindergarteners reported results that "contradict previous research" on DI, with no
numbers on the abstract page ([S36](https://digitalcommons.longwood.edu/etd/95)). Assessment: Funnix lessons run longer than the 15 to 20
minute budget; plan to split them.

### Reading Mastery, Connecting Math Concepts, Spelling Mastery (school DI)

FACT: McGraw Hill sells Reading Mastery Signature Edition to schools, for example \$524.04 for
a 3-year package for one teacher and 10 students, with purchase orders and quotes; no

## 4. The bilingual angle: English decoding alongside Japanese school

The short answer is that the evidence says learning to read English and Japanese at the same
time does not hurt, but Japanese literacy will not teach English decoding for her.

**Sound awareness carries over; decoding mostly does not.** FACT: a meta-analysis of
correlational studies found a moderate to large link between a child's first- and
second-language phonological awareness and decoding, but a small link for oral language; how
much decoding carries over depends on the writing system ([S43](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9817.2010.01477.x), abstract). FACT: in Bialystok's
studies, English-Chinese bilingual 6-year-olds' reading progress in the two languages was
uncorrelated, while children reading two alphabetic languages progressed together; the authors
report no disadvantage, only specialization ([S44](https://dash.harvard.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/7312037d-0265-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b/content)). FACT: once spoken vocabulary was matched,
bilingual and monolingual children did not differ in early reading, and English letter-sound
skills neither helped nor harmed reading Chinese characters ([S45](https://www.bilingualism-matters.ppls.ed.ac.uk/?p=11061)).

**Japanese specifics.** FACT: Japanese children who learn only hiragana have more
phoneme-level awareness than earlier research assumed ([S46](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ928516), abstract). FACT: phonological
awareness at both the mora (kana-beat) level and the phoneme level matters for reading kana;
Japanese dyslexic children were weak at both ([S47](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17720344/), abstract). FACT: a case study described a
16-year-old raised in Japan, English at home, attending Japanese school, who read kanji and kana
at university level but read and spelled English very poorly; the authors argue that a
consistent script with large sound units (kana) hides a phoneme-level weakness that English
exposes ([S48](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0010-0277(99)00016-5), abstract). FACT: in 421 children aged 6 to 10, children exposed early to two
languages outperformed monolinguals on phonological awareness and word reading ([S50](https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.12745), abstract).

**Is 6 late?** FACT: phonics effects were larger when begun in kindergarten or first grade than
later ([S20](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543071003393)). **Unverified** (2026-10-17): Suggate, Schaughency and Reese (2013) reportedly found
New Zealand children taught to read at 7 caught up with those taught at 5 by about age 11; I
read only the repository record ([epub.uni-regensburg.de/62808](https://epub.uni-regensburg.de/62808/)),
not the paper. Assessment: 6 is the normal age for starting English decoding (US first grade,
England's Year 1), not late. Because her English is spoken, not learned, she is starting with
the oral vocabulary that makes decoding meaningful.

**Risk of confusion.** Assessment: the evidence above points to separation, not confusion: the
two scripts are learned as different systems. The real risk is the one in [S48](#sources): success in
Japanese reading can mask an English-specific difficulty, so track English progress on its own
terms. 100 Easy Lessons teaches sounds, not letter names ([S26](https://www.startreading.com/faq)), which avoids clashing with
romaji letter names she may meet at school.

## 5. The three-year-old

At three, the evidence supports talk, books, sound play and letter-sound games, not a reading
program.

FACT: the National Early Literacy Panel found the strong, consistent predictors of later
reading, from birth to 5, were knowing letter names and sounds, phonological awareness,
rapid naming, writing letters or one's own name, and short-term memory for spoken language.
Its suggested activities are letter games, singing rhymes that stress sounds, teaching the
sound as well as the name, and moving from combining word parts to breaking words into sounds
([S24](https://www.nichd.nih.gov/publications/pubs/Documents/NELPEarlyBeginnings09.pdf)). FACT: interactive ("dialogic") reading, where the parent asks questions and the child
does more of the talking, raised expressive vocabulary by d = 0.59 over ordinary reading, and
the effect was larger for 2- to 3-year-olds than for 4- to 5-year-olds ([S52](https://doi.org/10.1080/10409280701838603), abstract). FACT:
interactive book reading also explained about 7 percent of the variation in kindergarten
alphabet knowledge ([S53](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654309332561), abstract). FACT: phonemic awareness training helped preschoolers too
([S21](https://doi.org/10.1598/rrq.36.3.2)). FACT: the 100 Easy Lessons authors say "about four," with some precocious
3-year-olds ([S26](https://www.startreading.com/faq)), although their home page says ages 3 to 6 ([S25](https://www.startreading.com/)).

Assessment: let her sit in on her sister's lesson if she wants, play "what sound does /m/ start
with," and read with her daily in the interactive style. Start 100 Easy Lessons when she shows
the FAQ's readiness signs ([S26](https://www.startreading.com/faq)), probably at 4 or 5. Pressing a reluctant 3-year-old buys
little and risks the pleasant association that makes the later lessons easy.

## 6. Math and science fundamentals

**Math.** FACT: about one hour in total of playing a linear number board game (a race game with
numbered squares) improved preschoolers' counting, numeral recognition, comparison and number
line estimates, and the gains held nine weeks later ([S54](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8624.2007.01131.x), abstract). FACT: Funnix Math is
included in the \$24.99 Funnix subscription ([S32](https://www.funnix.com/pricing)). Assessment: for the 6-year-old, Funnix Math
is the DI math option that fits a home budget; for both girls, numbered board games and counting
in daily life are cheap and well supported. Connecting Math Concepts is a classroom kit ([S38](https://mheducation.co.uk/schools/elementary/direct-instruction/connecting-math-concepts),
[S39](https://info.mheducation.com/rs/128-SJW-347/images/2025-EURO-DI-Catalogue.pdf)).

**Science.** Assessment: I found no DI science program for ages 3 to 6; the DI catalogue's science
material (Your World of Facts and older video programs) is for older grades, per secondary
listings. **Unverified** (2026-10-17): I did not open a primary catalogue page for those
titles. FACT: for 3rd and 4th graders, more children learned how to design a fair experiment
from direct instruction than from discovery, and they transferred it equally well ([S55](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.0956-7976.2004.00737.x),
abstract). FACT: in 7,757 US children, general knowledge at kindergarten entry predicted
first-grade knowledge, which predicted science scores from grade 3 to grade 8 ([S56](https://doi.org/10.3102/0013189x16633182), abstract).
FACT: at a museum exhibit, parents prompted to explain produced more talk about how gears work,
and parents' questions predicted children's own testing ([S57](https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.13232), abstract). FACT: spacing science
lessons out helped early-elementary children remember and generalize better than massing them
([S58](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.2997), abstract).

Assessment: at 3 and 6, science fits naturally into reading nonfiction aloud and talking about
how things work, with explicit explanation when a specific procedure is the goal (how to make a
fair test, why the moon changes shape). General knowledge built now is what later science and
reading comprehension draw on ([S56](https://doi.org/10.3102/0013189x16633182)).

## 7. The daily routine

**Consistency beats length.** FACT: the 100 Easy Lessons FAQ says to stop when the child tires at
about 20 minutes and resume the next day, allows splitting a lesson into two sittings, and says a
consistent schedule helps ([S26](https://www.startreading.com/faq)). FACT: phonemic awareness instruction had larger effects at 5 to 18 total
hours ([S21](https://doi.org/10.1598/rrq.36.3.2)) and spaced lessons beat massed ones ([S58](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.2997)). FACT: the parent study used 15 minutes a
night, five nights a week ([S27](https://centralreach.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/04/2016-McConnell-and-Kubina.pdf)). FACT: Rosenshine's 80 percent success level, review at the
start of each lesson, and frequent checks of every response are the explicit-instruction
pattern ([S11](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/periodicals/Rosenshine.pdf)).

**Keeping it pleasant.** FACT: the FAQ says to practice the lesson beforehand, stay positive,
praise effort and correct first-try answers, correct immediately and kindly, and use stickers if
they help ([S26](https://www.startreading.com/faq)). FACT: Engelmann's preschool emphasized praise and celebrating effort ([S4](https://www.apmreports.org/episode/2026/10/15/sold-a-story-s2-e2-the-remedy-is-always-teaching)).
Assessment: end every session on a success, even if that means ending early on a word she
knows.

**A 15 to 20 minute evening session** (Assessment):

| Minutes | 6-year-old | 3-year-old |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 15 | 100 Easy Lessons, today's lesson or half of it, script read as written | Nearby with crayons, or joins the sound games at the start |
| 15 to 20 | She reads the lesson's story; praise; sticker on the chart | Two minutes of blending ("what word is m-a-t?") or first-sound games, then shared picture book |
| Bedtime, separate | Read-aloud of a nonfiction or story book, ask "why" and "what if" questions | Interactive reading of the same or another book |

Five weekdays, with weekends off or for a board game. At five lessons a week, 100 lessons takes
about 20 weeks; with splits, plan on six to eight months. Assessment.

## What I would do for a 6-year-old and a 3-year-old

Assessment, all of it.

1. Buy the paperback *Teach Your Child to Read in 100 Easy Lessons* (Amazon Japan, about ¥3,700),
   watch Haddox's free training videos first, print her sound cards and progress chart ([S25](https://www.startreading.com/)).
2. Six-year-old: one lesson or half-lesson, five evenings a week, 15 to 20 minutes, ending on a
   success. Read the script word for word; the script is the method. Do not skip the word
   repetitions, the one error the parent study found ([S27](https://centralreach.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/04/2016-McConnell-and-Kubina.pdf)).
3. Three-year-old: daily interactive reading and sound and letter games; no formal program.
   Reassess at 4 against the readiness signs ([S26](https://www.startreading.com/faq)).
4. Math: a numbered board game twice a week for both; add Funnix Math for the 6-year-old if you
   want a structured sequence and can tolerate screen time (it comes with the \$24.99 plan).
5. Science: nonfiction read-alouds and "how does it work" conversations.
6. After Lesson 100: UFLI's free decodable passages and ordinary early readers; then consider
   Funnix 2 or a spelling sequence.
7. Watch for an English-only struggle that school success in Japanese would hide ([S48](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0010-0277(99)00016-5)). If she
   stalls for weeks on blending while doing fine in Japanese, get an English reading assessment.

Pros and cons of the book versus Funnix (Assessment):

- Book: cheap, no screen, 20 minutes, parent fully in control; but the parent must rehearse the
  script, and the drill can feel repetitive.
- Funnix: the narration does the teaching, includes math, works anywhere online; but \$25 a
  month, 25 to 30 minute lessons, screen time, and the parent still has to sit with her.
- My read: start with the book (about 75 percent likely to carry her to Lesson 100 if done
  consistently); switch to Funnix if the parent finds the script itself is the barrier.

## Open questions

- Does the family want the 3-year-old to start formal lessons early, or is the goal simply a
  head start? That changes whether "wait until 4" is the right default.
- How much English does the 6-year-old meet at school (Japanese public schools add English in
  later grades, which I did not verify), and will romaji or letter names at school conflict with
  the sound-first approach?
- Is there an ebook or fixed-layout digital edition of 100 Easy Lessons? Not verified.
- Does Funnix work reliably from Japan and on the family's devices? The free guest trial
  answers this in ten minutes ([S33](https://www.funnix.com/faq)).
- The High/Scope finding rests on one small study written up by the rival program; the primary
  paper was not read ([S18](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0885-2006(97)90009-0)).
- Suggate's "later readers catch up" finding was not read in full ([S51](https://epub.uni-regensburg.de/62808/)).
