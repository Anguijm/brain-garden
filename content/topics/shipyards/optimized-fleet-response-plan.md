# Optimized Fleet Response Plan (OFRP): the cycle a shipyard availability sits inside

This note explains the Optimized Fleet Response Plan, the Navy's schedule for taking a ship from a shipyard availability through training and deployment and back to the next availability, for a planner who receives those hulls. It answers four questions: how long the cycle is for a carrier and for a surface combatant, where the availability sits and how long that slot is planned to be, what the plan assumes about the yard finishing on time, and what happens to the next availability when a deployment runs long. The last is the mechanism behind what the Waterfront Brief has called "interval compression".

## Which document governs, and what revision was read

The instruction read for this note is OPNAVINST 3000.15A, signed by the Chief of Naval Operations on 10 November 2014; no later OPNAV revision was found. FACT: the instruction is dated "10 Nov 2014" and cancels "OPNAVINST 3000.15", the earlier Fleet Response Plan ([OPNAVINST 3000.15A](https://www.secnav.navy.mil/doni/Directives/03000%20Naval%20Operations%20and%20Readiness/03-00%20General%20Operations%20and%20Readiness%20Support/3000.15A.pdf), read 2026-09-05). The working detail lives one level down in a fleet instruction, cited by GAO as COMUSFLTFORCOM/COMPACFLTINST 3000.15A of December 8, 2014, which has since been revised. FACT: the three fleet commanders "released a 12-star-signed revision to the plan on Oct. 20" 2020, which describes OFRP as "a notional 36-month readiness-generation plan" that "is not a firm schedule but rather a construct" ([USNI News, 2020-10-28](https://news.usni.org/2020/10/28/navy-issues-revision-to-ofrp-deployment-scheme), read 2026-09-05). Unverified: that revision is COMUSFLTFORCOM/COMPACFLTINST 3000.15B. The surface force training manual dated 1 November 2018 already lists a "3000.15B" as a reference, two years before the revision USNI reported, and no public copy of either was found on 2026-09-05.

## How long the cycle is

The cycle is 36 months for a carrier strike group and for the cruisers and destroyers that deploy with it, measured from the start of one maintenance phase to the start of the next. FACT: "An OFRP cycle is the time from the beginning of a maintenance phase to the beginning of the next maintenance phase. The length of the cycle will be established for each force element" ([OPNAVINST 3000.15A](https://www.secnav.navy.mil/doni/Directives/03000%20Naval%20Operations%20and%20Readiness/03-00%20General%20Operations%20and%20Readiness%20Support/3000.15A.pdf), read 2026-09-05). FACT: the four phases "are planned to comprise a total of 18-36 months, depending on the force element. Carrier Strike Groups, the Navy's major force element, use a 36-month OFRP cycle" ([GAO-16-466R](https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-16-466r.pdf), read 2026-09-05). FACT: the earlier plan assumed "deployments of 6 months in length and approximately 27 months between deployments"; OFRP "is now based on notional 7-month deployments and approximately 36 months between deployments" ([AFTT Final EIS, chapter 1](https://media.defense.gov/2020/May/13/2002299471/-1/-1/1/1.0%20AFTT%20FEIS%20PURPOSE%20AND%20NEED.PDF), read 2026-09-05). FACT: the Chief of Naval Operations said in February 2026 that the cycle "often ends up clocking in at 42 to 44 months due to maintenance and modernization availabilities that run too long" ([USNI News, 2026-02-12](https://news.usni.org/2026/02/12/improving-maintenance-timelines-key-to-on-time-strike-group-deployments-cno-says), read 2026-09-05).

## Where the availability sits and how long that slot is planned to be

The availability is the maintenance phase, the first phase of the cycle, and for a surface combatant it is planned at about seven months including the shakedown after it. FACT: "the maintenance phase for surface combatants includes approximately 24 weeks for the CNO availability plus 4 weeks for a post availability shakedown; however, the maintenance phase can take up to 16 months for an aircraft carrier undergoing a docking planned incremental availability" ([OPNAVINST 3000.15A](https://www.secnav.navy.mil/doni/Directives/03000%20Naval%20Operations%20and%20Readiness/03-00%20General%20Operations%20and%20Readiness%20Support/3000.15A.pdf), read 2026-09-05). A CNO availability is a depot-level maintenance period scheduled by the Chief of Naval Operations' staff; it is the availability a private yard bids on. FACT: "Maintenance Phase duration varies based on planned CNO Availability length" ([COMNAVSURFPAC/COMNAVSURFLANTINST 3502.7](https://allhands.navy.mil/Portals/1/Optask/references/COMNAVSURFPAC_COMNAVSURFLANTINST-3502_7-1Nov18-Surface_Force_Training_Readiness_Manual.pdf?ver=W4gxIOXq_F2u55iCVjiVzQ%3D%3D), read 2026-09-05). Carrier availabilities come in three sizes: a planned incremental availability (PIA), a docking PIA (DPIA) and a refueling and complex overhaul (RCOH). FACT: "a PIA is a 6-month availability", "A DPIA is a 16-month dry-docking availability", and "An RCOH is a 44-month availability" ([GAO-16-466R](https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-16-466r.pdf), read 2026-09-05).

The table is the planned split. The carrier and surface combatant columns come from GAO's Figure 3 on page 12 of the report, which is an image, so the numbers are not in the text extraction a grounding check reads; this desk rendered the page and read the chart directly on 2026-09-05, and the figures below are what it shows. The same chart gives the earlier Fleet Response Plan for comparison: a 32-month carrier cycle with a 6-month maintenance phase, and a 27-month surface combatant cycle with a 4.5-month maintenance phase. FACT: GAO's note on that figure says it "does not include significant maintenance events such as docking planned incremental availabilities and refueling complex overhauls" ([GAO-16-466R](https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-16-466r.pdf), read 2026-09-05), so this is the PIA cycle.

| Phase | Carrier strike group, planned | Surface combatant, planned | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Maintenance (CNO availability plus shakedown) | 7 months (19% of cycle) | 6.5 months (18%); about 24 weeks plus 4 weeks shakedown; notional months 1 to 7 | GAO-16-466R Figure 3; OPNAVINST 3000.15A para 5d(1); SFTRM Figure 4-1 |
| Basic, advanced and integrated training | 7.5 months (21%); "eight months of basic and integrated training" per Fleet Forces | 9.5 months (26%); notional months 8 to 17 | GAO-16-466R Figure 3; USNI News 2016-01-19; SFTRM Figure 4-1 |
| Deployment | 7 months (19%) | 7 months (19%); notional months about 19 to 25 | GAO-16-466R Figure 3 and page 17; SFTRM Figure 4-1 |
| Sustainment after deployment | 14.5 months (40%); "roughly 14-month" per Defense News | 13 months (36%); notional to month 36 | GAO-16-466R Figure 3; Defense News 2020-02-10; SFTRM Figure 4-1 |
| Whole cycle | 36 months | 36 months | OPNAVINST 3000.15A; GAO-16-466R |

Under the instruction the deployment sits inside the sustainment phase rather than being a phase of its own. FACT: "The sustainment phase begins upon completion of the integrated or advanced phase, continues throughout the post-deployment period, and ends with the commencement of the next maintenance phase. Deployments may be conducted within the sustainment phase" ([OPNAVINST 3000.15A](https://www.secnav.navy.mil/doni/Directives/03000%20Naval%20Operations%20and%20Readiness/03-00%20General%20Operations%20and%20Readiness%20Support/3000.15A.pdf), read 2026-09-05). The post-deployment window is where the next availability gets planned. FACT: ships in sustainment "will use this time to conduct both internal (ship's force) and external material assessments to support Availability Work Package (AWP) development for the next Maintenance Phase" ([COMNAVSURFPAC/COMNAVSURFLANTINST 3502.7](https://allhands.navy.mil/Portals/1/Optask/references/COMNAVSURFPAC_COMNAVSURFLANTINST-3502_7-1Nov18-Surface_Force_Training_Readiness_Manual.pdf?ver=W4gxIOXq_F2u55iCVjiVzQ%3D%3D), read 2026-09-05). The Availability Work Package is the list of jobs the yard will be asked to price and execute.

## What the plan assumes about maintenance finishing on time

The plan assumes the yard delivers on schedule, and says so in as many words. FACT: GAO listed the Navy's key assumptions as funding at fiscal 2016 levels, the shipbuilding plan's 11 carriers, presence demands not above 2016 levels, and "Shipyards will complete maintenance on time", adding that "If any of these assumptions are not realized, then the Navy is at risk of not achieving its OFRP employability" goals ([GAO-16-466R](https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-16-466r.pdf), read 2026-09-05). Employability is the Navy's word for the days a ship is available to an operational commander. FACT: Navy maintenance officials told GAO "The maintenance timeframes established under the OFRP are adequate and based on technical and engineering requirements" ([GAO-16-466R](https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-16-466r.pdf), read 2026-09-05).

Assessment: the plan carries no maintenance slack of its own. The 24-week figure is the engineering duration with no contingency on it, and the only elastic time in the cycle is downstream of the yard, in training and sustainment, which is why the sources below treat an overrun as a training and deployment problem rather than a shipyard one.

## What happens to the next availability when a deployment is extended

An extended deployment adds work to the next package and takes away the time in which the package is planned, and when that availability then overruns, the loss is passed to the next ship in the queue.

The dates are fixed first. FACT: "The deployments are scheduled years in advance. It is part of our force offering adjudicated by the Secretary of Defense", and "The shock absorber for that becomes the training cycle", Rear Adm. Jeffrey Harley told the House Armed Services readiness subcommittee ([hearing transcript, 2015-09-10](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CHRG-114hhrg96239/html/CHRG-114hhrg96239.htm), read 2026-09-05).

Extension adds work. FACT: "we have extended deployment lengths, which increased the wear on our ships, and resulted in additional maintenance and repairs that lengthened planned maintenance availabilities" ([hearing transcript, 2015-09-10](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CHRG-114hhrg96239/html/CHRG-114hhrg96239.htm), read 2026-09-05). FACT: the acting Secretary of the Navy told House appropriators in May 2026, "For every 30 days that the ship is extended on deployment, it adds 6 percent of maintenance, so five months extra would add 30 percent for maintenance" ([Breaking Defense, 2026-05-13](https://breakingdefense.com/2026/05/navy-to-run-into-financial-constraints-this-summer-due-to-current-operations-cno-says/), read 2026-09-05). The basis of the 6 percent was not given.

Overrun then rolls to the next ship. FACT: Capt. Thom Burke, the fleet readiness director, described the pattern the plan was meant to end as "having one ship go long, causing another ship's availability to be delayed or canceled or significant parts of it deferred" ([hearing transcript, 2015-09-10](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CHRG-114hhrg96239/html/CHRG-114hhrg96239.htm), read 2026-09-05). FACT: the Navy's options for an overrun are to "Condense training period (most common, according to Navy officials)", "Reduce sustainment period", "Deploy another ship" or "Delay the deployment (rarely if ever used, according to Navy officials)", and when Eisenhower's availability grew from 14 to more than 23 months, Truman's resulting back-to-back deployments "prevented the Truman from entering her OFRP maintenance phase at Norfolk Naval Shipyard as scheduled in fiscal year 2015" ([GAO-16-466R](https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-16-466r.pdf), read 2026-09-05). FACT: "A ship that requires a longer maintenance period may need to cut into basic training and compress that schedule, while still being ready to start integrated training on the I-date" ([USNI News, 2016-01-19](https://news.usni.org/2016/01/19/u-s-fleet-forces-new-deployment-plan-designed-to-create-sustainable-naval-force), read 2026-09-05). The I-date is the fixed day integrated training starts, and the whole strike group is held to it.

Extension is also how the Navy surges, because the surge pool was never paid for. FACT: the then fleet maintenance director said of the post-deployment period, "I call it 'sustainment opportunity' because right now it's not funded" ([Defense News, 2020-02-10](https://www.defensenews.com/naval/2020/02/07/the-us-navys-vaunted-deployment-plan-is-showing-cracks-everywhere/), read 2026-09-05). FACT: Ford arrived at Norfolk Naval Shipyard in July 2026 for a PIA "following a nearly 11-month deployment", after Eisenhower's and Bush's PIAs each ran about a year against a typical "six to eight months" ([USNI News, 2026-07-14](https://news.usni.org/2026/07/14/carrier-uss-gerald-r-ford-starts-first-major-post-deployment-maintenance-period), read 2026-09-05).

Assessment: "interval compression" is a fair name for what these facts add up to, with one correction. The sourced mechanism is not that maintenance intervals fall due at once; it is that wear from the extra months enlarges the work package, the sustainment window in which that package is assessed and written shrinks by the same months, and whatever the last availability deferred to protect its end date is still in the queue. Expect a package that grows late, locks with less assessment behind it, and finds more growth work after start. FACT: in January 2023 the Fleet Forces commander, Adm. Daryl Caudle, said he would rather see a "core" strike group of the carrier, one cruiser and an oiler go through OFRP together "and the destroyers independently cycle through maintenance, training and deployments", and had tasked a team to examine it ([Defense News, 2023-01-18](https://www.defensenews.com/naval/2023/01/18/fleet-forces-team-tackles-decoupling-destroyers-from-carriers/), read 2026-09-05). Speculation: if that decoupling is carried through, it loosens the I-date coupling for surface combatants but not the wear-driven growth.

## What the Navy and GAO have said about results

On-time completion has stayed well below the goal from the first year of the plan to the latest reporting. FACT: the Navy's goal was 60 percent of availabilities on cost and on schedule, and "13 of the 59 (22%) Fiscal Year 2014 CNO availabilities completed on time or early. In Fiscal Year 2015, 19 of 52 (36%)" ([hearing transcript, 2015-09-10](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CHRG-114hhrg96239/html/CHRG-114hhrg96239.htm), read 2026-09-05). FACT: in January 2024 GAO "found the Navy continued to face maintenance delays with only 20 percent (12 of 61) of carrier strike group maintenance phases on time in fiscal year 2021 and 39 percent (11 of 28) maintenance phases on time in fiscal year 2022" ([GAO-25-108104](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-25-108104), read 2026-09-05). FACT: "In fiscal year 2022, private repair companies only completed 36 percent of nonnuclear-powered surface ship repair periods on time" ([GAO-26-108888](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-108888), read 2026-09-05).

## How much to trust this

The cycle definition, phase order, the 24-week plus 4-week surface combatant maintenance phase, the 16-month carrier DPIA ceiling and the sustainment definition are FACT from the primary instruction, read on the DONI copy. The phase durations in the table are FACT from GAO-16-466R, read from the rendered figure by this desk on 2026-09-05 (a text-based grounding check will not find them, because the figure is an image); the month ranges from Figure 4-1 of the surface force manual are approximate readings of a bar chart and are Assessment at the month level. The "shock absorber" and "one ship go long" mechanism is FACT from a 2015 hearing; nothing found since contradicts it, and the 2026 Ford example fits it. The 6 percent per 30 days figure is one official's testimony as reported by one outlet, with no method behind it. Which fleet revision is current is Unverified: the 2020 revision was reported but not read. GAO's January 2024 OFRP report (GAO-24-106363C) is controlled and could not be read; its figures are quoted from GAO's 2025 summary. The claim that maintenance intervals "fall due simultaneously" after an extension, which the newsletter has used, has no source here and should not be stated as FACT. All sources were read on 2026-09-05.
