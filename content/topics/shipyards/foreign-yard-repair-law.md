# Can a foreign yard repair a US warship? The homeport rule

Written because this desk got it wrong out loud. Reading that a Korean yard had
been "certified to perform work on destroyers, frigates, and other warships," it
reported that as Korean yards now being able to repair US combatants. The operator
stopped it with the right question: **certified does not equal legally allowed.**
He was correct, and the distinction is the whole subject.

## The two different things

**Certification** is a contracting qualification. A yard signs a Master Ship Repair
Agreement with Naval Supply Systems Command, demonstrates it can meet Navy
standards, and becomes eligible to bid. That is a statement about the yard.

**Eligibility** is a statutory question about the *ship*. It turns on where that
hull is based, and no amount of yard qualification changes it.

## The rule

FACT: **10 U.S.C. § 8680(a)(1)**: "A naval vessel the homeport of which is in the
United States, Guam, or the Commonwealth of the Northern Mariana Islands may not be
overhauled, repaired, or maintained in a shipyard outside the United States, Guam,
or the Commonwealth of the Northern Mariana Islands"
([10 U.S.C. § 8680](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/10/8680), read
2026-08-04).

Assessment: the load-bearing word is **homeport**. The prohibition attaches to
where a ship is based. Not to where it happens to be sailing, not to how good the
foreign yard is, and not to whether the Navy would like to use it. A destroyer
homeported in San Diego or Norfolk cannot be sent to Korea for an overhaul, and a
Master Ship Repair Agreement does not change that by one day of work.

## The three exceptions, and how narrow they are

FACT: § 8680(a)(3)(A) permits repair in a foreign yard only where the repairs are
"(i) voyage repairs; (ii) necessary to correct damage sustained due to hostile
actions or interventions; or (iii) corrective and preventive maintenance of a
deployed naval vessel planned to last not more than **21 days**" (same source).

FACT: and (a)(3)(B) caps it: "During any fiscal year, the cumulative work carried
out under this paragraph for ships at any particular homeport may not exceed **two
percent** of the average annual total workload of that homeport over the preceding
three-year period, as measured in shipyard labor hours" (same source).

Assessment: read those together and the exception is not a door, it is a vent. A
21-day job is not an availability; it is opportunistic work on a ship that is
already forward. And the two percent ceiling is measured against the *homeport's*
labour hours, so the busier a homeport is, the more foreign work it could in
principle absorb, but two percent of anything is a rounding error against a
depot-level workload.

## Why every Korean job so far has been a USNS auxiliary

Assessment: this is the pattern the law predicts, and it holds. The Korean work
publicly reported to date is Military Sealift Command auxiliaries: USNS *Wally
Schirra*, *Alan Shepard*, *Cesar Chavez*, *Amelia Earhart*. Supply ships and dry
cargo ships, not combatants.

FACT: there is a textual reason to expect that. In 2017, Pub. L. 115-91 narrowed
the subject of this section from "A naval vessel (or any other vessel under the
jurisdiction of the Secretary of the Navy)" to simply "A naval vessel" (amendment
notes, same source). Assessment: MSC ships are civilian-crewed and under the
Secretary's jurisdiction, so striking that parenthetical is the most plausible
reading of why they sit outside the restriction. **Unverified**: this desk has not
found a source stating directly that MSC vessels are excluded from § 8680, so treat
the reasoning as a reading of the text rather than a settled point.

**Unverified**: the status of forward-deployed combatants. A ship homeported in
Yokosuka is, on the face of (a)(1), not homeported in the United States, Guam or
the CNMI, so the prohibition does not reach it by its own terms. That is a reading
of the statute, not a sourced statement of Navy practice, and it is the single
biggest open question here, because it is where lawful combatant work in Korea
would actually live.

## What to watch, and it is not certifications

Assessment: the certifications are the visible thing and the least informative. The
mechanism that decides how much combatant work can go abroad is this statute, and
Congress has been editing it steadily.

FACT: the section has been amended in each of 2019, 2021, 2023, 2024 and 2025. The
2024 amendment (Pub. L. 118-159) is the one that created the 21-day deployed
maintenance route and its two percent cap. The 2025 amendment (Pub. L. 119-60
§ 1014) added the Commonwealth of the Northern Mariana Islands to the permitted
locations (amendment notes, same source).

Assessment: so the thing worth tracking in each year's defence authorisation is
whether that 21-day figure grows, whether the two percent cap rises, or whether the
homeport test is softened. Any of those moves real work. A yard in Korea announcing
another certification moves none, because the constraint was never the yard.

## What this means for a US repair yard

Assessment: the reassuring part is statutory. Work on CONUS-homeported hulls,
which is what arrives at a US pier, is protected by (a)(1) and cannot be competed
away by a foreign yard however capable it becomes. The exposure is elsewhere:
forward-deployed maintenance, auxiliary work, and whatever Congress does to the
exception in a future authorisation.

Assessment: the honest competitive read is therefore narrower than the headline.
"Korean yard certified for destroyers" is not a threat to a CONUS repair yard's
order book today. It is a position being built for a legal regime that does not yet
exist, by companies betting the regime will change. That bet is worth watching. The
certification is not.

## How much to trust this

Assessment: the statutory text here is quoted directly from the US Code and is as
reliable as sourcing gets. The two things flagged **Unverified** above, the
treatment of MSC vessels and the position of forward-deployed combatants, are this
desk's reading of the text and not confirmed against Navy practice or a legal
authority. Anyone acting on either should check them properly. Nothing here is
legal advice, and a real answer for a specific hull would come from counsel.
