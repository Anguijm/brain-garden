# Four threads worth pulling, August 2026

Second-stage research on four stories the weekly gather surfaced. None of them
necessarily belongs in an issue; the point was to find out whether anything sits a layer
below the headline. In three of the four, something does, and in each case it is more
useful than the story that led to it.

## 1. There is a named route into NAVSEA for shipyard process improvements

This is the one worth acting on, and it is not being tracked anywhere in this vault.

FACT: the NAVSEA **Discovery Team** exists to find technologies and process improvements
for the Navy's four public shipyards. It sits inside **Engineering Initiatives for Naval
Shipyards (EI-NSY)** and is aligned with the Maritime Industrial Base programme. The
effort traces to 2021, when Naval Sustainment System-Shipyards set up a structure of
"pillars" aimed at improving naval maintenance, and an **Engineering Intervention Board**
was established specifically to find ways to reduce the labour a shipyard needs to do its
work ([NAVSEA, *NAVSEA Discovery Team Advances Shipyard Maintenance
Innovation*](https://www.navsea.navy.mil/Media/News/Article-View/Article/4502829/navsea-discovery-team-advances-shipyard-maintenance-innovation/),
28 May 2026).

FACT: in its first year the team identified **90,000 man-days of potential savings** across
the four public shipyards over a two-year period, beating its own initial goal, and it has
taken in **more than 250 ideas** to date (same source).

FACT: it is explicitly an open door. The Discovery Team "serves as an entry point for
employees across the enterprise to submit improvement ideas," with cross-organisational
stakeholders reviewing what comes in (same source).

FACT: the people running it are named, and one of them is local to this reader's region.
Mike Tracy, quoted setting the goal as completing "maintenance faster, safer and more
efficiently," is **chief planner and estimator for Puget Sound Naval Shipyard**. Dr. Paul
Lara, chief engineer at NSWC Carderock, is pushing awareness of it. NUWC Keyport, NUWC
Newport and Carderock all contribute through the Navy's Ship Modernization Office (same
source).

Assessment: this is the thread. A named board whose stated job is cutting shipyard labour
content, an open submission channel, a published savings figure to argue against, and a
Puget Sound planner and estimator as one of its public faces. Anyone with a method that
takes hours out of a job has somewhere specific to send it, and Keyport is close enough to
matter.

FACT: the organisational ground is moving under it. After the Naval Sustainment
System-Shipyards effort ended, NAVSEA's Naval Systems Engineering and Logistics
Directorate (SEA 05) chose to continue the Engineering Pillar, and the article notes this
is happening while NAVSEA defines its Portfolio Acquisition Executive structures (same
source). Assessment: that is the same reorganisation the brief covered in week 30, seen
from underneath. Worth watching whether the Discovery Team survives the portfolio
transition with its budget intact, because an improvement body is exactly the kind of
thing a reorganisation quietly loses.

Assessment: the team's own stated weakness is the opening. Tracy: "The greatest opportunity
and potential strength we see is increased collaboration with other improvement and
innovation organizations." They are asking.

## 2. The Navy's first organic cold spray centre, and the word doing the work

FACT: NSWC Crane opened the **MADMAN Repair Center** (Metal Additive Manufacturing) on
23 June 2026, using low-pressure and high-pressure cold spray to extend the life of naval
components. Cold spray is a solid-state process: compressed gas fires metal powder at
supersonic speed so it bonds on impact, restoring material without the heat of welding,
which means no heat-affected zone and no loss of the parent metal's strength
([NAVSEA, *NSWC Crane opens advanced repair
center*](https://www.navsea.navy.mil/Media/News/Article-View/Article/4547982/nswc-crane-opens-advanced-repair-center-forging-new-era-of-fleet-readiness/),
16 July 2026).

FACT: the commanding officer's framing is a shift "from 'replace' to 'repair'," and the
facility "is the first organic industrial base facility for **non-structural** cold spray
within the Navy," enabling repairs in-house "that were previously outsourced" (Capt. Rex
Boonyobhas, same source).

Assessment: **non-structural** is the whole sentence, and it is a limit on the *method*,
not on the work. To be unambiguous, because an earlier draft of this note was not: a repair
yard does structural work every day, by welding, plate renewal, inserts and doublers, and
nothing here changes that. What the qualifier limits is where **cold spray** is an approved
way to do it. Crane's organic capability covers components that do not carry primary load:
corroded housings, worn seal surfaces, restoring a dimension on a shaft or bore. Cold spray
as an approved repair method for load-bearing structure is the thing that has not been
authorised.

FACT: the gap is a live research question rather than a settled one. A review titled
*Critical Assessment 11: Structural repairs by cold spray* exists precisely to assess "the
status of the technology and the barriers to widespread commercial application" for
repairing defects and damage in metal structures
([Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1179/1743284714Y.0000000723)).
**Unverified** (2026-08-03): what those barriers are. The article is paywalled past its
abstract and this desk has not read the body, so nothing here characterises its findings.

Assessment: that is the thread to watch, and the reason it matters is substitution rather
than new scope. Structural repairs that today mean cutting out and renewing metal, with the
heat input, distortion and inspection burden that carries, are the candidates cold spray
would eventually compete for. Non-structural organic capability now exists and has a named
home; approval for load-bearing use is the boundary. When it moves, the question for a yard
is whether a job it currently welds becomes a job it sprays, which is a different tooling,
training and qualification bill. The first sign will be a qualification announcement rather
than a facility opening.

FACT: there is a named programme office. Jeff Campbell appears as **NAVSEA 05T1, Cold
Spray Project Manager**, alongside MADMAN director Troy Pierson and rapid prototype branch
manager Scott Gamble (NAVSEA photo caption, same release). Assessment: 05T1 is the address
for the question "what is authorised now, and what is next."

## 3. The additive qualification path is four things, and three are already done

The useful find here is not a new capability but the shape of the approval.

FACT: NAVSEA certified Huntington Ingalls to use a stainless steel alloy, **316/316L**, to
produce pipefittings and other components for aircraft carriers and submarines. John Ralls,
then deputy chief engineer for additive manufacturing at Newport News, broke the approval
into four elements: **the processes and procedures for manufacturing the part, the material,
the people operating the equipment, and the application or use case.** Each needs individual
Navy approval ([Breaking Defense, *HII gets Navy's green light to expand use of 3D printed
parts in shipyards*](https://breakingdefense.com/2023/04/hii-gets-navys-green-light-to-expand-use-of-3d-printed-parts-in-shipyards/),
5 April 2023).

FACT: Ralls' point was that the first three had been cleared: "The process and procedures
are approved. The material has been sufficiently demonstrated... and the operator that uses
those has demonstrated a level of proficiency" (same source).

Assessment: that is the reusable insight. Three of the four gates are about the shop, not
the part, so they are cleared once and then amortised across everything that follows. The
fourth, the use case, is per-application. A yard that has cleared process, material and
operator qualification is arguing about one thing per new part instead of four. That is the
difference between additive as a demonstration and additive as a capability, and it is a
sequencing argument any yard can copy.

FACT: the Navy has also centralised the paperwork. **N-RAM** is a Flank Speed SharePoint
site consolidating additive and advanced manufacturing specifications, standards, policy
letters and technical guidance, tagging each document current or superseded, with a tracker
that follows candidate parts from requirement through contracting. Lead engineer Ryan
Donnelly describes the aim as changing the question from "Who has this?" to "Have you
checked N-RAM?" ([NAVSEA, *Inside
N-RAM*](https://www.navsea.navy.mil/Media/News/Article-View/Article/4368368/inside-n-ram-navys-hub-for-additive-manufacturing-information/),
30 December 2025).

Assessment: it came out of a structured improvement workshop, and what it fixed is worth
naming plainly because it is the most ordinary failure there is. People could not find the
current version of a specification, so they emailed experts one at a time. The fix was one
place, with each document marked current or superseded. Access needs Flank Speed, so this is
a route for government personnel and cleared partners rather than an open reference.

## 4. Where the submarine money actually goes, and why it is not a hiring story

The operator's own read was that this work does not touch his resource pool, being
nuclear-certified and SubSafe-qualified. That is right, and following the money leads
somewhere more interesting than trades competition.

FACT: the 29 July 2026 award totals **\$76.6 billion**, split as **\$42.1 billion** for nine
Block VI Virginia-class boats, **\$29.5 billion** for Columbia-class Build II, and **\$5
billion for investments in nuclear shipbuilding programmes and shipbuilding
infrastructure** for both builders ([Defense News, *Navy awards \$76.6 billion for Virginia,
Columbia-class
submarines*](https://www.defensenews.com/industry/techwatch/2026/07/30/navy-awards-766-billion-for-virginia-columbia-class-submarines/),
30 July 2026).

FACT: the scale of the labour problem is stated plainly by the Navy. The submarine
manufacturing base "has shrunk to just one-third of its capacity from 30 years ago,"
production "must nearly double," and meeting that requires **an additional 3.5 to 4.5
million submarine module production and outfitting hours annually** ([NAVSEA, *Navy Partners
with Private Industry to Grow Submarine Industrial Base
Capacity*](https://www.navsea.navy.mil/Media/News/Article-View/Article/3913023/navy-partners-with-private-industry-to-grow-submarine-industrial-base-capacity/),
20 September 2024).

FACT: the Navy's response is not only to hire. It is co-investing to push **module
manufacturing out to private capacity**, explicitly incentivising "private investment in
the expansion of domestic shipyard capacity for submarine module manufacturing" (same
source).

FACT: the clearest example opened this year and it is not a shipyard. The advanced
manufacturing firm Hadrian opened a **2.2 million square foot highly automated plant** in
Cherokee, Alabama on 20 March 2026 to mass-produce Virginia and Columbia components, funded
by **\$900 million** of Navy money alongside **\$1.5 billion in private capital**, more than
\$2.4 billion total, creating up to 1,000 jobs. The Secretary of the Navy called it "the
first of three facilities designed to address the most critical bottlenecks in the maritime
industrial base" ([U.S. Navy, *Advanced Shipbuilding 'Factory of the Future' Opens in
Alabama*](https://www.navy.mil/Press-Office/Press-Releases/display-pressreleases/Article/4439992/advanced-shipbuilding-factory-of-the-future-opens-in-alabama/), 20 March
2026).

Assessment: this is the third-order thread and it cuts against the obvious reading. Faced
with a labour shortfall measured in millions of hours, the Navy is not simply bidding up
welders. It is buying **automated component production from a non-shipbuilder**, and
promising two more. The competitive question that raises for any repair yard is not who
takes the trades, it is whether component fabrication that yards do by hand today gets
bought from an automated plant tomorrow, at a price no hand shop matches.

Assessment: two more should be watched by name and location, because "the first of three"
is a commitment the Navy can be held to. Where the other two land, and what they make, says
more about the next decade of shipyard work than the headline number does.

## What this changes

Assessment: three practical items, in order of how soon they matter.

1. **The Discovery Team is a live channel and nobody here was tracking it.** A method that
   removes labour hours has a named destination, a review process, and a Puget Sound
   planner and estimator among its public faces.
2. **Watch NAVSEA 05T1 for the load-bearing cold spray boundary.** Not because structural
   repair is new to a yard, it is the daily work, but because cold spray becoming an approved
   method for it would substitute for jobs currently done by cutting and welding.
3. **Qualify the shop, not the part.** Process, material and operator clear once and carry
   forward; only the use case repeats. That ordering is the cheapest lesson in this memo.

## How much to trust this

Assessment: the load-bearing material is Navy and NAVSEA public affairs releases and one
trade report, all linked above and all read directly rather than from summaries. Those are
reliable for what an organisation says it is doing and unreliable for whether it worked:
the 90,000 man-days figure is a NAVSEA claim about its own initiative and no outside
assessment of it was found, and the Alabama plant's output is a projection, not a record.

Not established: whether the Discovery Team survives the portfolio reorganisation, what the
barriers to structural cold spray repair actually are, and whether the two remaining
component factories exist beyond an intention. Each is checkable later and none is checked
here.
