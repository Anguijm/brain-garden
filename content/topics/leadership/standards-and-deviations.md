# Standards and deviations: how settled work safely changes

The two companion notes cover the improvement lane, the discretionary work where you surface
friction and try bounded bets. This note is the other lane: governed or standard work, where a
policy already says how the job is done and the question is not "what should we try" but "what
is the one best-known way, and how do we change it without someone getting hurt." A naval
tag-out is one example, but so is a pharmacy filling a prescription, a refinery isolating a
vessel, or an airline running a checklist. The instinct you described, that a deviation has to
be documented and measured so it can be judged as either a new standard or a one-time exception,
is exactly right. It turns out to be the same discipline every high-consequence industry has
built independently, under different names.

## First, why have a standard at all

Assessment: without a settled method you cannot improve, because you have nothing to improve
from. Taiichi Ohno, the architect of the Toyota Production System, is widely quoted as saying
"without a standard, there can be no improvement" (a paraphrase that circulates in the field
rather than a verified line from his book, so read it as attribution, not gospel). The idea
holds regardless of who first said it: a standard is the baseline you measure change against, so
you can tell better from merely different, and can train to it, verify it, and compare. The
other half matters just as much, and is also attributed to Ohno: standards should be set by the
people who do the work, not forced from above. A method imposed from a desk is the version
people quietly route around; a method the floor wrote is one they will actually follow and
defend.

## The standard is never the whole truth

Assessment: no written procedure fully matches how the work is really done, and it never can.
FACT: safety researchers call this the gap between "work-as-imagined," the idealized version in
the procedure, and "work-as-done," how the job actually unfolds once it meets real conditions;
the written version always leaves out the adjustments skilled people make to keep the work
moving (Erik Hollnagel, "Safety-I and Safety-II"). The practical consequence is that a deviation
is not automatically a sin. Many are necessary adaptations that carry real frontline expertise,
and some are the shortcut that will eventually flood a space. You cannot tell which is which by
pretending deviation does not happen. The whole job is to surface it and judge it.

## The loop: document, measure, judge

This is the heart of it, and it is the part you already had. A deviation gets documented (what
was done differently, why, and who authorized it) and measured (did it help, hurt, or do
nothing, against a real number), and then judged: promote it to the new standard, or close it as
a one-time exception. What is worth knowing is that this exact loop is standard practice across
industries that have nothing to do with each other, which is the strongest sign it is right.

- **Process safety** runs it as Management of Change. FACT: the US process-safety rule requires
  that a change be documented with its technical basis, its impact on safety and health, any
  changes to procedures, the authorization, and the time period for the change (OSHA Process
  Safety Management, 29 CFR 1910.119). Assessment: standard practice under that rule, and in
  OSHA's guidance, is that a temporary change carries a time limit and is re-judged at the limit,
  extended with fresh approval, made permanent, or reversed to the original. That is your
  one-time-versus-new-standard question, with a clock on it.
- **Pharmaceutical quality** runs it as deviation, CAPA, and change control. FACT: a deviation
  is investigated, and if it was a genuine one-off fully handled by immediate action it is
  closed with documentation, while a systemic or recurring cause drives a permanent procedure
  change through change control, complete with retraining (GMP quality systems, ICH Q10).
- **Improvement science** runs it as Plan-Do-Study-Act. FACT: you plan a change, do it on a
  small scale, study what the data show, then act by adopting, adapting, or abandoning it; the
  loop traces to Walter Shewhart in the 1930s and W. Edwards Deming, who insisted on "study"
  rather than "check" so people actually look at the result (W. Edwards Deming Institute).

Different words, one skeleton: document the deviation, bound it, measure it, then adopt it for
everyone or put it back. Your tag-out example is this skeleton wearing Navy clothes.

## Two ways the loop fails

Assessment: the loop is not bureaucracy for its own sake. It exists because there are exactly
two ways settled work goes wrong, and both are about deviations that skip the loop.

The first is silent drift. FACT: the sociologist Diane Vaughan, studying the Challenger
disaster, named "the normalization of deviance," the process by which a deviation that does not
immediately cause harm stops being seen as a deviation at all and becomes the unexamined normal,
because the absence of a bad outcome is read as proof it is fine (Vaughan, "The Challenger
Launch Decision," 1996). The shortcut still happened; it just never got documented, measured, or
judged. This is why writing the deviation down is not paperwork, it is the one thing that keeps a
quiet shortcut from becoming the standard nobody actually chose.

The second is punished honesty. Assessment: if reporting a deviation gets you disciplined,
deviations go underground and you lose the very data you need to improve. FACT: the answer in
safety-critical fields is a "just culture," which separates honest error and considered
at-risk choices from genuine recklessness and responds to each differently, treating reports as
data to learn from rather than confessions to punish (James Reason, "Managing the Risks of
Organizational Accidents," 1997; Sidney Dekker, "Just Culture"). FACT: Dekker's own caution is
that the line between acceptable and blameworthy is not a fixed, objective thing but a judgment
that shifts with context, which is exactly why it has to be named out loud and applied
consistently instead of decided case by case in the heat of the moment.

## Who owns what

Assessment, and this is the level distinction you drew. Technical authority owns the envelope,
the non-negotiables that are not up for a local vote, and owns the change process itself. The
frontline owns the work-as-done knowledge and is the one who surfaces the deviation, so if they
are not in the room the standard will be wrong and quietly ignored. The first-line supervisor
owns fidelity and honest routing, making sure the agreed method is followed and that a deviation
gets reported rather than buried; they are the hinge the whole loop turns on. Management stewards
the loop and holds the just-culture line, which means protecting the person who reports honestly
and moving fast enough that reporting feels worth it.

## The leadership move

Assessment: the manager's job in governed work is not to police compliance, it is to run the
loop. FACT: the clearest evidence that this can work is the NUMMI auto plant, where Paul Adler
found that heavy standardization did not deaden workers as expected, because the workers
themselves, not the engineers, analyzed the jobs, designed the procedures, and built the
consensus for new standards, and that ownership made the standardization motivating rather than
alienating (Adler, "Time-and-Motion Regained," Harvard Business Review, 1993). So the move is
three parts: get the people who do the work to author the standard, make honest and documented
deviation safe, and run the judging loop fast enough that good deviations become the standard and
one-offs get closed clean. A standard the floor wrote and can change is a living baseline. One
imposed from above and frozen is the thing people route around, which is drift waiting to happen.

## How much to trust this

Assessment: the core loop is about as well-supported as an operating idea gets, and for a
specific reason. FACT: the same document-measure-judge structure has been codified independently
in process safety (Management of Change), pharmaceutical quality (deviation, CAPA, and change
control), and improvement science (Plan-Do-Study-Act), none of which borrowed from the others.
Assessment: the same thinking runs through aviation and healthcare safety practice too, which is
strong evidence the loop reflects something real rather than one consultant's model. The softer parts are the human-factors framings. Assessment: "work-as-done
versus work-as-imagined" and "just culture" are useful lenses with live academic debate behind
them, not settled law, and Dekker himself concedes the culpability line is hard to draw. Treat
those as sensible guidance, not proof. The load-bearing caveat is that it is the whole system
that works: a standard with no honest reporting rots into normalization of deviance, and honest
reporting with no standard has nothing to improve from. You need both, plus a manager who
protects the reporter and closes the loop fast.

**Evidence check (2026 review, verdict: well-supported for the loop, sensible-but-contested for
the culture framing).** The document-measure-judge loop is independently codified in
[process safety](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.119) (OSHA
Management of Change), [pharmaceutical
quality](https://intuitionlabs.ai/articles/deviations-capa-change-control) (deviation, CAPA, and
change control), and [improvement science](https://deming.org/explore/pdsa/) (the Shewhart and
Deming cycle), and the failure mode it guards against is documented in
[aerospace](https://sma.nasa.gov/docs/default-source/safety-messages/safetymessage-normalizationofdeviance-2014-11-03b.pdf)
(Vaughan's normalization of deviance). The frontline-authored-standard claim rests on a strong
single case ([NUMMI](http://faculty.marshall.usc.edu/Paul-Adler/research/HBR%20NUMMI.pdf), Adler
1993), and the culture guidance ([Safety-II](https://www.england.nhs.uk/signuptosafety/wp-content/uploads/sites/16/2015/10/safety-1-safety-2-whte-papr.pdf);
[just culture](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10111-008-0110-7))
is a well-argued perspective, not a proven law. Bottom line: document and measure every
deviation, judge it as new-standard or one-off on a clock, protect the person who reports it, and
you have the discipline every high-consequence industry converged on.
