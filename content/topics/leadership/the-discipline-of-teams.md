# The discipline of teams

The big idea of this article is that calling a group a "team" does not make it one, and the
difference matters enormously. Jon Katzenbach and Douglas Smith argue that a real team is a
specific, demanding thing with a clear definition, and that most groups we label teams are
really just working groups. Knowing which one you have, and which one the job actually needs,
is the first discipline of managing teams.

FACT: the article draws on the authors' research for their book *The Wisdom of Teams*, in
which they talked with hundreds of people on more than 50 teams in 30-plus companies, from
Motorola and Hewlett-Packard to a Desert Storm unit and the Girl Scouts. (Katzenbach &
Smith, HBR, 1993.)

## A team is not the same as a group

Start with the distinction the whole article turns on. A **working group** performs based on
what its members do as individuals. They share information and help each other, but each
person is accountable for their own results, and nothing is produced that required them to
work as one. A **team** produces things that only the joint effort can produce, and its
members hold *each other* accountable, not just themselves.

Assessment: the point is not that teams are always better. Working groups are often the
right, lower-risk choice, especially when the work is really a set of individual jobs done
well. A team is worth the extra effort and friction only when the task genuinely needs joint
work. Faking it, calling a group a team without the substance, is the worst outcome: you get
the cost and none of the benefit.

## The definition, and why each word earns its place

Here is the authors' working definition, which is worth reading slowly:

> A team is a small number of people with complementary skills who are committed to a common
> purpose, set of performance goals, and approach for which they hold themselves mutually
> accountable.

Each piece is a requirement, not decoration:

- **Small number.** FACT: nearly every effective team they studied had between 2 and 25
  people, most fewer than 10. Big groups struggle to interact, so they split into subteams
  or settle for vague "missions." Keep teams small.
- **Complementary skills.** A team needs the right mix of three kinds of skill: technical or
  functional expertise, problem-solving and decision-making, and people skills. Assessment:
  interestingly, they found no team started with all the skills it needed. Teams are where
  people *build* the missing skills, so pick for potential, not just proven ability.
- **Common purpose.** The best teams spend real time shaping a purpose they own, usually in
  response to a challenge handed to them from above. Assessment: management should set the
  challenge and then leave room for the team to make it theirs. Groups that never find a
  shared purpose stay mediocre.
- **Specific performance goals.** A purpose becomes real when it turns into concrete targets
  ("cut changeover time to two hours," "get the product to market in half the usual time").
  Clear goals keep score, level status differences, and let the team rack up the small wins
  that build momentum.
- **A common approach.** The team has to agree on the nuts and bolts of how it will work: who
  does what, how decisions get made, how the schedule holds. Crucially, everyone, including
  the leader, does real work. A "leader" who only reviews and delegates breaks the team.
- **Mutual accountability.** This is the hardest and most telling part. FACT: the authors
  frame it as the difference between "the boss holds me accountable" and "we hold ourselves
  accountable." You cannot order it into being; it grows out of the shared purpose, goals,
  and work, and it brings trust and commitment with it.

## Three kinds of team, three challenges

Assessment: the article also sorts teams by what they do, because each faces a different
trap.

- **Teams that recommend things** (task forces, project groups). Their risks are starting
  slow and fumbling the handoff. The fix: give them a clear charter and the right members,
  and pull the people who must *act* on the recommendation into the work early, so it does
  not die on delivery.
- **Teams that make or do things** (front-line operations, product development). Their risk
  is management "doing teams" as a fad. The fix: relentless focus on real performance at the
  points where the company's value is actually created, not team-building for its own sake.
- **Teams that run things** (leadership groups). The hardest kind, because senior people are
  busy and fiercely individual. FACT: the authors found that real teams at the top were
  usually small, often just twos and threes, not the whole set of direct reports. Their trap
  is confusing the broad company mission with a specific team purpose of their own.

## How much to trust this

Assessment: this is a definitional framework, not a statistical study, and on those terms it
is very strong. It has held up for thirty years because the core distinction, individual
accountability versus mutual accountability, is real and clarifying the moment you apply it
to a group you actually sit on. Use the definition as a diagnostic: when someone says "we're
a team," check it against the six requirements and you will usually learn something.

The honest limit: the evidence is interviews and cases chosen by the authors, so it tells you
what good teams *had in common*, not, in a controlled way, what *causes* success. The famous
company examples are also of their time (this is 1993). None of that undercuts the framework;
it just means read it as a sharp lens, not a proof. Its best use is the humble one: deciding
whether you even need a team, or whether a well-run working group would do the job with less
risk.
