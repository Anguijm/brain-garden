# The neuroscience of trust

The big idea of this article is that trust is not vague and mystical, you can build it on
purpose. Paul Zak traces trust to a brain chemical (oxytocin) and, from that research, offers
eight concrete manager behaviors that raise it. High-trust workplaces, he argues, measurably
outperform low-trust ones.

FACT: this ran in Harvard Business Review in 2017. (Zak, "The Neuroscience of Trust.")

## Eight behaviors that build trust

Zak's practical payoff is a short list: eight things a manager can do on purpose to raise
trust on a team.

![Diagram: eight trust-building behaviors around a center reading "a high-trust culture." Recognize excellence. Set hard but achievable goals. Give people discretion in how they work. Let people choose what they work on. Share information openly. Build relationships on purpose. Support whole-person growth. Show vulnerability by asking for help.](img/trust-behaviors.png)
*Eight things a leader can do to build trust. Diagram.*

- **Recognize excellence:** public, immediate, unexpected, personal praise right after a win.
- **Set challenging but achievable goals:** a bit of "good stress" bonds a team; vague or
  impossible goals just make people quit.
- **Give people discretion** in how they do the work once they're trained.
- **Let people choose** which projects they take on.
- **Share information openly:** uncertainty is a chronic stressor.
- **Build relationships intentionally:** social ties at work improve performance.
- **Support whole-person growth:** develop people, not just their output.
- **Show vulnerability:** a leader who asks for help signals security and invites cooperation.

FACT: Zak reports that people at high-trust companies (versus low-trust) had 74 percent less
stress, 106 percent more energy, 50 percent higher productivity, and 40 percent less burnout.

## How much to trust this

Assessment: the eight behaviors are sensible and align with the rest of this library
(recognition, autonomy, openness, vulnerability all show up elsewhere here). As a practical
checklist for building trust, it's fine.

But the honest caveats are large. FACT: the causal oxytocin evidence comes from small lab
games with nasal-spray doses and tiny money transfers; extrapolating a single "trust molecule"
to whole company cultures is a big leap, and much of the oxytocin-behavior literature has faced
replication trouble since. The eye-catching corporate percentages are self-reported
correlations from Zak's own survey (high performers may *cause* trust as much as the reverse),
and he sells trust-consulting built on this model. Assessment: keep the eight behaviors, and
treat the neuroscience and the numbers as suggestive packaging, not proof; the evidence check
below pins down just how far the oxytocin story has fallen.

**Evidence check (2026 review, verdict: weak).** The oxytocin foundation is not merely 'suggestive lab games,' it has actively failed replication. A 2015 critical review ([Nave, Camerer & McCullough, Perspectives on Psychological Science](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691615600138)) concluded that the finding that intranasal oxytocin boosts trust has not replicated and that the cumulative evidence does not robustly show trust is caused by oxytocin. A 2020 preregistered replication of the original trust-game study ([Declerck et al., Nature Human Behaviour](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-020-0878-x); N=677, over 95% power) found no effect in its confirmatory test, and some neuroscientists (Leng & Ludwig, 2016) argue that little intranasally applied oxytocin even reaches the brain, so treat the 'trust molecule' mechanism as largely unsupported. Keep the eight behaviors as reasonable advice backed by a modest, correlational trust-performance link, not by the dramatic causal numbers. Bottom line: do the eight behaviors because they are sensible management practice, but do not repeat the oxytocin story or the big percentages as facts.
