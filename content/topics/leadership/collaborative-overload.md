# Collaborative overload

The big idea of this article is that collaboration has a hidden cost, and it falls on a few
people. Rob Cross, Reb Rebele, and Adam Grant show that a small handful of helpful, capable
employees end up carrying most of the collaborative load, until they burn out and become
bottlenecks. The fix is not less collaboration, it is *managing* it like the scarce resource
it is.

FACT: this ran in Harvard Business Review in 2016. FACT: across 300-plus organizations, 20 to
35 percent of the value-adding collaboration came from just 3 to 5 percent of employees.
(Cross, Rebele & Grant, "Collaborative Overload.")

## Three things people give, and only one runs out

Assessment: the key insight is that people help each other with three different resources, and
they are not equal.

![Diagram: three resources people give. On the left, two that are shareable and don't run out: informational (knowledge and expertise) and social (introductions and network access). On the right, one that is finite and drains: personal (your own time and energy). A note reads: people default to asking for the finite one.](img/collaboration-resources.png)
*Two kinds of help you can give freely, and one that runs out. Diagram.*

- **Informational** (your knowledge and expertise) and **social** (introductions, access to
  your network) can be shared without using them up. Give someone knowledge or an intro and you
  still have it.
- **Personal** (your time and energy) is finite. Every meeting and request spends it, and it's
  gone.

FACT: the trap is that people default to asking for the *personal* resource, a 30-minute
meeting, when a five-minute answer or an introduction would do. So the most helpful people get
buried. Assessment: as someone becomes known as capable and willing, requests pile up, their
own work stalls, and the virtuous cycle turns vicious. Women and top performers get hit hardest.

## What to do about it

Assessment: leaders have to actively redistribute and filter collaboration, not just cheer it
on.

- **Map who's overloaded.** Network analysis usually surprises leaders; they can't name half
  the people carrying the load.
- **Filter the requests.** Teach your best people to say no, to hand off, or to give the
  cheaper resource (an answer or an intro) instead of a meeting. Reset when people call
  meetings at all.
- **Redistribute and buffer.** Push decisions down so fewer things need the same few people;
  create "utility players" to absorb demand.
- **Reward the assists.** FACT: only about half of an organization's top collaborators are also
  its top performers, partly because helping is invisible and unrewarded. Track and reward
  collaboration the way sports track assists, not just goals.

## How much to trust this

Assessment: this names a real, underappreciated problem, and the three-resources idea is a
genuinely useful tool ("do they need my *time*, or just my knowledge?"). It's a valuable
counterweight to the rest of this library's enthusiasm for collaboration and teamwork.

The honest caveats. FACT: many of the headline numbers (the 3-to-5 percent, the tipping points)
come from the authors' own consulting datasets, stated without full method, so treat them as
strong indications rather than precise constants. The gender findings rest on cited experiments
and a self-report poll of varying strength. And the 2016 tool examples are dated; the problem
has almost certainly grown worse in the Slack-and-Zoom era. Assessment: the core diagnosis and
the resource distinction are the keepers.

**Evidence check (2026 review, verdict: mixed).** Independent peer-reviewed work backs the core mechanisms (Kim et al. 2023 links helping *escalation* to burnout; Bergeron et al. 2013 shows heavy helpers advance more slowly; Heilman & Chen 2005 shows the gender asymmetry), and network science confirms the general shape that load concentrates on a few people. But the "collaboration burns you out" framing is more conditional than the article implies: a 2026 meta-analysis of 186 studies (Wiese et al.) finds helping is on average positively linked to well-being and life satisfaction, so the real danger is *overload and non-volitional* helping specifically, not collaboration itself, and the headline 3-5%/20-35% figures remain proprietary to the authors' consulting data and unreplicated.
