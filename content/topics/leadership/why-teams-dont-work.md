# Why teams don't work

The big idea of this article is a bracing corrective to team hype: most teams *underperform*
their potential. In this interview, the pioneering team researcher J. Richard Hackman explains
that coordination and motivation problems usually eat up the benefits of collaboration, so
"having a team is often worse than having no team at all", unless a leader deliberately sets up
the conditions for it to work.

FACT: this ran in Harvard Business Review in 2009 (Diane Coutu interviewing Hackman), drawing
on decades of his research. (Coutu & Hackman, "Why Teams Don't Work.")

## The five conditions

Hackman's answer is to design the right conditions in before the team ever meets, rather than
coaching personalities afterward.

![Diagram: raw inputs (people, resources) passing through five gates to reach an effective team. Gate 1, a real team (bounded, stable membership). Gate 2, a compelling direction. Gate 3, an enabling structure (right task, small size, clear norms). Gate 4, a supportive context (rewards, information). Gate 5, expert coaching. A note reads: these raise the odds; they don't guarantee magic.](img/five-conditions.png)
*Hackman's five conditions for an effective team. Diagram.*

- **A real team.** Bounded, with clear, stable membership. FACT: in a study of 120-plus senior
  teams, fewer than 10 percent of members agreed on who was even *on* the team.
- **A compelling direction.** Everyone knows and agrees on what they're doing together.
- **An enabling structure.** A good task, the right *small* number of people, and clear norms.
  FACT: Hackman's rule is "no double digits", he caps his own classes at six, because the links
  to manage grow explosively with size.
- **A supportive context.** Reward, information, and HR systems that back teamwork.
- **Expert coaching.** Help with the group's *process*, delivered at the right moments (a real
  launch, a midpoint check, a debrief).

## Two contrarian truths

Assessment: two of Hackman's findings cut against common sense.

- **Newness is the danger, not staleness.** FACT: 73 percent of airline-cockpit incidents
  happened on a crew's *first day* together; keep teams stable rather than constantly
  reshuffling them.
- **Every team needs a deviant.** FACT: in Hackman's research, teams with a dissenting voice,
  someone who asks "why are we doing this at all?", outperformed those without one. "It's when
  you lose the deviant that the team can become mediocre." Assessment: the move that holds up
  is *protecting* real dissent when it appears; studies of formally appointing a devil's
  advocate find that works less reliably than the genuine article (see the evidence check).

Assessment: and the leader should be "bossy about the ends, not the means", set a clear
direction, then let the team find its own way, in whatever style fits you ("you are not Jeff
Bezos").

## How much to trust this

Assessment: this is one of the most solid team notes in the library, because Hackman spent a
career gathering real evidence, and it's a healthy skeptic's counterweight to the "teamwork
always wins" message elsewhere. The five conditions, "keep it stable," and "protect dissent"
are genuinely actionable; hold "keep it small" as a sensible default rather than a law,
since the size evidence is weaker than the rest (see the evidence check).

The caveats are minor. FACT: the cockpit, orchestra, and senior-team figures are real research;
the "no double digits" rule and the jazz-player leadership advice are Hackman's rules of thumb
and opinion. And it's a 2009 interview, so the "virtual teams" bits predate modern remote work.
Assessment: trust the conditions and the small/stable/deviant findings; hold the exact
thresholds loosely.

**Evidence check (2026 review, verdict: mixed).** The broad claim that teams underperform their potential is well supported, but hold the specifics loosely: the largest meta-analysis on team size ([Bernerth et al. 2023, *Journal of Organizational Behavior*, 208 samples covering 21,435 teams](https://doi.org/10.1002/job.2708)) found essentially no general link between size and performance (a near-zero average correlation, meaning size by itself told you almost nothing about how a team performed, though a highly variable one), so "no double digits" is a rule of thumb, not a law, and much of Hackman's "enabling conditions" evidence rests on self-reported effectiveness rather than hard output. Treat the framework as a well-argued lens, and note that an appointed "deviant" works less reliably than genuine dissent. Bottom line: put your energy into stable membership, a clear direction, and protecting real dissent, and stop treating head count as the main lever.
