# The set-up-to-fail syndrome

The big idea of this article is uncomfortable: when someone on your team is underperforming,
you, the boss, may be quietly causing it. Jean-François Manzoni and Jean-Louis Barsoux show
how a boss's early doubt about an employee sets off a spiral that actually *makes* the
employee worse, then reads that as proof the doubt was right. It is the dark twin of the
Pygmalion effect: people live down to low expectations.

FACT: this ran in Harvard Business Review in 1998, based on studies of boss-and-employee pairs.
(Manzoni & Barsoux, "The Set-Up-to-Fail Syndrome.")

## The spiral

Assessment: the whole thing is a self-reinforcing loop, and once you see it, it is hard to
unsee.

![Diagram: a downward loop of arrows. A boss forms a doubt about an employee. The boss tightens supervision and control. The employee reads that as distrust and starts to withdraw and lose motivation. Performance actually drops. The boss takes that as proof and tightens control further, and the loop repeats, spiraling down.](img/set-up-to-fail-spiral.png)
*The set-up-to-fail spiral. Each turn makes the next one worse. Diagram.*

1. Something small makes the boss doubt an employee (a missed deadline, a lukewarm handover,
   or just poor chemistry). FACT: studies find bosses sort people into an "in" group and an
   "out" group as early as five days in.
2. The boss, meaning well, tightens the reins: more check-ins, more approvals, more
   second-guessing.
3. The employee reads the extra scrutiny as "my boss doesn't trust me," starts to doubt
   themselves, and pulls back, offering fewer ideas, avoiding the boss, doing the minimum. One
   described becoming "a robot."
4. That withdrawal looks to the boss like proof the person really is weak, so the boss clamps
   down harder. Back to step 2, spiraling down until the employee quits or is pushed out.

FACT: up to 90 percent of managers treat some reports as a trusted in-group (given autonomy
and the benefit of the doubt) and others as an out-group (managed by rules and close control).
And the bias runs deep: when an out-group member succeeds, the boss credits luck; when they
fail, it is their fault.

## The costs, and the fix

Assessment: the syndrome is expensive all around. The employee loses confidence and often
leaves. The boss burns energy policing and loses standing as others watch the unfairness. And
the whole team feels it: "it made us all feel like we're expendable."

The cure is a real conversation, or a few, done carefully. FACT: the authors give five
ingredients rather than a script:

- **Set a safe, neutral stage**, and do *not* call it "feedback" (which sounds like a
  one-way verdict). Admit up front that you may be part of the problem.
- **Agree on the facts** of where performance is actually weak, backed by specifics, not
  feelings, and let the employee push back and name their strengths.
- **Dig into causes together**, including your own behavior. Ask straight out: "What am I
  doing that makes this harder for you?"
- **Agree on goals and on how much supervision continues** (framed as temporary and
  shrinking as things improve, not permanent).
- **Agree to flag problems early**, both ways, from now on.

Assessment: better still is prevention, get genuinely and evenly involved with *everyone*
early, so guidance feels routine rather than like a punishment aimed at the weak.

## How much to trust this

Assessment: the mechanism is very believable and worth watching for in yourself, because the
scary part is that it runs on *good* intentions. "Am I controlling this person more because
they are actually weak, or are they weak because I am controlling them?" is a genuinely useful
question. It pairs naturally with the [feedback](the-feedback-fallacy) and
[individualizing](what-great-managers-do) ideas elsewhere in this library.

The honest caveats. FACT: the authors' own studies are small (about 50 boss-employee pairs,
plus an informal survey of executives), and some of the striking numbers (the 90 percent, the
five days) are borrowed from other researchers, not measured here. The vivid quotes are
illustrations, not data. Assessment: treat the *spiral* as a well-argued pattern to guard
against, and the specific figures as suggestive rather than precise. The core lesson, check
your own role before blaming the person, is sound.
