# One-on-one meetings

The big idea of this article is that the regular one-on-one meeting (the "1:1") is one of a
manager's most powerful tools, and most managers run it badly. Steven Rogelberg's key reframe
is that the 1:1 belongs to the *direct report*, it is time for their needs, obstacles, and
growth, not a status update the boss runs for their own convenience.

FACT: this ran in Harvard Business Review in 2022, based on surveys of over a thousand workers
and interviews with senior leaders. (Rogelberg, "Make the Most of Your One-on-One Meetings.")

## What a 1:1 is for

Assessment: the meeting should be dominated by the employee's world, their concerns, what is
blocking them, what they are trying to learn and become. It is *not* a place to micromanage,
not a sign you distrust the team, and not where you offload your own fires. FACT: in the
survey, nearly half of employees rated their 1:1s as subpar, usually because the manager
canceled them, dominated them, or turned them into a status report.

## How to run them

![Diagram: a three-part meeting flow. Open: be present (phone away), check your mood, start with rapport and appreciation. Core: listen (the report talks 50 to 90 percent of the time), then add your perspective and solve problems together. Close: agree on action items for both of you, write them down, thank them, end on time. A side note reads: the report co-owns the agenda.](img/one-on-one-structure.png)
*A good 1:1: open, core, close, with the report doing most of the talking. Diagram.*

- **Cadence.** FACT: a weekly, roughly 30-minute meeting rated most desirable across all job
  levels and lined up with the highest engagement; every other week for 45 to 60 minutes was
  second. Weekly is best for new or junior people, a new manager, and remote teams; monthly
  rated worst. A whole year of weekly 1:1s with one person is only about 25 hours, cheap
  against losing and replacing them.
- **The report co-owns the agenda.** FACT: meetings were rated best when the employee helped
  set the agenda. Have both of you bring topics, and work the employee's list first.
- **Be present, and listen.** Kill notifications, put the phone away, check your own mood
  (it's contagious). Aim for the report talking 50 to 90 percent of the time.
- **Close with action items** for both of you, written down, and actually follow up.
- **Never cancel casually.** If you must, move it *up*, not out, or just shorten it. Canceling
  tells people they don't matter.

Assessment: good questions carry the meeting. "Is anything slowing you down right now, and how
can I help?" "What feedback from me would be useful?" "What would you like to be doing in a
few years?"

## How much to trust this

Assessment: this is the most straightforwardly practical note in the library, and the central
reframe (the 1:1 is *theirs*, not yours) is a genuinely useful correction. The cadence and
agenda advice is easy to apply Monday morning, and 1:1s are where the coaching, feedback, and
trust ideas elsewhere in this library actually get delivered.

The honest caveat is that the specifics are survey-based, so they are associations, not proof.
FACT: the "50 to 90 percent talk time," the "weekly is best" finding, and the location
rankings come from self-reported surveys, and the vivid "Bill" is explicitly a composite
character, not a real case. The author also wrote a book arguing meetings matter, so the framing
is his thesis. Assessment: none of that makes the advice wrong, it is sensible and low-risk,
just hold the exact numbers as informed guidance rather than hard law.
