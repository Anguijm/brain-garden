# Improvement science from a leadership perspective

There is a name for the thing these notes have been circling. It is called improvement science,
and it is the study of how work gets better, plus the harder second question of whether the
methods we use to make work better actually do. It is not a brand, a belt, or a binder. It is
about a century old, it grew out of factory statistics, and it has since been rebuilt more or
less independently in healthcare, in aviation, in pharmaceutical manufacturing, and in public
education. This note is the leader's version: what the field is, the handful of ideas from it
that are worth your time, and an unusually blunt account of how much of it does not survive
contact with evidence. That last part is not a hedge. It is the most useful thing in here,
because the parts that fail are the parts most organizations buy, and the parts that hold are
the parts most organizations skip.

## What it actually is

FACT: the Health Foundation, a UK research body, defines it as "finding out how to improve and
make changes in the most effective way... systematically examining the methods and factors that
best work to facilitate quality improvement," and draws the line this way: "whereas 'improvement'
focuses on optimising the benefits of change, 'improvement science' focuses on maximising
learning from improvement" (Health Foundation, *Evidence scan: Improvement science*, 2011).
FACT: the Institute for Healthcare Improvement calls it "an applied science that emphasizes
innovation, rapid-cycle testing in the field, and spread in order to generate learning about what
changes, in which contexts, produce improvements," and traces it directly to W. Edwards Deming.

Assessment: the distinction in that first definition is the whole reason the field is worth
knowing. Improving something is a project. Improvement science is the discipline of learning
enough from the attempt that the next one goes better and that you can tell whether it worked at
all. Most organizations do the first and skip the second, which is why they keep relaunching the
same initiative.

Two things are worth knowing about its reach. FACT: the four ideas at its core come from Deming,
who called them the System of Profound Knowledge and named them "(1) appreciation for a system;
(2) knowledge about variation; (3) theory of knowledge; and (4) psychology" (Deming, *The New
Economics*, 1993). Assessment: the field has also travelled well outside
manufacturing and healthcare. FACT: the Carnegie Foundation for the Advancement of Teaching built
its school-improvement work on it, citing Deming and the Institute for Healthcare Improvement
explicitly, and its second core principle is a sentence any shipyard manager will recognize: "Variation in performance is the
core problem to address. The critical issue is not what works, but rather what works, for whom
and under what set of conditions."

## The one skill that pays for itself: reading variation

Assessment: if you take one thing from this whole field, take this. Most management damage is
done by people reacting to normal fluctuation as though it meant something.

FACT: Deming split the causes of trouble in two. "Common causes are called common because they
affect equally all workers in a section. They are faults of the system. They stay there until
removed by management... Special cause can be corrected on statistical signal by the production
worker himself" (Deming, "Report to Management," 1972). The useful way to hold the distinction is
by what each demands of you: common-cause variation is the process breathing, and the only way to
reduce it is to change the process; special-cause variation is something specific that happened,
and you go find it.

FACT: that gives a manager exactly two ways to be wrong. "Mistake 1 is to treat an outcome
resulting from a common cause as if it were a special cause. Mistake 2 is to treat an outcome
resulting from a special cause as if it were a common cause" (Mohammed et al., *The Lancet*,
2001). The same paper offers the cleanest analogy I have found. Sign your name ten times and no
two signatures match, but no signature is better or worse than the others. "If we want to reduce
the variation between signatures, we must change the way we write all signatures, not just the
ones that fail an adequate test." Mistake 1 is standing over the worst signature demanding an
explanation.

Mistake 1 has a name. Deming called it tampering: adjusting a process in response to noise, which
reliably makes the output worse. FACT: he demonstrated it with a funnel and a marble, where the
stated purpose was "to demonstrate the unbelievable losses from over adjustment," and with the red
bead experiment, whose lesson was "it is all too easy to blame workers for faults that belong to
the system" (Olsen, *INFORMS Transactions on Education*, 2007, citing *Out of the Crisis*).
FACT: the takeaways from the bead experiment are three sentences long: "A system in control will
still have variation... If a system is in control then only management has the power to reduce
defects because only management has the power to change the process. Pleading with workers or
setting quotas will have no effect other than demoralizing the workforce." Assessment: read that
last sentence twice, because it is the argument against most of what passes for performance
management.

So how do you tell the difference in practice? You plot the number over time and you use rules
instead of your gut. FACT: the standard run-chart rules are a shift (six or more consecutive
points on the same side of the median), too few or too many crossings of the median, and an
astronomical point that everyone looking at the chart agrees is unlike the rest (Perla, Provost &
Murray, *BMJ Quality & Safety*, 2011). The same paper names the failure exactly: "people will
often over- or under-react to a single or most recent data point (and begin tampering, possibly
making things worse)." One more piece of technique from it, which matters: set your median from
baseline data and freeze it, so that new data cannot quietly move the yardstick you are judging
against.

One honest correction, because the older teaching is wrong. FACT: the fourth classic rule, a
"trend" of five or more consecutive rising or falling points, was tested by simulation and failed.
"The trend rule is virtually useless for detection of linear drift over time, the purpose it was
intended for... We recommend that the use of the trend rule should be discontinued" (Anhøj &
Olesen, *PLOS ONE*, 2014). Treat an apparent trend as a reason to look, not as a signal.

The same statistics kill ranking. FACT: a control chart of nine neonatal units whose mortality
ranged from 15% to 28% showed only common-cause variation, giving "no grounds for taking action on
individual neonatal units," and league tables under stable conditions "are unreliable and their
guidance is equally unreliable" (Mohammed et al., 2001). FACT: the definitive statistical review
of performance league tables found the uncertainty around a rank so wide that most of the table
carries no information, and warned that "apparent improvements for low ranking institutions may
simply be a reflection of 'regression to the mean'" (Goldstein & Spiegelhalter, *Journal of the
Royal Statistical Society*, 1996). Assessment: if you rank ten shops on last quarter's numbers and
go ask the bottom two what is wrong, you are probably interrogating noise, and next quarter they
will improve whether or not you do anything, which will then teach you the wrong lesson.

And this is not a rare failure. FACT: a study of the board papers of thirty English health trusts
examined 1,488 performance charts and found that only 88 of them, six percent, showed the role of
chance at all; only seventeen of the thirty board packs contained even one such chart (Schmidtke
et al., *BMJ Quality & Safety*, 2017). Assessment: that is the actual state of practice at the
top of large, serious organizations. The chart on the wall is usually this month against last
month, colored red or green, which is the exact input that produces tampering.

## The method, in three questions

Assessment: the method itself is refreshingly small, and you can run it on a napkin. FACT: the
Model for Improvement asks three questions before any change: "What are we trying to accomplish?
How will we know that a change is an improvement? What change can we make that will result in
improvement?" (Langley et al., *The Improvement Guide*, 2009). Underneath them sits the cycle you
have probably seen: plan the change, do it small, study what the data actually show, then act by
adopting, adapting, or abandoning it.

Two bits of history are worth carrying, because they tell you what the method is for. FACT:
Deming rejected the more common "plan-do-check-act" label outright, writing to a colleague in 1990
"be sure to call it PDSA, not the corruption PDCA," and elsewhere "what you propose is not the
Deming cycle... How the PDCA ever came into existence I know not" (Moen & Norman, *Quality
Progress*, 2010). "Check" means verify that you complied. "Study" means find out what happened,
including the part you did not expect. FACT: Moen and Norman record that the requirement to
write down a prediction before you run the test was added deliberately, to force the comparison
between what you expected and what you got. Assessment: that single habit, writing the predicted
number before the change, is most of the value. It is what separates an experiment from a rollout
with a story attached.

## Now the uncomfortable part

Assessment: nearly all of the above is sound. Almost none of it is what actually happens when an
organization "does improvement," and the field's own literature is where you find that out.

FACT: a systematic review of published applications of the plan-do-study-act method found that
fewer than twenty percent (14 of 73) fully documented a sequence of iterative cycles, only two of
those fourteen started with a small-scale test before scaling, only four of forty-seven stated an
explicit prediction, no study at all used statistical process control to analyze its data, and
just two of seventy-three articles met all five underlying principles (Taylor et al., *BMJ Quality
& Safety*, 2014). FACT: and this is not merely bad writing, because a later study of 421
documented cycles inside a funded, actively coached improvement programme found 2% (7 of 421) met
all six fidelity measures, though the same study showed the practice is trainable, with explicit
predictions rising from zero to 17.5% under sustained support (McNicholas et al., *BMJ Quality &
Safety*, 2019).

FACT: the field's most famous success, a collaborative that cut central-line infections across
Michigan intensive care units, was replicated in England as "Matching Michigan" and did not beat
the background trend. "Each successive cluster to join the project had an entry-level infection
rate close to the post-intervention level of the preceding cluster," and the improvement was
judged "likely part of a wider secular trend" (Bion et al., *BMJ Quality & Safety*, 2013). FACT:
the ethnography that followed found that of seventeen participating units, one transformed its
practice, five boosted efforts already underway, and eleven made little change, and it named both
diseases in its opening line: "a failure to outperform the secular trend" and "the decline
effect," where a promising intervention stops working when someone else tries it (Dixon-Woods et
al., *Implementation Science*, 2013).

Assessment: the reason is that the original was never really a checklist. FACT: the retrospective
theory of the Michigan programme identified its active ingredients as social: a densely networked community
exerting peer pressure, reframing infections as a professional problem rather than a technical
one, and using published infection data as a disciplinary force (Dixon-Woods et al., *Milbank
Quarterly*, 2011). England shipped the checklist without the network and got the trend.

The pattern repeats. FACT: a study of surgical safety checklists across 101 Ontario hospitals and
more than 200,000 procedures found no significant reduction in mortality (odds ratio 0.91,
P=0.13) or complications (P=0.29) (Urbach et al., *New England Journal of Medicine*, 2014). FACT:
a systematic review of Lean in healthcare found that after screening 1,056 articles, 22 passed
quality review, none were randomized, only one had a control group, and the conclusion was that
Lean interventions have "no statistically significant association with patient satisfaction and
health outcomes" and "a negative association with financial costs and worker satisfaction"
(Moraros, Lemstra & Nwankwo, *International Journal for Quality in Health Care*, 2016). FACT: a
review of improvement collaboratives reported improvement in 83% of studies but its own authors
cautioned that this should be "interpreted cautiously since fewer than a third met established
quality and reporting criteria, and publication bias is likely," and an earlier review found that
of 72 published studies only nine had a control design, of which two showed no effect
(Wells et al., 2018; Schouten et al., *BMJ*, 2008).

FACT: even audit and feedback, the single most-studied improvement mechanism there is, produces
"a mean absolute increase in desired practice of 6.2%" across 292 trials (Cochrane review).
Assessment: six points is a real effect. It is not a transformation, and anyone selling you one
should be asked for their control group.

Assessment: the sharpest summary comes from inside the field. FACT: Mary Dixon-Woods, one of its
leading researchers, writes that "the evidence that it produces positive impacts in healthcare has been
very mixed, with many of the better-designed studies producing disappointing results," diagnoses
the cause as fidelity ("the superficial outer appearance of the intervention or QI method is
reproduced, but not the internal mechanisms"), warns that "multiple ill-coordinated small-scale QI
projects may, accordingly, degrade rather than improve the ability to achieve improvements," and
prescribes two things: "stop looking for magic bullets" and "fidelity will lie in the mechanisms
rather than fussy adherence to specific forms" (Dixon-Woods & Martin, *Future Hospital Journal*,
2016). FACT: the same point was made about Toyota seventeen years earlier: "observers confuse the
tools and practices they see on their plant visits with the system itself" (Spear & Bowen,
*Harvard Business Review*, 1999).

One housekeeping note, since you will hear both numbers. Assessment: "seventy percent of change
initiatives fail" has no empirical basis. A researcher traced five published instances of the
claim and found none of them supported by evidence. "Nearly sixty percent of Six Sigma projects
fail" traces to a single aerospace firm's internal project list, described in a 2010 newspaper
column. Neither is a statistic. Do not let anyone use them on you, in either direction.

## What actually makes it stick

Assessment: strip the branding away and the evidence points at a short list, most of which is
about capacity and attention rather than method.

**Capacity near the work beats sponsorship at the top.** FACT: reviews consistently associate top
management leadership with improvement success, with 72% of tested associations significant and
positive across 47 studies (Kaplan et al., *Milbank Quarterly*, 2010). But FACT: when the same
research group tested a full contextual model across 74 real projects, the factors that predicted
success were "resource availability, QI team leadership, team QI skills, microsystem motivation,
microsystem QI culture, and microsystem QI capability," with "weaker evidence for effects of
senior leader project sponsors" (Kaplan et al., *Health Care Management Review*, 2013).
Assessment: read those together and the message is that the executive matters mostly through what
the executive allocates. A sponsor who supplies belief supplies nothing. A sponsor who supplies
hours, skills, and a capable first-line leader supplies the thing that predicted success.

**The middle is the transmission, not the obstacle.** FACT: middle managers do four specific jobs
in any change: they pass on the facts, translate them into what this unit specifically must do,
identify the actual tasks required, and justify the change to the people doing it; their position
between the top and the floor is what lets information cross at all (Birken et al., *Implementation
Science*, 2012). FACT: when the link was measured, top-management exhortation had only a weak
direct effect on middle managers' commitment, while the firmer path ran through concrete allocated
policies such as what showed up in performance reviews (Birken et al., 2015). Assessment: your
support is worth what it changes about someone's calendar and evaluation, and nothing more.

**No slack, no improvement, and the deficit compounds.** FACT: observing 26 nurses across nine
hospitals for 239 hours, researchers logged 194 system failures and found that "on average, 33
minutes were lost per eight-hour shift due to coping with system failures that could have been
addressed and removed," and that for 93% of problems people followed an unwritten rule: do what it
takes to finish the task, no more, no less (Tucker & Edmondson, *California Management Review*,
2003). Assessment: that is the mechanism by which an organization becomes permanently unable to
fix itself. The workaround succeeds, the task completes, the cause is never surfaced, and it
recurs forever. FACT: researchers who modelled this dynamic recorded a plant manager's own
description of it: "Supervisors never had time to make improvements... they had to spend all their time just trying to keep the
line going... It was a kind of snowball effect that just kept getting worse," and the successful
cases were the exception, where leadership explicitly reinvested the time freed up by a gain into
the next improvement instead of harvesting all of it (Repenning & Sterman, *California Management
Review*, 2001).

**Few priorities, held for a long time, by people who stay.** FACT: an evidence review of 195
studies on the context around successful improvement concluded for "not individual, 'heroic'
leadership, but distributed and exercised in a more subtle and pluralist system. Continuity of
leadership is very important and lack of continuity highly detrimental," and that "pursuing fewer
priorities over a long time period is associated with achieving change" (Fulop & Robert, Health
Foundation, 2015).

**And management practice itself does cause output, which is the strongest evidence in this whole
note.** FACT: a randomized controlled trial in Indian textile plants gave treatment plants five
months of hands-on support implementing 38 operational management practices while control plants
received only the one-month diagnosis. "Adopting these management practices raised productivity by
17% in the first year through improved quality and efficiency and reduced inventory," and the
authors impute that "annual profitability increased by over \$300,000" per plant (Bloom et al.,
*Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 2013). FACT: nine years later about half
the practices had been dropped, and the single largest driver of the drop was the arrival of a new
plant manager (Bloom et al., NBER working paper, 2018). FACT: across 35,000 US manufacturing
plants, management practices "account for more than 20 percent of the variation in productivity, a
similar, or greater, percentage as that accounted for by R&D, ICT, or human capital," and 40% of
that variation sits between plants inside the same firm (Bloom et al., *American Economic Review*,
2019). Assessment: two plants, same owner, same policies, same capital, and a large share of the
performance difference is how they are run. That is the case for taking this seriously, and note
what produced the 17%: not a diagnosis, not a course, but five months of somebody on site helping
people change how they actually worked.

## Never let the number become the goal

Assessment: the second question of the method, how will we know a change is an improvement,
contains the trap that swallows entire agencies.

FACT: the canonical formulation is Marilyn Strathern's, not Goodhart's, though she credits the
idea to him: "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure" (Strathern,
*European Review*, 1997). FACT: measured, this looks like a health service reporting 96%
compliance with a four-hour treatment target while an independent survey of the same patients
found 77%; nine trusts "inappropriately" adjusting waiting lists and three deliberately
misreporting them; and a third of ambulance services "correcting" response times to fall under
eight minutes (Bevan & Hood, *BMJ*, 2006).

Assessment: the version closest to home is the 2014 US Department of Veterans Affairs Inspector
General report on the Phoenix VA. FACT: about 1,400 veterans were appropriately on the electronic
wait list and
"over 3,500 additional veterans, many of whom were on what we determined to be unofficial wait
lists." Of 79 staff interviewed, "thirty staff stated they used the wrong desired date of care,
resulting in appointments showing a false 0-day wait time" and "eleven staff stated they 'fixed'
or were instructed to 'fix' appointments with wait times greater than 14 days." And the finding
that makes this an improvement-methods story rather than only a scandal: "the emphasis by Ms.
Sharon Helman, the Director of PVAHCS, on her 'Wildly Important Goal' effort to improve access to
primary care resulted in a misleading portrayal of veterans' access to patient care." The problem
was not local; investigations at 93 sites "confirmed wait time manipulations were prevalent
throughout VHA," and the agency ended up removing the 14-day goal from employee performance
contracts.

Assessment: a named improvement framework, applied with energy, produced reported success that was
manufactured. The lesson is not that goals are bad. It is that the moment a number carries
consequences for the person reporting it, you have to measure it a second way, from outside the
chain that is accountable for it, or you are measuring compliance behavior instead of the work.

## Where this fits in this library

Assessment: three notes here are instances of this field, and they split cleanly into two lanes.

The **discretionary lane** is work where nobody has written down the one right way and the job is
to surface friction and try bounded bets. **[Frontline workers know how to solve your
problems](frontline-problem-solving)** carries the why and the evidence, including the hard
industrial studies, and **[Turning frontline input into action: a build-it
guide](frontline-input-playbook)** is the de-branded build.

The **governed lane** is work where a policy already says how the job is done and the question is
how it safely changes. **[Standards and deviations: how settled work safely
changes](standards-and-deviations)** covers the document, measure, judge loop, and how a
documented deviation gets ruled a new standard or a one-off.

Both lanes are the same underlying discipline: state what you are trying to accomplish, decide in
advance how you will know, change one thing at a small scale, look honestly at what the data show
over time, and keep or discard on the evidence. The difference is only how much freedom you have
before you start.

## How much to trust this

Assessment: the core intellectual apparatus, systems, variation, prediction, and psychology, is as
solid as anything in management, and the variation material is the most reliably useful thing in
this entire library because it is arithmetic rather than opinion. The evidence that structured
management practice causes performance is genuinely strong and unusually well identified, resting
on a randomized trial rather than a survey.

The weak part is the delivery. Assessment: the published record of improvement methods being
applied is poor, the marquee replications failed, the effect sizes on the best-studied mechanisms
are single-digit percentages, and the field's own leaders say the tools get copied while the
mechanisms do not. Anyone who tells you a method reliably transforms an operation is selling
something, and the honest read is that what transfers is social and structural: a network with
peer pressure, visible data, real capacity near the work, few priorities held for years, and
leaders who stay. The methods are the scaffolding around those, not a substitute for them.

Two specific cautions. Assessment: much of the leadership evidence in this field measures
*perceived* success rather than audited outcomes, and is cross-sectional, so read it as a pattern
rather than proof. And Deming's often-quoted claim that 94% of trouble belongs to the system is
his own estimate from experience, not a measurement, so use it as a stance rather than a number.

**Evidence check (2026 review, verdict: well-supported for variation and for management practice,
weak-to-mixed for the improvement methods themselves).** The variation material is statistical and
verified ([run chart rules](https://www.crohnscolitisfoundation.org/sites/default/files/2025-07/Perla%20et%20al_2010_The%20run%20chart-%20a%20simple%20analytical%20tool%20for%20learning%20from%20variation%20in%20healthcare%20processes.pdf),
with the [trend rule specifically refuted by simulation](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0113825)),
and the causal claim for management practice rests on a
[randomized controlled trial](https://poverty-action.org/sites/default/files/publications/Does-Management-Matter-Evidence-from-India-Bloom-et-al-2013.pdf)
showing a 17% productivity gain. Against that, a
[systematic review](https://www.crohnscolitisfoundation.org/sites/default/files/2025-07/Taylor%20et%20al_2013_PDSA_Systematic_Review_BMJ.pdf)
found fewer than 20% of published applications of the method actually used it as designed, the
flagship programme [failed to replicate](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3585494/) against
the secular trend, [Lean in healthcare shows no significant outcome
benefit](https://academic.oup.com/intqhc/article/28/2/150/1750317), and the field's own senior
critic concludes that ["fidelity will lie in the mechanisms rather than fussy adherence to specific
forms"](https://sites.bu.edu/ciis/files/2016/10/Dixon-Woods.pdf). Bottom line: learn to read
variation, put real hours and skill next to the work, hold few priorities for a long time, verify
any number that carries consequences from outside the chain that reports it, and treat every
branded method as scaffolding you will have to earn results through, not a mechanism that delivers
them.
