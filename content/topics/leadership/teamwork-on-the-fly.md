# Teamwork on the fly

The big idea of this article is that a lot of modern work can't wait for a stable team that has
trained together for years. Amy Edmondson calls the alternative "teaming", coordinating on the
fly with a shifting mix of people around a shifting problem. It's a verb and a skill, more like
a pickup basketball game than a rehearsed play, and it means executing and learning at the same
time.

FACT: this ran in Harvard Business Review in 2012. (Edmondson, "Teamwork on the Fly.")

## The hardware and the software of teaming

Assessment: Edmondson splits teaming into two layers.

![Diagram: two columns. The "hardware" of teaming (project management): scope the work, structure light scaffolding, and sort tasks by how interdependent they are. The "software" of teaming (leadership): emphasize purpose, build psychological safety, embrace failure, and put conflict to work. A banner reads: execution during, not after, learning.](img/teaming.png)
*Teaming has a hardware side (structure) and a software side (behavior). Diagram.*

- **The hardware** (project-management side): *scope* the work (who's needed, expecting the
  definition to change), *structure* light scaffolding (shared channels, preset role slots like
  an ER's "pods"), and *sort* tasks by interdependence (the back-and-forth "reciprocal" tasks
  are the bottlenecks, so schedule when people convene).
- **The software** (leadership side): *emphasize purpose*, *build psychological safety*,
  *embrace failure* (teaming produces failures that feed the next step), and *put conflict to
  work* (differences of view are a reason to team in the first place).

FACT: the vivid example is the Beijing Olympics "Water Cube", dozens of specialists from 20
disciplines and four countries, starting in two warring design camps, produced a landmark
neither firm would have reached alone by teaming across the conflict.

## How much to trust this

Assessment: "teaming" is a genuinely useful idea for anyone whose work no longer fits a stable,
long-lived team, and the hardware/software split is a clean way to think about it. It builds
directly on Edmondson's well-established psychological-safety research.

The caveats are the usual for this kind of piece. FACT: the memorable cases (Water Cube, the
RAZR phone, the Chilean mine rescue) are hand-picked success stories, not tested evidence, so
they risk survivorship bias; the one empirical claim (teams facing more novelty learned more)
is cited without method. Assessment: take the framework as a solid lens and the anecdotes as
illustration.

**Evidence check (2026 review, verdict: mixed).** The one empirically solid leg is psychological safety, and even there a 2017 meta-analysis (Frazier et al., 136 studies) finds only a moderate correlation with performance (about r = .3), mostly from cross-sectional data that can't prove causation, and a 2024 CIPD evidence review rates the construct's evidence as weak relative to other climate factors. "Teaming" itself, as distinct from psychological safety, rests mainly on hand-picked case studies rather than tested effects, so treat it as a useful lens rather than a proven method.
