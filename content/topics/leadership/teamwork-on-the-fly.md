# Teamwork on the fly

The big idea of this article is that a lot of modern work can't wait for a stable team that has
trained together for years. Amy Edmondson calls the alternative "teaming", coordinating on the
fly with a shifting mix of people around a shifting problem. It's a verb and a skill, more like
a pickup basketball game than a rehearsed play, and it means executing and learning at the same
time. (This note also folds in Edmondson's short companion piece on the three pillars of a
teaming culture; the original is archived.)

FACT: the anchor article ran in Harvard Business Review in 2012. (Edmondson, "Teamwork on the
Fly.")

## The hardware and the software of teaming

Assessment: Edmondson splits teaming into two layers.

![Diagram: two columns. The "hardware" of teaming (project management): scope the work, structure light scaffolding, and sort tasks by how interdependent they are. The "software" of teaming (leadership): emphasize purpose, build psychological safety, embrace failure, and put conflict to work. A banner reads: execution during, not after, learning.](img/teaming.png)
*Teaming has a hardware side (structure) and a software side (behavior). Diagram.*

- **The hardware** (project-management side): *scope* the work (who's needed, expecting the
  definition to change), *structure* light scaffolding (shared channels, preset role slots like
  an ER's "pods"), and *sort* tasks by interdependence (the back-and-forth "reciprocal" tasks
  are the bottlenecks, so schedule when people convene).
- **The software** (leadership side): *emphasize purpose*, *build psychological safety*,
  *embrace failure* (teaming produces failures that feed the next step), and *put conflict to
  work* (differences of view are a reason to team in the first place).

FACT: the vivid example is the Beijing Olympics "Water Cube", dozens of specialists from 20
disciplines and four countries, starting in two warring design camps, produced a landmark
neither firm would have reached alone by teaming across the conflict.

## The culture side: curiosity, passion, empathy

Assessment: in a 2013 companion piece, Edmondson argues that in the most innovative companies
teaming *is* the culture, and leaders build it by growing three dispositions in everyone.

![Diagram: three pillars holding up a "teaming culture." Curiosity (find out what others know). Passion (fuel effort and care). Empathy (see others' perspective). Under each, the leader behavior that builds it: ask and listen; show enthusiasm; attune to everyone.](img/teaming-pillars.png)
*Three dispositions a leader grows to make teaming the culture. Diagram.*

- **Curiosity** drives people to learn what others know and bring; leaders build it by asking
  genuine questions and actually listening.
- **Passion** fuels the effort to stretch for a shared goal; leaders build it by showing
  visible enthusiasm (it's infectious).
- **Empathy** is seeing another's perspective, which Edmondson calls critical to collaborating
  under pressure; leaders build it by attending to everyone, regardless of rank.

Assessment: the point underneath is that people's *natural* instinct on an unfamiliar team is
self-protection (you can't look good or be right all the time), which shuts down all three, so
the culture takes conscious work. FACT: the worked example is patient-safety pioneer Julie
Morath, who shifted a resistant hospital not by arguing harder but by *asking* ("was everything
as safe as you'd like this week?"). Assessment: the three pillars are a memorable frame, but
they were asserted in a short opinion piece, never tested; they are psychological safety in
different clothes.

## How much to trust this

Assessment: "teaming" is a genuinely useful idea for anyone whose work no longer fits a stable,
long-lived team, and the hardware/software split is a clean way to think about it. It builds
directly on Edmondson's psychological-safety research, which is heavily studied though mostly
correlational (see the check below).

The caveats are the usual for this kind of piece. FACT: the memorable cases (Water Cube, the
RAZR phone, the Chilean mine rescue, the Morath story) are hand-picked success stories, not
tested evidence, so they risk survivorship bias; the one empirical claim (teams facing more
novelty learned more) is cited without method. Assessment: take the framework as a solid lens
and the anecdotes as illustration.

**Evidence check (2026 review, verdict: mixed).** The one empirically solid leg is psychological safety, and even there a [2017 meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1111/peps.12183) (Frazier et al., 136 samples, *Personnel Psychology*) finds only a moderate association with task performance, drawn almost entirely from cross-sectional data that can't establish causation. A [2024 CIPD/CEBMa evidence review](https://www.cipd.org/globalassets/media/knowledge/knowledge-hub/evidence-reviews/2024-pdfs/8550-psychological-safety-scientific-summary.pdf) still treats psychological safety as a useful concept but judges the underlying studies "rather low" in quality (most are cross-sectional; only eight of 89 rated moderate or better), leaving the direction of many effects uncertain. "Teaming" itself, as distinct from psychological safety, rests mainly on hand-picked case studies rather than tested effects, and the three culture pillars have never been empirically tested at all, so treat both as useful lenses rather than proven methods. Bottom line: borrow the scoping and safety-building habits for fluid, cross-functional work, but don't promise anyone that teaming itself will lift performance.
