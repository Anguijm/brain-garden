# The leader as coach

The big idea of this article is that a manager's job is shifting from telling to asking.
Herminia Ibarra and Anne Scoular argue that in a fast-changing world, the boss no longer has
all the answers, so the best leaders act as coaches: they draw solutions *out* of their
people with good questions instead of pouring instructions *in*. The payoff is people who can
adapt and solve problems on their own.

FACT: this ran in Harvard Business Review in 2019. (Ibarra & Scoular, "The Leader as Coach.")

## Ask, don't just tell

Assessment: the authors sort coaching by two things: how much you put in (information and
advice) and how much you draw out (the other person's own thinking).

- **Directive** ("here's what to do"): high input, low draw-out. This is telling, or
  mentoring. Useful, but it builds little independence.
- **Laissez-faire** (leaving people alone): fine when someone is already doing well.
- **Non-directive** (listening and questioning): low input, high draw-out. This unlocks the
  person's own insight, and most managers are bad at it because they default to telling.
- **Situational** (the sweet spot): mixing the two to fit the moment. The advice is to
  practice non-directive coaching until it's second nature, *then* balance it with directive
  help when it's genuinely needed.

FACT: managers overrate themselves at this. In a study of 3,761 executives, 24 percent who
rated their coaching above average were placed in the *bottom* third by the people who worked
with them. Assessment: the common failure is "tell and sell", deciding the answer in advance
and using leading questions to nudge the person toward it. That is not coaching.

## The GROW model

The article's practical tool is a four-step structure for a coaching conversation, called
GROW.

![Diagram: a four-step flow. Goal, what do you want from this conversation. Reality, the facts of the current situation (ask what, when, where, who, not why). Options, what could you do (if you had a magic wand?). Will, what will you do, and on a 1 to 10 scale how likely are you to do it. An arrow loops back if the commitment score is 7 or lower.](img/grow-model.png)
*GROW: a structure for drawing out someone's own solution. Diagram.*

- **Goal**: what does the person want out of *this* exchange? ("What do you want when you
  walk out the door that you don't have now?")
- **Reality**: the facts of the current situation. Ask *what, when, where, who*, and avoid
  *why* (it invites excuses and sounds like judgment).
- **Options**: broaden the thinking. ("If you had a magic wand, what would you do?") Then
  weigh each option's upside, downside, and risk.
- **Will** (or way forward): nail the action ("What will you do?"), then check commitment:
  "On a scale of 1 to 10, how likely are you to do this?" Assessment: the authors' rule of
  thumb is that 8 or higher means they'll probably follow through, 7 or lower means they
  probably won't, so loop back; it's practical craft, not a tested threshold.

Assessment: the plainest advice in the article is the best. "Shut up and listen." Ask
open questions (the simplest, "What else?", is often the most powerful), leave room for
silence, and listen to understand rather than to reply.

## How much to trust this

Assessment: the core shift, ask more than you tell, is sound and widely echoed, and GROW is a
genuinely useful pocket structure for a hard conversation. Coaching is also the leadership
style managers say they use *least* (they claim no time), so a nudge toward it is valuable.

The honest caveats. FACT: co-author Anne Scoular runs a firm that trains leaders to coach,
and several of the article's showcase examples (a law firm, a consultancy) are the authors'
own clients or trainees, so the evidence is promotional, not independent. The flagship
Microsoft turnaround story is favorable and drawn from a case the author co-wrote.
Assessment: the one solid empirical bit is the "managers overrate their coaching" study;
Goleman's claim that coaching is the least-used style comes from his firm's proprietary
consulting data and was never independently replicated, so treat it as a claim, not a
finding. Treat GROW's specifics (the no-"why" rule, the
1-to-10 threshold) as the authors' practical craft, not proven law. The idea is strong; the
salesmanship around it is worth noticing.

**Evidence check (2026 review, verdict: mixed).** The best RCT-only meta-analysis, [de Haan & Nilsson (2023)](https://doi.org/10.5465/amle.2022.0107) in *Academy of Management Learning & Education*, finds coaching's overall effect is only moderate (a Hedges g of about 0.59, a medium-size effect), not large, with signs of publication bias inflating it. That evidence also comes mostly from qualified, often external coaches; for managers coaching their own teams the literature is thinner and methodologically weaker, and reviews describe it as promising but inconsistent rather than proven ([Beattie et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1177/1523422313520476)). GROW's specific session structure has essentially never been tested rigorously, so treat it as a useful habit rather than a validated method. Bottom line: practice asking before telling in your everyday conversations, expect moderate improvement rather than transformation, and use GROW as a loose guide, not a formula.
