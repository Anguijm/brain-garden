# Learn to delegate well

The big idea of this article is that the hardest leadership transition is the shift from *doing*
the work to *leading* it, and most managers under-delegate because they cling to the hands-on
execution that earned them praise. Jesse Sostrin's reframe is a paradox: to grow your impact,
be *more essential and less involved*.

FACT: this is a 2017 HBR.org article by Jesse Sostrin. (Sostrin, "To Be a Great Leader, You
Have to Learn How to Delegate Well.")

## Essential, not involved

Assessment: leaders confuse being *involved* (hands in the work) with being *essential* (the
thing that makes it go). His diagnostic: if you took an unexpected week off, would your
priorities still move? If not, you're more involved than essential.

High-essential, high-involved is the bottleneck (burnout, stalled team). Low-essential, high-involved is a super-sized individual contributor (busy, not productive). *Aim for essential-but-not-involved: the catalyst, not the bottleneck. Diagram.*

Four moves to delegate well:

- **Start with your reasons** — share the context and why it matters, not just the task.
- **Inspire their commitment** — define the work, check it against their capacity, and confirm
  their understanding face to face (they can't read your mind).
- **Engage at the right level** — stay involved enough to support and hold accountable, not so
  much that you micromanage; ask them what cadence fits.
- **Practice "yes," "no," and "yes, if"** — say yes to what draws on your best; route the rest
  to others; and make the case for where your impact is higher.

## How much to trust this

Assessment: the "essential vs. involved" reframe is a genuinely useful mental model, and the
four moves are sensible. It's a cleaner, more modern framing of the same lesson as
[who's got the monkey?](whos-got-the-monkey).

The honest caveat is that it's coaching opinion, not research: no data, a single composite
example ("Anika"), and the results are asserted. It also under-addresses the real hard parts of
delegating (trust, competence gaps, what happens when a handoff fails). Assessment: keep the
reframe and the four moves; don't expect the harder questions answered here.

**Evidence check (2026 review, verdict: mixed).** The underlying idea holds up directionally, but with two caveats the article ignores: meta-analyses find delegation mainly improves how people feel about the leader and only modestly improves actual performance (rho around .31), and the "be less involved" advice is not linear — multiple studies find an inverted-U where over-delegating raises stress and job demands and hurts performance past an optimal point.
