# What makes a leader?

The big idea of this article is simple: being smart and technically skilled gets you into
a leadership job, but it is not what makes you good at it. What separates strong leaders
from average ones, Daniel Goleman argues, is **emotional intelligence**, the ability to
understand and manage your own feelings and to read and handle other people's. He calls it
the one thing a leader cannot lead without.

FACT: this is one of the most-read articles Harvard Business Review has ever published.
Goleman first ran it in 1998 and HBR reprinted it in 2004. (HBR, "What Makes a Leader?")

## The core claim

Emotional intelligence (often shortened to EI or EQ) is the trait that matters most at the
top. Goleman looked at how nearly 200 large companies described the qualities of their best
performers, then sorted those qualities into three buckets: technical skills, raw brainpower
(IQ), and emotional intelligence.

FACT (as reported by the author): when he weighed how much each bucket contributed to being
an excellent performer, emotional intelligence "proved to be twice as important as the
others for jobs at all levels." And the higher the job, the more it mattered: among senior
leaders, he reports that nearly **90 percent** of the gap between star performers and
average ones came down to emotional intelligence rather than brains. (Goleman, from his
analysis of competency models at 188 companies.)

Assessment: the honest reading is that IQ and technical skill are the price of entry (he
calls them "threshold capabilities"), and emotional intelligence is what turns a competent
manager into a leader people follow. How airtight that "twice as important" number is, is a
separate question, covered at the end.

## The five parts of emotional intelligence

Goleman breaks emotional intelligence into five pieces. The first three are about managing
yourself. The last two are about managing your relationships with other people, and they
rest on the first three.

![Diagram: emotional intelligence in two groups. Managing yourself holds three parts stacked as a base, self-awareness, self-regulation, and motivation. Managing relationships holds two parts on top, empathy and social skill, with social skill drawn as the peak that the other four feed into.](img/eq-components.png)
*The five parts of emotional intelligence, from managing yourself up to managing others. Diagram.*

| Part | What it is | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| **Self-awareness** | Knowing your own moods and what drives you, and how they affect others | honest self-assessment, self-confidence, can laugh at yourself |
| **Self-regulation** | Controlling or redirecting bad impulses; thinking before acting | trustworthiness, comfort with change and uncertainty |
| **Motivation** | Being driven to achieve for reasons beyond money or status | drive, optimism after setbacks, commitment |
| **Empathy** | Understanding other people's feelings and factoring them in | keeps talent, reads people, serves customers well |
| **Social skill** | Managing relationships and building networks on purpose | leads change, persuades, builds teams |

A plain word on each:

**Self-awareness** is the foundation. Self-aware people are honest with themselves and
others. They know their strengths and their weak spots, and they can talk about both
without drama. FACT: Goleman says a good sign of self-awareness is someone who admits a
past failure openly, often with a smile. Assessment: he warns against reading that candor
as weakness. In an interview, the person who frankly names a mistake usually has more
self-command, not less.

**Self-regulation** is the ability to sit with a feeling instead of acting on it. Assessment:
Goleman notes this one is often undervalued. People who stay calm can look like "cold fish,"
while hotheads get mistaken for passionate leaders, but he argues that big displays of
temper have never been a mark of good leadership.

**Motivation**, here, means an inner drive to do the work well, not a hunger for a bigger
paycheck. The tell is someone who keeps raising their own bar and stays optimistic when
things go wrong.

**Empathy** is not softness. Assessment: Goleman is blunt that it does not mean adopting
everyone's feelings or trying to please everybody. It means *weighing* people's feelings,
along with other factors, when you make a decision. It is what lets a leader keep good
people and coach them well.

**Social skill** is empathy and self-command turned outward: building rapport and a wide
network so that when you need to move people, you can. He calls it "friendliness with a
purpose," and sums up the whole point with a line worth keeping: nothing important gets done
alone.

## Can you learn it?

Yes, but not from a weekend seminar. FACT: Goleman argues emotional intelligence grows with
age (the old word is "maturity") and can be deliberately built, but only through months of
practice and honest feedback, because it lives in the emotional part of the brain, which
learns by repetition rather than by reading. (Goleman, citing the Consortium for Research
on Emotional Intelligence in Organizations.)

Assessment: the practical takeaway is that you develop it the slow way. Get real feedback
on how you land with people, pick one habit to change, and practice it until it sticks. A
one-day course will not do it.

## How much to trust this

Assessment: the *shape* of the argument is very likely right, and matches common sense and a
lot of other research: leaders who cannot read or manage people struggle no matter how smart
they are. Treat the five-part model as a genuinely useful checklist for what to work on.

Be more careful with the exact numbers. FACT: the "twice as important" and "90 percent"
figures come from Goleman's own analysis of companies' private competency lists, using a
method that leans on judgment, and they have not been independently reproduced at that
scale. Assessment: read them as "emotional intelligence clearly matters a lot, especially
higher up," not as precise measurements. Two more honest flags: the brain science in the
article is a simplified, dated popular-science version, and Goleman himself builds and sells
emotional-intelligence training, so he has a stake in the idea. None of that sinks the
model. It just means you hold the slogans loosely and keep the useful core.

**Evidence check (2026 review, verdict: mixed).** Independent meta-analyses find emotional intelligence's link to performance and leadership is real but modest, and largely overlaps with IQ and personality, so once you control for those it adds little; the famous "twice as important" and "90 percent" figures are Goleman's own and have never been reproduced. A meta-analysis of 62 samples found much of the apparent EI-leadership connection vanishes when the two aren't scored by the same person, dropping from a strong same-rater correlation to a weak one ([Harms & Credé 2010](https://doi.org/10.1177/1548051809350894)), and a critical review concludes no rigorous study shows EI beats IQ plus the Big Five at predicting leadership ([Antonakis et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.leaqua.2009.01.006)), so treat the five-part model as a useful checklist, not a measured cause of leadership success.
