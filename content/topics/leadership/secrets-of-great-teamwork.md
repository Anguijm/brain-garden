# The secrets of great teamwork

The big idea of this article is that what makes a team work is not the personalities on it,
it is the conditions around it. Martine Haas and Mark Mortensen build on decades of research
by J. Richard Hackman to name the handful of conditions that let any team succeed, and add
one more that today's scattered, digital teams especially need.

FACT: this ran in Harvard Business Review in 2016 and draws on the authors' study of teams
across many global companies (over 300 interviews and 4,200 surveys). (Haas & Mortensen,
"The Secrets of Great Teamwork.")

## Modern teams are 4-D

Assessment: the reason old advice falls short is that today's teams are what the authors call
**4-D**: diverse, dispersed, digital, and dynamic (members come and go). Those traits create
new ways to fail, but the fundamentals that fix them are surprisingly few.

FACT: the foundation comes from Hackman, who spent 40 years studying teams and found the
thing that mattered most was not members' personalities or styles but a set of "enabling
conditions." (Hackman, *Leading Teams*.)

## The four conditions

![Diagram: four pillars holding up "team effectiveness." Three original pillars: compelling direction (a clear, challenging, meaningful goal), strong structure (right people and number, good task design, clear norms), supportive context (rewards, information, training, resources). A fourth pillar added for modern teams: shared mindset (a common identity and shared information that beats "us vs them" and knowledge gaps).](img/enabling-conditions.png)
*Three classic conditions plus one modern addition. Diagram.*

- **A compelling direction.** The team needs a goal that is clear, challenging (modest goals
  do not motivate), and consequential (people have to care about reaching it). FACT: on
  scattered teams, members often quietly hold *different* ideas of the goal, so it takes a
  frank conversation to get everyone truly aligned.
- **A strong structure.** The right people, the right *number* of people (add only who you
  need, since bigger teams communicate worse and free-ride more), tasks designed so the team
  owns a real piece of work with feedback, and a few clear norms of behavior.
- **A supportive context.** The resources to actually do the job: fair rewards, access to
  information, training, and the money and tools required. FACT: one manager blamed a
  "cultural clash" for a struggling overseas team until he visited and found the real problem
  was that they had far worse equipment and less funding than headquarters.
- **A shared mindset** (the modern addition). FACT: scattered teams drift into two failures:
  "us versus them" thinking between subgroups, and incomplete information, where some people
  know things others do not. The fix is to build a common identity and shared understanding,
  by stressing how each subgroup matters to the goal and creating shared experiences.

## Building a shared mindset

Assessment: two of the authors' practical tricks are worth stealing:

- **Structured unstructured time.** Deliberately set aside a few minutes, often the first ten
  of a meeting, for non-work talk, so distant teammates build a real picture of each other.
- **Check the conditions on a cadence.** Every few months, rate the team 1 to 5 on each of
  the four conditions and on its results (quality, teamwork, and whether members are growing),
  then look at where the lowest scores line up. That is usually where the fix is.

## How much to trust this

Assessment: this is one of the more solid frameworks in the library, partly because it rests
on Hackman's long, well-regarded body of team research rather than a single study. The
"fix the conditions, not the people" idea is a genuinely useful reframe: when a team is
struggling, look at its direction, structure, support, and shared mindset before blaming
personalities.

The honest caveats are mild. FACT: the specific claims come from the authors' own field
research and company anecdotes (named "Jim," "Alec," and so on), which illustrate rather than
prove, and the article reports no hard effect sizes. And it predates the COVID-era explosion
of remote work, though its warnings about distance and digital communication read as ahead of
their time rather than dated. Assessment: treat the four conditions as a reliable checklist,
and the individual stories as illustrations.
