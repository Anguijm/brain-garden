# Begin with trust

The big idea of this article is that leadership is not about you, it is about making the
people around you better, and none of that works without trust. Frances Frei and Anne
Morriss break trust into three parts, show that most of us reliably break the same one, and
argue that finding and fixing that weak spot is the fastest way to become a leader people
will actually follow.

FACT: this ran in Harvard Business Review in 2020 and is built around the authors' work
helping fix the culture at Uber. (Frei & Morriss, "Begin with Trust.")

## The trust triangle

People decide whether to trust you along three lines. Frei and Morriss call them the three
drivers of trust.

![Diagram: a triangle labeled TRUST in the center. The top vertex is Authenticity, "I experience the real you." The bottom-left vertex is Logic, "your reasoning and judgment are sound." The bottom-right vertex is Empathy, "I believe you care about me and my success." A caption reads: trust breaks at whichever corner you wobble on.](img/trust-triangle.png)
*The three drivers of trust. You lose trust at whichever corner is weakest. Diagram.*

- **Authenticity:** people feel they are getting the real you, not a mask or a filtered
  version. An authenticity wobble is when people sense you are hiding what you really think.
- **Logic:** people have faith in your judgment and your ability to deliver. A logic wobble
  is when they doubt your reasoning, or, just as often, when your reasoning is fine but you
  communicate it so poorly that it *seems* shaky.
- **Empathy:** people believe you care about them, not just yourself. An empathy wobble is
  when you seem more interested in your own success (checking your phone while others talk is
  the classic tell).

## Find your wobble

Assessment: the most useful move in the article is diagnosing your own weak corner. FACT:
the authors say almost everyone has one driver they break more than the others, their
"wobble," and under stress people double down on exactly the behavior that makes others
doubt them.

To find yours: think of a recent moment you were not trusted as much as you wanted. Give the
other person the benefit of the doubt, assume their doubt was fair, and ask which corner you
wobbled on. Were you hiding something (authenticity)? Did they doubt your reasoning or
delivery (logic)? Did they feel you cared more about yourself (empathy)? Assessment: look
across three or four such moments to find your pattern, and get one honest friend to check
your read, since we are not reliable judges of ourselves.

Each corner has its own fix:

- **Empathy:** put the phone fully away, out of sight, and take responsibility for everyone
  in the room getting what they need, even when it is not your meeting.
- **Logic:** anchor your case in what you truly know, then stop. And lead with your main
  point instead of saving it for the end, so people can follow you even if they get
  interrupted.
- **Authenticity:** say what you actually think rather than what you guess people want to
  hear, and build places where people can bring their real selves.

## How much to trust this

Assessment: the trust triangle is a clean, genuinely useful self-diagnostic. "Which of these
three do I most often break?" is a sharp question, and the fixes are concrete. The framing
that leadership is about empowering others (the test is whether things keep working after
you leave) is a healthy corrective to hero-worship leadership.

The honest caveats. FACT: this is a practitioner framework from the authors' consulting and
their book, not something drawn from controlled studies, so treat it as a well-built lens,
not proven science. And the centerpiece example, the Uber turnaround, is not neutral: Frances
Frei was a paid Uber adviser who held Uber shares (she discloses it), so the flattering
"we helped fix Uber" story is self-interested and should be read as their account, not an
independent verdict. Assessment: the triangle stands on its own usefulness regardless of how
the Uber story is scored.
