# Eight ways to build collaborative teams

The big idea of this article is a paradox. The hard problems companies face today need big
teams of diverse, highly educated specialists, often spread across locations. But those same
four traits, size, distance, diversity, and expertise, are exactly what make a team *stop*
cooperating. Lynda Gratton and Tamara Erickson studied teams that beat the paradox and pulled
out eight practices that let large, complex teams collaborate anyway.

FACT: the findings come from a study of 55 large teams, with surveys sent to 2,420 people
(1,543 replied, a 64 percent response rate) across 15 multinational companies. Team sizes
ranged from 4 to 183 people, averaging 44. (Gratton & Erickson, HBR, 2007.)

## The paradox, in one picture

The conditions that force you to build a team are the same ones that pull it apart.

![Diagram: four downward arrows pressing on a team's ability to cooperate. Large size (past about 20 people), working virtually across locations, high diversity of background, and a high proportion of highly educated experts. Each is labeled as both necessary for hard work and corrosive to cooperation.](img/eight-practices.png)
*The four traits that make a team necessary are the same four that make it hard. Diagram.*

FACT: the study found cooperation drops as a team grows past about 20 members, as more of it
goes virtual, as background diversity rises, and, strikingly, the more highly educated experts
it holds, the more likely it is to stall in conflict. Assessment: one leg of that needs
softening: later meta-analyses find demographic diversity has essentially no reliable effect
on team performance (see the evidence check), so read the diversity arrow as this study's
finding, not a law. The job is still not to avoid these traits, which the work demands, but
to deliberately counteract the friction they bring.

## The eight practices, in four groups

The practices run from the slow, deep work of company culture down to the near-term choices
about how you build a single team.

**Things top leaders do (build a collaborative culture):**

1. **Invest visibly in relationships.** Make a real, hard-to-copy investment that signals
   you mean it, like spaces designed to make people mix. (The example: a bank that built its
   headquarters around an indoor "main street" so thousands of employees cross paths daily.)
2. **Model collaboration yourself.** FACT: where senior executives visibly cooperated, the
   teams below them cooperated too. People copy what they see the top doing.
3. **Build a "gift culture."** Make everyday mentoring and coaching normal, so help flows
   freely instead of as tit-for-tat trades.

**Things HR can do (the two that actually worked):**

4. **Teach the skills of collaborating.** Train people in the real skills: appreciating
   others, having purposeful conversations, resolving conflict, and running projects.
5. **Support a sense of community.** Sponsor the events, groups, and networks that make
   people comfortable reaching out.

**Choosing the leader:**

6. **Pick leaders who are both task- and relationship-focused, and can switch.** FACT: the
   most productive teams had "ambidextrous" leaders who led on the task up front (nailing
   goals and responsibilities) and shifted to relationships once the work was in full swing.

**Building the team:**

7. **Seed it with existing relationships.** FACT: when 20 to 40 percent of members already
   knew and trusted each other, the team cooperated strongly from day one. (Too many
   pre-existing ties, though, can harden into rival cliques.)
8. **Make roles clear but leave the path open.** Cooperation rose when each person's *role*
   was sharply defined (so no one fights over turf) while the team kept freedom over *how* to
   reach the goal (which invites creativity). The counterintuitive lesson: clarity about who
   does what, ambiguity about how.

## The surprise that didn't hold up: rewards

Assessment: one finding is worth pulling out because later research contradicts it. FACT: in
*this* study, the type of reward system, team bonus versus individual, tied to collaboration
or not, had no detectable effect on how well these complex teams performed; of the formal HR
programs, only collaboration-skills training and support for informal community moved the
needle. But a 2014 meta-analysis of 146 studies found team-based rewards genuinely do improve
team performance (see the evidence check below). Assessment: so the safe reading is narrower
than the article's: don't expect incentive design alone to rescue a big, complex team, and
don't neglect the training and community work this study found effective. Rewards remain a
real lever; this study just failed to detect it.

## How much to trust this

Assessment: the practices are sensible and mostly match other research and lived experience,
so they are a solid checklist when you are about to stand up a big, mixed team. The
role-clarity-with-open-path idea and the "seed it with existing relationships" threshold are
the two most useful and least obvious.

Now the honest caveats, and there are a few. FACT: every one of the eight is a *correlation*
from a single research program, not a proven cause, so treat them as good bets, not
guarantees. The study is also from 2007, before modern remote-work tools and the post-2020
shift to hybrid, so its picture of "virtual" work is dated. And several of the companies held
up as models later ran into serious trouble (one collapsed in the 2008 financial crisis, two
others were bailed out or broken up), a good reminder that a company can be great at team
collaboration and still fail for other reasons. Read the eight as a design guide, not a
scoreboard.

**Evidence check (2026 review, verdict: mixed).** Two of the eight claims don't hold up against later meta-analyses. A 2014 meta-analysis of 146 studies ([Garbers & Konradt, *Journal of Occupational and Organizational Psychology*](https://doi.org/10.1111/joop.12039)) found team-based financial rewards do improve performance (a positive effect of about g = 0.45, close to a medium-sized effect on the usual scale), contradicting the article's "rewards barely mattered" surprise. And diversity meta-analyses ([Horwitz & Horwitz 2007, *Journal of Management*](https://doi.org/10.1177/0149206307308587); Bell et al. 2011) find surface-level demographic diversity has essentially no reliable effect on performance, while more job-related kinds of diversity can even help slightly, so treating diversity as a force that corrodes cooperation overstates the case. The leadership and pre-existing-relationships practices fare better in the wider evidence. Bottom line: when you stand up a big expert team, seed it with people who already trust each other, train collaboration skills, define roles sharply, and still use team rewards despite this article's null result.
