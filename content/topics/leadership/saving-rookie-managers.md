# Saving your rookie managers from themselves

The big idea of this article is that companies promote their best individual performers into
management, leave them to "learn by osmosis," and then watch them stumble in completely
predictable ways. Carol Walker names the classic rookie mistakes, and argues the new manager's
*boss* is the key fix: knowing the traps lets you coach people through them before they harden
into failures.

FACT: this ran in Harvard Business Review in 2002. (Walker, "Saving Your Rookie Managers from
Themselves.")

## Five rookie traps, and the boss's fix

Assessment: the root cause is that the job flipped from personal achievement to enabling
others, and insecurity turns the new manager inward. The article names five traps.

![Diagram: a two-column table. Left column, the rookie trap; right column, the boss's fix. Won't delegate (fears losing control) -> redefine the role, stage small delegations. Hides problems from the boss -> make it a partnership, "the cover-up is worse than the crime." Doesn't project confidence -> honest image feedback, own the message. Fights fires, ignores the big picture -> teach strategy, insist on written goals. Avoids giving feedback -> frame it around the report's own goals.](img/rookie-traps.png)
*The five rookie-manager traps and how a boss heads them off. Diagram.*

- **Won't delegate.** They keep doing the work that got them promoted, fearing loss of stature
  or control. Fix: redefine the role, and stage small, low-risk delegations to build
  confidence. "Delegation does not mean abdication", set milestones and check-ins.
- **Hides from the boss.** They stay "under the radar" and cover up failing projects. Fix:
  make it a partnership, tie their success to yours, and preach that "the cover-up is always
  worse than the crime."
- **Doesn't project confidence.** So focused on substance they ignore the image they cast, and
  their team mirrors their anxiety. Fix: give honest image feedback fast; make them own the
  message rather than acting as a mouthpiece for above.
- **Fights fires, misses the big picture.** Fix: teach that strategy is required to advance,
  and insist on written goals (distinct from the activities that support them).
- **Avoids giving feedback.** Fix: frame it around the employee's own goals, and target
  behaviors, not personalities.

## How much to trust this

Assessment: this is a practical, high-value companion to [becoming the boss](becoming-the-boss),
seen from the *boss's* side, and the five traps are instantly recognizable. The delegation and
"don't be a mouthpiece" points are especially useful. Its closing twist is honest: managers at
*all* levels make these mistakes, not just rookies.

The caveat is simply the evidence type. FACT: it's a practitioner essay built on the author's
consulting cases, and figures like "10 percent strategic, 90 percent tactical" appear with no
study or dataset cited behind them. Assessment: read those numbers as illustration of the
pattern she is describing, not as measurement. Assessment: take it as sound, experience-based
coaching advice, which is exactly what it is.

**Evidence check (2026 review, verdict: mixed).** The article's core diagnosis is actually backed by hard data: Benson, Li & Shue's 2019 study [*Promotions and the Peter Principle*](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjz022) (Quarterly Journal of Economics), which tracked nearly 40,000 salespeople across 131 firms, confirmed that top individual performers really are the ones most likely to be promoted and then flop as managers. But two of its five fixes are shakier than the confident tone implies: meta-analyses find delegation/empowerment has a real but only moderate effect on performance and can backfire when overdone, and [Kluger and DeNisi's classic 1996 meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.119.2.254) (Psychological Bulletin) found feedback actually made performance worse in over a third of cases. Assessment: the reconciliation is in what the feedback points at: task-focused feedback tends to help, person-focused feedback is what backfires (the same lesson as [the feedback fallacy](the-feedback-fallacy)). So "delegate more" and "give more feedback" are double-edged, not automatic wins. Bottom line: coach rookies to delegate in staged, low-risk steps and to aim feedback at the task rather than the person, instead of just telling them to do more of both.
