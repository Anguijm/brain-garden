# Feedback isn't enough

The big idea of this article is that telling people they're missing the mark is not the same as
helping them hit it. Peter Bregman and Howie Jacobson argue that feedback, even skillful
"constructive" feedback, rarely changes behavior on its own, because it triggers shame and
defensiveness. Real growth comes from an ongoing coaching relationship where you show up as an
*ally*, not a critic.

FACT: this is a 2021 HBR.org article. (Bregman & Jacobson, "Feedback Isn't Enough to Help Your
Employees Grow.") It's a natural sequel to [the feedback fallacy](the-feedback-fallacy).

## Why feedback alone fails, and what to do instead

Assessment: the reason feedback backfires is that people hear "you're not good enough and you
need to change," feel shame, and go into fight-or-flight, denying the problem or blaming others,
even when they look like they're taking it well. The fix is a four-step coaching process.

![Diagram: a four-step coaching loop. Shift from critic to ally (empathize, express confidence, ask permission). Identify an energizing outcome (the future they want). Discover a hidden opportunity (how can this problem help?). Create a level-10 plan (an action they're fully confident they'll take). An arrow loops back: act, assess, repeat.](img/ally-coaching.png)
*Grow people as an ally, not a critic: four steps. Diagram.*

- **Shift from critic to ally** — signal caring, confidence, and commitment so the person can
  drop their defenses. The move: empathize ("that sounds hard"), express confidence ("I know you
  can handle it"), ask permission ("want to think it through together?").
- **Identify an energizing outcome** — focus on the future they *want*, not the painful past.
- **Discover a hidden opportunity** — reframe the problem: "how could this help us get there?"
- **Create a level-10 plan** — a concrete next action they rate 10/10 on confidence they'll do
  it. What matters is that they take a new action and keep moving, not that the plan is perfect.

## How much to trust this

Assessment: the core point, that feedback lands only inside a trusting relationship and has to
be paired with real coaching, is sound and consistent with the rest of this library's
develop-people notes. The "ally, not critic" framing is genuinely useful.

The honest caveats. FACT: it's a book-promotion piece, so its strongest evidence is *borrowed*
(Boyatzis on how criticism reduces engagement; Buckingham and Goodall's [feedback
fallacy](the-feedback-fallacy)); its own headline results (turnover cut to 3 percent, reviews
from 50 to 95 percent) are self-reported about one consulting client, uncontrolled. Assessment:
trust the principle, which is well-echoed; treat the specific numbers as marketing.

**Evidence check (2026 review, verdict: mixed).** Peer-reviewed backing is mixed: the best meta-analysis, [Kluger & DeNisi 1996](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.119.2.254) (*Psychological Bulletin*), confirms feedback alone often fails (a third of interventions made performance worse) but found this was NOT reliably driven by whether feedback was positive or negative (what mattered was a task versus self focus), so the "criticism triggers shame" mechanism is shakier than implied, and experts actually seek out negative feedback (Finkelstein & Fishbach 2012, *Journal of Consumer Research*). Coaching does help, but meta-analytic effects are moderate ([delta ~ 0.36; Jones et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1111/joop.12119)), and the neuroscience the piece leans on (Boyatzis's positive-emotional-attractor model) has been criticized as overstated (Int. J. Evidence Based Coaching & Mentoring, 2024).
