# Seven transformations of leadership

The big idea of this article is that what really separates leaders is not their personality
or style but their inner "action logic", how they interpret the world and react when their
power or safety is challenged. David Rooke and William Torbert lay out seven action logics in
a rising order of effectiveness, and argue that leaders can consciously develop from one to
the next.

FACT: this ran in Harvard Business Review in 2005, based on a sentence-completion assessment
given to thousands of managers. (Rooke & Torbert, "Seven Transformations of Leadership.")

## The seven action logics

![Diagram: a seven-rung ladder rising from least to most effective, each labeled with the share of leaders at that level. Opportunist 5 percent, Diplomat 12 percent, Expert 38 percent, Achiever 30 percent, Individualist 10 percent, Strategist 4 percent, Alchemist 1 percent. The bottom three are marked less effective; the top four more effective.](img/action-logics.png)
*The seven action logics, from least to most effective. Diagram.*

- **Opportunist** (5%) — wins any way possible; self-serving, manipulative.
- **Diplomat** (12%) — avoids conflict, follows the group's norms.
- **Expert** (38%, the largest group) — rules by logic and expertise; a great individual
  contributor who can be dismissive of others.
- **Achiever** (30%) — delivers results through teams; the first genuinely effective level.
- **Individualist** (10%) — sees that no single way of working is "natural," and bridges the
  gaps between strategy and performance.
- **Strategist** (4%) — leads deep organizational and personal transformation.
- **Alchemist** (1%) — leads change across whole societies (their example is Mandela).

FACT: the bottom three logics (55% of the sample) were markedly less effective at carrying out
strategy; only the top few consistently transformed their organizations. A study of ten CEOs
found all five who scored as Strategists succeeded at transformation, versus two of the other
five.

## How much to trust this

Assessment: the underlying idea, that leaders can genuinely develop, not just polish a fixed
style, is valuable and hopeful, and the ladder is a useful mirror ("which of these is my
default under pressure?"). The "Expert to Achiever" jump (deliver results, don't just perfect
your expertise) is the most common and most useful.

The honest caveats. FACT: the whole framework rests on a proprietary assessment tied to the
authors (the Leadership Development Profile), and some of the evidence is tiny (only six
Alchemists were ever found). The stage model assumes "up" is always better and mostly linear,
which is a strong assumption. Assessment: treat the seven logics as a thought-provoking
developmental map, not a validated measurement, and Mandela-as-Alchemist as illustration, not
data.

**Evidence check (2026 review, verdict: mixed).** A 2006 review in The Leadership Quarterly (McCauley et al.) found the research linking developmental stage to leadership effectiveness has serious limitations: small, non-random samples and weak designs. The stage-measurement idea itself is well validated, but the specific "higher logic makes better leaders" payoff rests on a handful of small studies (n roughly 40-75), one of which found the ordinary personality trait conscientiousness did much of the predictive work, so treat the effect as real-but-modest and far less certain than the article's confident tone implies.
