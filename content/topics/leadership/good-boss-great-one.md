# Are you a good boss, or a great one?

The big idea of this article is that most bosses get reasonably good and then stop growing,
not out of complacency but because they don't know how to keep developing. Linda Hill and Kent
Lineback argue that becoming a *great* boss is a long journey of self-development along three
fronts, and that it never really ends.

FACT: this ran in Harvard Business Review in 2011, adapted from the authors' book *Being the
Boss*. (Hill & Lineback, "Are You a Good Boss, or a Great One?")

## The three imperatives

Assessment: management is getting a group's work done through influence, and influence rests on
trust. The authors say great bosses keep working on three interdependent things.

![Diagram: three pillars standing on a base. The pillars are: manage yourself (trust, built from competence plus character), manage your network (relationships across the whole organization, where you have no formal authority), and manage your team (forge a real "we" with shared purpose and culture). The base reads: continuous self-development (prep, do, review).](img/three-imperatives.png)
*The three imperatives, on a base of never-ending self-development. Diagram.*

- **Manage yourself.** Your influence rests on people's trust, and trust has two parts: belief
  in your *competence* and belief in your *character* (your motives). FACT: it is *not* built
  on being liked ("do it because we're friends") or feared ("do it because I'm the boss"),
  both of which the authors show backfiring.
- **Manage your network.** Reach out across the whole organization, not just up and down your
  own line, to get things done with people you have no authority over. Politics and conflict
  are normal features of organizations, not signs something is broken.
- **Manage your team.** Forge a real "we" with a shared purpose and culture, rather than
  managing everyone one-on-one.

Assessment: the connective tissue is continuous self-development. The authors' practice is
"prep, do, review", each day, preview how events can develop you, try new approaches, then
reflect on what happened versus what the leader you want to be would have done.

## How much to trust this

Assessment: the three imperatives are a clean, useful map, and one piece is genuinely
under-covered elsewhere in this library: **managing your network** (the outward, political,
cross-organization side of the job). "I'm not failing" as the watchword of the comfortable and
stuck is a sharp line worth remembering.

The caveats are the usual for this kind of piece. FACT: the evidence is the authors'
observation and named vignettes, not controlled study, and the self-assessment tool is
unvalidated. Assessment: take it as a well-argued framework for your own growth, not measured
proof, and use the "prep, do, review" habit, which is genuinely practical.

**Evidence check (2026 review, verdict: mixed).** The framework's building blocks hold up in the research. Trust really does rest on competence plus character: the standard trustworthiness model splits it into ability, benevolence, and integrity (Mayer, Davis & Schoorman, 1995), and a [132-sample meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.92.4.909) (Colquitt, Scott & LePine, 2007, *Journal of Applied Psychology*) confirms those dimensions build trust and that trust in turn predicts performance. Leadership skill is trainable, not fixed: a [meta-analysis of leadership training](https://doi.org/10.1037/apl0000241) (Lacerenza et al., 2017, *Journal of Applied Psychology*) finds measurable gains in learning and on-the-job results. Daily reflection has support too, though from a single large multi-experiment study (Di Stefano, Gino, Pisano & Staats, 2016) rather than a body of meta-analyses. Two honest caveats remain: trust's link to actual performance (as opposed to attitudes and goodwill) is positive but modest and only correlational, not proven cause-and-effect, and no study has ever tested the specific three-imperatives model or its self-assessment tool, so treat the model as a well-grounded map, not a validated instrument. Bottom line: keep investing in demonstrated competence and character, take training and a daily prep-do-review habit seriously because both measurably help, and use the three imperatives as a growth checklist rather than a scorecard.
