# Psychological safety

Psychological safety means people feel safe to say what they really think, ask questions,
admit mistakes, and disagree, without fear of being humiliated or punished. Amy Edmondson
(who coined it) and Michaela Kerrissey argue most people now get it wrong: it does not mean
being nice, comfortable, or protected from consequences. This note is the chapter's hub for
the idea; it merges three articles that circle the same point (the operator's library keeps
one strong note instead of three overlapping ones; the originals are archived).

FACT: Edmondson originated the modern idea of psychological safety and is one of the
most-cited researchers on it; the anchor piece here ran in Harvard Business Review in 2025.
(Edmondson & Kerrissey, "What People Get Wrong About Psychological Safety.")

## The short version

Assessment: psychological safety is a *shared* belief that a team is safe for interpersonal
risk, speaking up, asking questions, admitting mistakes, disagreeing, without fear of being
punished or humiliated. Edmondson's shorthand: "felt permission for candor." It is a property
of the *group*, not a personality trait. Assessment: Google's internal "Project Aristotle"
study is famous for calling it the top factor in team effectiveness, and that study was never
published for peer review, so treat the ranking as company communication rather than settled
science. Google's own write-up of it is no longer online, so this note cannot check the claim
against a source, which is its own reason for caution. The peer-reviewed picture is in the
evidence check below.

## What it is not

Assessment: the Edmondson and Kerrissey article is built around six misunderstandings. The
clarifying move each time is to separate psychological safety from something softer it gets
confused with.

- **It is not about being nice.** FACT: "nice" often means not saying what you think.
  Psychological safety is about *candor*. The authors draw the line sharply: "Nice is the easy
  way out of a difficult conversation. Kind is being respectful, caring, and honest."
- **It is not getting your way.** It means your view gets *heard*, not that it wins. (Their
  poster case: an employee who said "you didn't support my idea, and that made me feel
  psychologically unsafe." That is a misuse.)
- **It is not job security.** It is not freedom *from* layoffs; it is freedom *to* speak up
  without torching your career.
- **It is not the enemy of high standards.** This is the big one, below.
- **It is not a policy you can mandate.** You build it interaction by interaction; you cannot
  decree it.
- **It is not only the leader's job.** Leaders matter a lot, but anyone can invite input and
  respond well. It is "local", it varies team to team even inside one company.

## Safety and standards are two different dials

Assessment: the most important correction is that psychological safety and high performance
standards are not opposite ends of one slider. They are two separate dials, and you want both
turned up.

![Diagram: a two-by-two grid. The vertical axis is psychological safety, low to high; the horizontal axis is performance standards, low to high. Low safety and low standards is the apathy zone. High safety and low standards is the comfort zone. Low safety and high standards is the anxiety zone. High safety and high standards is the learning and high-performance zone.](img/psych-safety-2x2.png)
*Safety and standards are separate dials. You want the top-right. Diagram.*

Assessment: the authors' argument is that in a changing, uncertain world, top performance
needs *both* high standards and psychological safety, because safety is what lets a team
surface the hard truths and learn fast. Without it, people hide problems and drift into
groupthink. High standards with low safety produces fear and face-saving silence; high safety
with low standards produces a pleasant team that does not excel. The sweet spot is demanding
*and* safe. (Honest note: this clean four-box picture is the widely-used version of
Edmondson's model; the 2025 article only gestures at it, the full quadrant diagram comes from
her other work. The evidence check below explains why the "and standards" half is not
optional.)

## How to build it

FACT: Edmondson and Kerrissey offer three leader moves, and a surprising piece of advice:

- **Message, model, mentor.** Name the real challenges the team faces; model it yourself by
  asking questions and admitting what you do not know; and give people feedback on how well
  they invite and handle input.
- **Lead with the goal, not the slogan.** Their pull-quote: "talking less about psychological
  safety and more about the goal and why everyone's input matters is the first step in
  building psychological safety."
- **Run better conversations, and build rituals for candor** (end-of-week honest reviews,
  after-action reviews, "failure" retrospectives) so speaking up becomes the routine.

FACT: Amy Gallo's 2023 explainer (built around an interview with Edmondson) boils the daily
practice down to a four-move loop:

![Diagram: a four-step loop for building psychological safety. Make clear why people's voices matter. Admit your own fallibility. Actively invite input (ask, don't wait). Respond productively (appreciation and curiosity, not blame). The loop feeds back into more candor.](img/build-psych-safety.png)
*Four moves that build psychological safety. Diagram.*

- **Make clear why voices matter:** people default to silence, so tell them why you need
  their input.
- **Admit your own fallibility:** own your mistakes so others can own theirs.
- **Actively invite input:** ask open questions ("What are you seeing? Where do you stand?"),
  don't wait for volunteers.
- **Respond productively:** meet a risky idea or hard truth with appreciation and curiosity;
  replace blame with "what did you learn?"

## Connection plus courage: the practitioner's version

Assessment: JP Pawliw-Fry's "high-performing team" model (HBR, 2025) is essentially this same
safety-plus-standards insight dressed in practitioner language, and it earns its place here
for one genuinely useful tool. His two-by-two crosses *connection* (people feel safe and
valued) with *courage* (norms that allow accountability and hard conversations):

![Diagram: a two-by-two grid. The horizontal axis is connection (low to high); the vertical axis is courage (low to high). Low connection, low courage is a fear-based culture. High connection, low courage is a family culture (nice but avoids hard things). Low connection, high courage is a transactional culture (results over relationships, burnout). High connection, high courage is the target: accountability plus care.](img/last-8-culture.png)
*You need both connection and courage. The top-right corner is the goal. Diagram.*

- **Family culture** (high connection, low courage): nice, but avoids hard conversations;
  mediocre standards, groupthink.
- **Transactional culture** (low connection, high courage): results over relationships;
  blame, anxiety, burnout.
- **Fear-based culture** (low connection, low courage): walking on eggshells; low trust.
- **The target** (high connection, high courage): accountability *and* care; feedback-rich,
  and safe enough to take smart risks.

The practical tool is a structured team conversation: show the map, be honest about where you
think the team sits, ask where *they* think it sits, and agree on a few concrete commitments.
FACT: his memorable framing is "the last 8 percent", the highest-stakes part of a conversation
that people habitually leave out. One honest flag: his headline numbers (surveys of 34,000 and
72,000 people, the culture-type percentages) are proprietary, unpublished research backing a
branded product he sells, so keep the map and the exercise and treat the statistics as
marketing color.

## How much to trust this

Assessment: psychological safety is one of the most *studied* ideas in this library, and the
corrections here are genuinely useful, especially "safety is not niceness" and "safety and
high standards go together," which fix the two most common ways people misuse the term. But
"most studied" is not "proven": the research base is large yet mostly correlational, and the
strong performance claims run ahead of it, which is exactly what the evidence check below
calibrates.

Flags on the source articles themselves. FACT: the Edmondson and Kerrissey piece is mostly
argument and anecdote (the Bay of Pigs, a few company stories) rather than data, and the
authors disclose paid work for Google and Microsoft, two companies used as favorable examples.
Gallo's explainer borrows all its weight from Edmondson's research and Project Aristotle.
Pawliw-Fry's numbers are unpublished vendor research. Assessment: read the company anecdotes
as illustrations, not proof; the concept stands or falls on the peer-reviewed literature
below.

**Evidence check (2026 review, verdict: mixed).** Independent reviews are more cautious than these articles: psychological safety is heavily studied and reliably correlates with good outcomes, but its link to actual performance is only moderate ([Frazier et al. 2017 meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1111/peps.12183), 136 samples), strongest for learning behavior and information-sharing and noticeably weaker when performance is measured objectively rather than by self-report. The evidence is mostly cross-sectional rather than causal, and a [2024 CIPD evidence review](https://www.cipd.org/globalassets/media/knowledge/knowledge-hub/evidence-reviews/2024-pdfs/8550-psychological-safety-scientific-summary.pdf) rated the underlying study quality "rather low." A [2023 study (Eldor, Hodor and Cappelli)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2023.104255) even finds the link is nonlinear: past a point, more safety can depress routine-task performance unless it is paired with collective accountability, which is the research reason the "and high standards" half is essential. Treat it as a genuine, useful construct that predicts candor, learning, and engagement well; treat the strong performance claims (and the never-peer-reviewed Google "Project Aristotle" story) as softer than they sound. Bottom line: work on candor deliberately (invite input, respond without blame), but always pair it with explicit standards and accountability, because safety alone will not lift performance and can even dull it.
