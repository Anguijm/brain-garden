# Psychological safety

The big idea of this article is to rescue a good concept from a bad reputation. "Psychological
safety" has become a buzzword, and Amy Edmondson (who coined it) and Michaela Kerrissey argue
most people now get it wrong. It does not mean being nice, comfortable, or protected from
consequences. It means people feel safe to say what they really think, ask questions, admit
mistakes, and disagree, without fear of being humiliated or punished.

FACT: Edmondson originated the modern idea of psychological safety and is one of the
most-cited researchers on it; this piece ran in Harvard Business Review in 2025. (Edmondson &
Kerrissey, "What People Get Wrong About Psychological Safety.")

## What it is not

Assessment: the article is built around six misunderstandings. The clarifying move each time
is to separate psychological safety from something softer it gets confused with.

- **It is not about being nice.** FACT: "nice" often means not saying what you think.
  Psychological safety is about *candor*. The authors draw the line sharply: "Nice is the easy
  way out of a difficult conversation. Kind is being respectful, caring, and honest."
- **It is not getting your way.** It means your view gets *heard*, not that it wins. (Their
  poster case: an employee who said "you didn't support my idea, and that made me feel
  psychologically unsafe." That is a misuse.)
- **It is not job security.** It is not freedom *from* layoffs; it is freedom *to* speak up
  without torching your career.
- **It is not the enemy of high standards.** This is the big one, below.
- **It is not a policy you can mandate.** You build it interaction by interaction; you cannot
  decree it.
- **It is not only the leader's job.** Leaders matter a lot, but anyone can invite input and
  respond well. It is "local", it varies team to team even inside one company.

## Safety and standards are two different dials

Assessment: the most important correction is that psychological safety and high performance
standards are not opposite ends of one slider. They are two separate dials, and you want both
turned up.

FACT: the point is that in a changing, uncertain world, top performance needs *both* high
standards and psychological safety, because safety is what lets a team surface the hard truths
and learn fast. Without it, people hide problems and drift into groupthink. Assessment: high
standards with low safety produces fear and face-saving silence; high safety with low
standards produces a pleasant team that does not excel. The sweet spot is demanding *and* safe.
(Honest note: this clean four-box picture is the widely-used version of Edmondson's model; this
particular article only gestures at it, the full quadrant diagram comes from her other work.)

## How to build it

FACT: the authors offer three leader moves, and a surprising piece of advice:

- **Message, model, mentor.** Name the real challenges the team faces; model it yourself by
  asking questions and admitting what you do not know; and give people feedback on how well
  they invite and handle input.
- **Lead with the goal, not the slogan.** Their pull-quote: "talking less about psychological
  safety and more about the goal and why everyone's input matters is the first step in
  building psychological safety."
- **Run better conversations, and build rituals for candor** (end-of-week honest reviews,
  after-action reviews, "failure" retrospectives) so speaking up becomes the routine.

## How much to trust this

Assessment: the underlying concept is one of the best-supported ideas in this whole library.
Psychological safety has a large, respected research base built over decades, and the six
corrections here are genuinely useful, especially "safety is not niceness" and "safety and high
standards go together," which fix the two most common ways people misuse the term.

Two honest flags on *this article* specifically. FACT: it is mostly argument and anecdote (the
Bay of Pigs, a few company stories) rather than data; the authors assert the evidence is
"extensive and robust" but point to their other work rather than showing numbers here. And they
disclose that both did paid work for Google and Microsoft, two companies used as favorable
examples. Assessment: none of that undercuts the concept, which stands on a deep literature; it
just means read the company anecdotes as illustrations, not proof.

**Evidence check (2026 review, verdict: mixed).** Independent reviews are more cautious than this article: psychological safety is heavily studied and reliably correlates with good outcomes, but its link to actual performance is only moderate (Frazier et al. 2017 meta-analysis), the evidence is mostly cross-sectional rather than causal, and a 2024 CIPD evidence review rated the underlying study quality "rather low." Treat it as a genuine, useful construct that predicts learning and engagement well, but read the strong performance and "best-supported" claims (and the never-peer-reviewed Google "Project Aristotle" story) as softer than they sound.
