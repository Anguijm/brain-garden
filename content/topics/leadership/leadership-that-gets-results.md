# Leadership that gets results

The big idea here is that there is no single best way to lead. The most effective leaders
have a *set* of styles and switch between them to fit the moment, the way a golfer picks a
club for the shot. Daniel Goleman lays out six leadership styles, shows what each one does
to the mood of a team, and argues that the leaders who can play four or more of them get the
best results.

This is the natural sequel to [what makes a leader](what-makes-a-leader). That note said
emotional intelligence is the raw material. This one shows the finished product: each style
draws on different emotional-intelligence skills.

## The six styles

Each style has a one-line motto that captures its spirit.

| Style | In a phrase | Works best when | Effect on team mood |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Coercive** | "Do what I tell you" | a real crisis or turnaround, or a problem employee | strongly negative |
| **Authoritative** | "Come with me" | the team is adrift and needs a clear direction | strongly positive |
| **Affiliative** | "People come first" | healing rifts or morale, rebuilding trust | positive |
| **Democratic** | "What do you think?" | you need buy-in, or input from capable people | positive |
| **Pacesetting** | "Do as I do, now" | quick results from a skilled, motivated team | negative |
| **Coaching** | "Try this" | helping a willing person grow for the long term | positive |

FACT: Goleman ties the styles to "climate," his word for the working mood of a team,
measured across six things like how much freedom people feel and how committed they are. In
his data, climate accounted for nearly a third of a unit's financial results, and the two
harsh styles (coercive and pacesetting) pushed climate down while the other four lifted it.
(Goleman, "Leadership That Gets Results," from a study of 3,871 executives.)

## The four to lean on, and the two to ration

Assessment: the practical heart of the article is knowing which styles to use freely and
which to use like a strong medicine, in small doses.

Four styles generally help, and you should build all of them:

- **Authoritative** is the single most positive. You set a clear destination ("here is
  where we are going and why") but let people find their own way there. Best when a group
  is lost and needs direction. It stalls with a team of experts who know more than you.
- **Affiliative** builds bonds and heals stress ("people come first"). Its weakness is that
  praise alone lets poor work slide, so never use it by itself. Pair it with authoritative.
- **Democratic** builds buy-in by asking "what do you think?" Its failure mode is endless
  meetings and no decision, so it is a bad fit in a crisis or with people who lack the
  knowledge to weigh in.
- **Coaching** develops people for the future ("try this"). Goleman says it is the *least
  used* style, because leaders claim they lack the time, which he calls a mistake. Its
  quiet message, "I believe in you and I expect your best," is one people rise to.

Two styles work in narrow cases and poison the mood if overused:

- **Coercive** ("do what I tell you") demands instant obedience. FACT: in the data it had
  the most negative effect on climate of any style. Assessment: save it for a true emergency
  or a genuine problem employee, then stop. Leading this way day to day breaks people.
- **Pacesetting** ("do as I do, now") sets a blistering standard and models it. It sounds
  admirable and it backfires. Assessment: it works only for a small, excellent, highly
  motivated team; used broadly it overwhelms people and leaves them guessing. The
  cautionary example is a star specialist promoted to manager who micromanaged until he
  asked for his old job back.

## What to do with this

Assessment: the move is not to pick your favorite style, it is to grow your range. Diagnose
the situation first (is this a crisis, a lost team, a burned-out team, a talented team that
needs stretching?), then reach for the style that fits, and switch as things change. If you
genuinely cannot master a style, Goleman's fallback is to put someone on your team who
supplies it, but the better path is to build the underlying emotional-intelligence skill
over time.

FACT: leaders who had mastered four or more styles, especially the four positive ones, had
the best climate and performance in his data. One leader in the article turned a division
that had missed its profit target for six straight years into one that beat it, within
seven months, mostly by widening the styles she used.

## How much to trust this

Assessment: as a practical menu, this is excellent and holds up well. The core insight,
that good leaders flex their approach to the situation instead of running one play, is
sound and widely echoed. Use the six styles as a real toolkit.

Hold the precise figures more loosely. FACT: the evidence is one large but private
consulting dataset (from the firm Hay/McBer, which Goleman worked with), and the links
between style, mood, and money are correlations, not proof of cause. The exact numbers have
not been independently reproduced. Assessment: the direction is believable (harsh styles
tend to sour a team; clear direction and coaching tend to lift it), but do not treat the
decimal-point correlations as precise laws. As with the [emotional intelligence
note](what-makes-a-leader), keep the useful frame and go easy on the slogans.

**Evidence check (2026 review, verdict: mixed).** Independent research is less flattering than the article. A 2010 meta-analysis ([Harms & Credé, *Journal of Leadership & Organizational Studies*](https://doi.org/10.1177/1548051809350894)) found that the correlation between emotional intelligence and (transformational) leadership collapses from about .59 to .12 once the two are no longer rated by the same person, so much of the apparent effect is rating bias; and a critical review ([Antonakis, Ashkanasy & Dasborough, 2009, *The Leadership Quarterly*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.leaqua.2009.01.006)) argued that no study has shown emotional intelligence to add predictive power for leadership beyond IQ and personality. The six-style and 'one-third of results' figures themselves come from a proprietary consulting dataset published in a non-peer-reviewed magazine, and have never been independently replicated.
