# How to build a superteam that keeps getting better

The big idea of this article is that the best teams don't just perform well, they *keep
improving*, and in an AI-driven world where the ability to learn and adapt is decisive, that is
the strength that matters most. Ron Friedman argues continuous improvement is driven by the
leader, and lays out seven concrete practices that build a learning culture.

FACT: this ran in Harvard Business Review in 2026, based on a survey of 6,000-plus knowledge
workers and adapted from Friedman's book *Superteams*. (Friedman, "How to Build a Superteam.")

## Seven practices

Assessment: Friedman defines "superteams" as those that rate themselves top on both
effectiveness and relative performance, and finds they share seven leader habits.

![Diagram: seven practices arranged around a center reading "a team that keeps getting better." Run more experiments. Make curiosity contagious. Ask what people are stuck on. Roll up your sleeves. Make feedback feel like support. Encourage growth, even when it doesn't benefit you. Lead with meaning, not just metrics.](img/superteam-practices.png)
*Seven leader practices behind teams that continuously improve. Diagram.*

1. **Run more experiments** — keep trying new approaches even when things work; make it safe,
   easy, and rewarding to test and occasionally fail.
2. **Make curiosity contagious** — admit what you don't know, and model learning from anyone.
3. **Ask what people are stuck on** — steer meetings toward blockers, not tidy progress updates.
4. **Roll up your sleeves** — stay involved in the work (involvement, not micromanagement).
5. **Make feedback feel like support** — frame mistakes as data, so feedback drives growth
   instead of defensiveness.
6. **Encourage growth, even when it doesn't benefit you** — support side projects, learning,
   even people leaving; alumni goodwill pays off.
7. **Lead with meaning, not just metrics** — connect the work to a purpose so people don't
   settle for "good enough."

Assessment: notice how much of this recombines ideas already in the library, experiments and
curiosity are [psychological safety](psychological-safety); feedback-as-support is [the feedback
fallacy](the-feedback-fallacy); rolling up your sleeves is [doing real
work](the-discipline-of-teams); meaning is [motivation](how-to-motivate). That's a point in its
favor: it's a sensible synthesis.

## How much to trust this

Assessment: the seven practices are reasonable and align with the rest of this library, so as a
checklist they're fine. The strongest parts are the ones backed by *outside* research
(psychological safety from Google; the feedback meta-analysis showing feedback often backfires;
a study that hands-on managers feel more effective than distant ones).

The honest caveats are real. FACT: the core survey is the author's own proprietary research,
defines a "superteam" purely by self-rating (a halo-bias trap), and backs a book and consulting
firm he sells; the headline percentages come from that dataset with no methodology disclosed.
The Oklahoma City Thunder success story is a hand-picked anecdote. Assessment: trust the
practices where they echo the better-grounded notes here, and treat the specific numbers as the
author's framing of his own data, not proof.
