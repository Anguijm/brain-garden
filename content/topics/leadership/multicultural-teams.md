# Managing multicultural teams

The big idea of this article is that cultural differences create predictable, often invisible
problems on global teams, and that the *right response depends on the situation*. Jeanne Brett
and colleagues warn that a manager's instinct to jump in and settle the conflict is frequently
the wrong move, because it sidelines people and stops the team from learning to solve its own
problems.

FACT: this ran in Harvard Business Review in 2006, based on interviews with global-team managers
and members. (Brett, Behfar & Kern, "Managing Multicultural Teams.")

## Four barriers, four responses

Assessment: the article gives two four-part models: the barriers, and the ways to handle them.

![Diagram: on the left, four cultural barriers: direct vs. indirect communication; accents and fluency; attitudes toward hierarchy; decision-making norms. On the right, four strategies as an escalation ladder from lightest to last resort: adaptation (work around it), structural intervention (rearrange the team), managerial intervention (set norms or step in), and exit (remove a member).](img/multicultural-teams.png)
*Four cultural barriers, and four responses from lightest touch to last resort. Diagram.*

The four **barriers**: direct versus indirect communication; trouble with accents and fluency
(which gets misread as low competence); differing attitudes toward hierarchy and authority; and
conflicting norms for how fast decisions get made.

The four **strategies**, from lightest touch to last resort:

- **Adaptation** — the team acknowledges the difference and works around it. FACT: the authors
  call this the best option, it costs the least manager time and the team *learns* from solving
  its own problem. It works when people can chalk the friction up to culture, not personality.
- **Structural intervention** — rearrange the team (for example, into smaller mixed subgroups)
  to reduce friction.
- **Managerial intervention** — set the ground rules early, or step in to break a stalemate.
  Use rarely; overuse makes the team dependent on you.
- **Exit** — remove a member. A true last resort.

Assessment: the standout lesson is to respect the *other* side's hierarchy when you escalate,
go up your own chain to reach theirs at the same level, rather than leapfrogging their people
(one team nearly wrecked a deal by complaining straight to the top).

## How much to trust this

Assessment: the four-strategy ladder is a genuinely useful decision aid, and the core counsel,
default to adaptation and don't over-intervene, is a wise, non-obvious correction to the
manager's rescue instinct. It's the most practical cross-cultural guidance in the library.

The honest caveats. FACT: the evidence is qualitative interviews and anecdotes, no data or
controls, and it leans on broad national-culture generalizations ("Americans decide fast, the
Japanese are indirect") that risk stereotyping and can hide plain personality differences (the
authors themselves note some "cultural" conflicts are really ordinary management failures). And
it's a 2006 piece, pre-remote-work. Assessment: take the four strategies as a solid heuristic;
hold the cultural generalizations loosely and treat people as individuals.

**Evidence check (2026 review, verdict: mixed).** A 2010 meta-analysis of 108 studies (10,632 teams) found cultural diversity has a near-null net effect on performance: it does raise conflict and lower cohesion, but it also lifts creativity and satisfaction, so the "problems" framing is only half the story. And a meta-analysis of 558 studies found about 80% of cultural-value variation lies within countries rather than between them, so the national generalizations here (Americans decide fast, the Japanese are indirect) are weak predictors for any individual and should be treated as loose priors, not rules.
