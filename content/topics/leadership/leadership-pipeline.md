# Developing your leadership pipeline

The big idea of this article is that companies get a reliable supply of future leaders only
when they stop treating succession planning (a secret list of replacements) and leadership
development (training courses) as separate things. Jay Conger and Robert Fulmer argue you
should fuse them into one ongoing, developmental process, so the people on the list actually
build the skills their next jobs demand.

FACT: this ran in Harvard Business Review in 2003, based on six best-practice companies.
(Conger & Fulmer, "Developing Your Leadership Pipeline.")

## Five rules for a real pipeline

Assessment: the authors give five rules, all resting on the first.

- **Focus on development.** Make it a flexible, growth-oriented process, and make development
  *experiential* (real stretch assignments, job rotation, action-learning on live problems),
  not just classroom time.
- **Identify your linchpin positions.** Concentrate on the pivotal jobs the organization
  can't afford to fill badly, and grow people for them early, well below the top.
- **Make it transparent.** Drop the old secrecy; people contribute more when they know where
  they stand and what the next rung needs.
- **Measure progress.** Track whether the right people are moving into the right jobs at the
  right pace (internal-hire rates, "ready now" candidates, pipeline diversity).
- **Keep it flexible.** Expect to revise the system every year; none of the best firms left
  theirs unchanged.

FACT: the cautionary tale is leaders like Coca-Cola's Douglas Ivester, groomed on paper as
the number two but forced out within a few years, accomplished in one area but never developed
the broad general-management skills the top job needed.

## How much to trust this

Assessment: the core principle, marry development to succession and grow people through real
assignments, not just courses, is sound and widely echoed. The "linchpin positions" idea
(focus your effort on the pivotal roles) is genuinely useful.

The honest caveats. FACT: this rests on six self-selected best-practice companies with no
comparison group, so it shows what admired firms do, not that it *causes* success; the
company-reported numbers (attrition rates, internal-hire targets) aren't independently
audited. Assessment: it also reads as an early-2000s "the web will fix this" piece. Take the
five rules as solid practice, the specifics as dated.

**Evidence check (2026 review, verdict: mixed).** Peer-reviewed meta-analyses (e.g. Lacerenza et al. 2017) confirm that leadership development broadly works, but it is formal, classroom-inclusive training whose effects are best documented; the article's central prescription that experiential, on-the-job development beats classroom learning (the 70-20-10 idea) has little empirical support and rests on the same retrospective, self-selected-success reasoning that Rosenzweig's Halo Effect flags as survivorship bias. Treat the fusion principle as a reasonable practice, not a demonstrated cause of a leader pipeline.
