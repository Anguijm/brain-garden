# The making of an expert

The big idea of this article is that world-class skill is *built*, not born. K. Anders
Ericsson and colleagues argue that experts are made through years of "deliberate practice",
effortful, focused work aimed squarely at improving, with feedback and usually a coach, not
through natural talent. This is one of the most influential ideas in modern learning, and,
importantly, one that later research has partly walked back. Both halves matter.

FACT: this ran in Harvard Business Review in 2007. (Ericsson, Prietula & Cokely, "The Making of
an Expert.")

## Deliberate practice, not just practice

Assessment: the crucial distinction is that not all practice helps. Most people practice what
they are already good at, because it is easier and more fun. Deliberate practice is the
opposite.

![Diagram: a rising loop. Pick a task just beyond your current ability. Put in focused, effortful concentration. Get immediate feedback. Correct the specific weakness. Repeat the same task, then raise the bar. An arrow spirals upward, labeled: a good coach keeps moving the target up.](img/deliberate-practice.png)
*The deliberate-practice loop: work at the edge of your ability, with feedback. Diagram.*

- **Work just beyond what you can already do**, on a specific weakness, not comfortable
  repetition.
- **Get immediate feedback** so you can see and fix mistakes (the article's example: a golfer
  hits ten balls from the same spot to learn, where playing a round gives one shot and no
  correction).
- **Concentrate hard, for a limited time.** FACT: even elite performers rarely sustain more
  than four to five hours of true deliberate practice a day.
- **Get a coach.** Good coaches give the honest, sometimes painful feedback you can't generate
  alone, and keep raising the target. Over time you build an "inner coach" and self-correct.

FACT: the article makes a strong claim, that talent barely matters (Benjamin Bloom found no
early predictors among 120 elite performers; there's no IQ-to-expertise correlation in chess,
music, or sports), and popularized the idea that reaching the top takes roughly ten years or
10,000 hours.

## How much to trust this: the important part

Assessment: this is the note where the honest appraisal really matters, because the article
states its case more strongly than the evidence now supports.

The durable, useful core is solid: **purposeful, feedback-driven practice at the edge of your
ability, with a good coach, beats mindless repetition or waiting to discover a talent.** That
holds up and is worth applying to yourself and to developing others.

But the strong version, "talent doesn't matter" and the magic "10,000 hours", has been walked
back. FACT: a 2014 meta-analysis of 88 studies (Macnamara and colleagues) found deliberate
practice explains only about 12 percent of the differences in performance overall, and far less
in some fields (about 1 percent in professions). The rest comes from starting age, working
memory, personality, opportunity, coaching quality, and yes, some innate factors. FACT: Ericsson
himself objected that the popular "10,000-hour rule" (spread by Malcolm Gladwell) distorted his
work; 10,000 was a rough average for one group of elite violinists, not a threshold, and the
amount varies enormously by field. Assessment: so keep the deliberate-practice *method*, and
drop the "anyone can master anything with enough hours" slogan. (This note is exactly the kind
of claim the vault's queued academic-evidence check is for.)

**Evidence check (2026 review, verdict: well-supported).** The note's calibration is sound and matches the literature, but the exact size of the effect is genuinely unsettled, not just "small": a 2019 double-blind replication (Macnamara and Maitra) found the original violinist result much weaker than reported, while critics (Debatin et al. 2021) argue the meta-analyses undercount deliberate practice because they ignore how individualized the practice was. So treat the "roughly 12 percent" figure as one contested estimate rather than a settled number; what is robust is the direction, that practice matters a lot but is not the whole story and 10,000 hours is not a magic threshold.
