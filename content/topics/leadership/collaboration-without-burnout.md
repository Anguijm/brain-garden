# Collaboration without burnout

This is the individual's guide to escaping the collaboration overload described in
[collaborative overload](collaborative-overload). Rob Cross and colleagues argue that a lot of
overload is *self-inflicted*, so you can reclaim your time on your own, without waiting for the
organization to fix anything.

FACT: this ran in Harvard Business Review in 2018. FACT: in the authors' study, people who
acted on just four or five of its practices clawed back 18 to 24 percent of their collaborative
time. (Cross, Taylor & Zehner, "Collaboration Without Burnout.")

## Three places to cut the load

The authors group the fixes into three categories: why you say yes, what stays on your plate, and how you run what's left.

![Diagram: three categories of fixes for collaboration overload. Beliefs (why you say yes: surface the identity triggers, stop feeling indispensable). Role and schedule (eliminate what doesn't fit your goals: audit your calendar, delegate, reset expectations). Behavior (make what's left productive: better meetings, email norms, the right medium).](img/reclaim-collaboration.png)
*Three places to cut collaboration overload. Diagram.*

- **Beliefs**: understand *why* you take on too much. Often it's identity: you want to feel
  accomplished, look influential, avoid being called "not a team player," or fear the work will
  be done wrong without you. The fix starts with letting go of feeling indispensable, and
  recruiting a "truth teller" to flag when you're jumping in needlessly.
- **Role and schedule**: cut the collaboration that doesn't serve your real goals. Define your
  "north star," audit your calendar and email a few months back, and decline, delegate, or hand
  off recurring things as development chances for others.
- **Behavior**: make the collaboration you keep efficient: agendas and hard stops for meetings,
  email norms, and the right medium (skip the meeting when an answer will do).

Assessment: the sharpest line is that "saying yes to something always means saying no to, or
participating less fully in, something else."

## How much to trust this

Assessment: this is genuinely practical, and its core insight, that you have more control over
your own overload than you think, is a useful counterweight to feeling like a victim of the
calendar. The three categories are a good self-audit.

The honest caveat is the evidence source. FACT: the headline numbers (85 percent of time on
collaboration, the 18 to 24 percent reclaimed) come from the authors' own affiliated research,
not an independent source, and the individual stories are illustrations. Assessment: the advice
is low-risk and sensible; treat the specific figures as the study's own claims.

**Evidence check (2026 review, verdict: mixed).** The general problem is real: meta-analyses link heavy workload to burnout, and an independent 2023 longitudinal study (Kim, Kim & Cho, *Journal of Business Research*, four waves of ~900 U.S. healthcare workers) confirms that [helping escalation drives emotional exhaustion and work-family conflict through rising job demands](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbusres.2022.113348). But the note's 'fix it yourself' framing is likely overstated: a [2017 JAMA Internal Medicine meta-analysis (Panagioti et al.)](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2016.7674) found individual-level burnout fixes yield only small benefits while organization-directed changes work better, so reclaiming your own time helps at the margin but is not a substitute for structural change. The specific 18-24% reclaim figure comes from the authors' own consulting data and has never been independently replicated. Bottom line: run the self-audit and cut what you can, but if your whole team is drowning, push for structural fixes instead of just telling people to manage their calendars better.
