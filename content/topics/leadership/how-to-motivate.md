# How do you motivate employees?

The big idea of this article is that most of what companies do to "motivate" people does
not actually motivate them. Frederick Herzberg argues that rewards, threats, perks, and
pep talks only get you short-term *movement*, like kicking a dog to make it walk. Real,
lasting motivation comes from the work itself. If you want people to care, make the job
worth caring about.

FACT: this is one of Harvard Business Review's all-time best-selling reprints. Herzberg
first published it in 1968 and it was reprinted, essentially unchanged, in 2003. (Herzberg,
"One More Time: How Do You Motivate Employees?")

## Movement is not motivation

Start with Herzberg's blunt central image. He calls the usual management toolkit **KITA**,
his polite abbreviation for "a kick in the pants." A kick can be negative (a threat or
punishment) or positive (a reward, a bonus, a nicer title). Either way, he says, it is
still a kick: you are pushing or pulling the person from the outside.

Assessment: the trap is that positive KITA *feels* like motivation. You dangle a reward,
the person moves, and it looks like it worked. But the moment the reward stops, so does the
movement, so you have to dangle it again. As Herzberg puts it, "I am motivated; you move."
Real motivation is when the person has "a generator of their own" and wants to do the work
without you prodding.

## Two different lists: what stops unhappiness vs. what creates drive

Herzberg's main finding is that the things that make people *unhappy* at work are a
completely different set from the things that make them *motivated*. They are not two ends
of one scale. They are two separate scales.

*The two separate scales: hygiene factors stop dissatisfaction, motivators create drive. Diagram.*

- **Hygiene factors** (Herzberg's word for the dissatisfiers) are things *around* the job:
  company policy, your boss, pay, working conditions, job security. FACT: get these wrong
  and people are miserable. But get them right and you do not get motivation, you just get
  the absence of complaints. Fixing hygiene takes people from unhappy up to neutral.
- **Motivators** are things *inside* the work: achievement, recognition for it,
  responsibility, the work itself, and growth. FACT: these are what actually move people
  from neutral up to genuinely engaged.

Assessment: the practical punchline is that better pay and perks are worth doing (a
mistreated, underpaid person will not be motivated by anything), but they buy you a
non-miserable team, not a driven one. Drive comes from the work.

FACT: Herzberg backs this with a composite of 12 studies covering 1,685 workers. Of the
factors people named as sources of satisfaction, 81 percent were motivators; of the factors
named as sources of dissatisfaction, 69 percent were hygiene. (Herzberg's "critical
incident" studies.)

## The fix: enrich the job, don't just enlarge it

Assessment: Herzberg's answer is **job enrichment**, building motivators into the work
itself, and he is sharp about what does *not* count. Piling on more of the same dull tasks
(he calls it "job enlargement" or "horizontal loading") just makes the emptiness bigger,
"adding zero to zero." Rotating people between boring jobs is "substituting one zero for
another."

Real enrichment ("vertical loading") means handing over things that grow a person:

- Give people a complete, natural piece of work they own end to end.
- Remove some controls while keeping them accountable for results.
- Grant more authority and freedom in how they do the job.
- Let people see their own results directly, not filtered through the boss.
- Feed in new, harder tasks so they keep learning and can become experts.

## How much to trust this

Assessment: the core insight, that pay and perks prevent unhappiness while the work itself
creates drive, is genuinely useful and has held up as a way to *think*. Anyone who has seen
a raise wear off in a month, or seen someone light up at real responsibility, recognizes
the pattern.

Now the honest caveats, and they matter. FACT: the theory rests on Herzberg's method of
asking people to recall their best and worst moments at work. Later researchers pointed out
a bias: people tend to credit good times to their own achievements (motivators) and blame
bad times on their surroundings (hygiene), so the clean split may be partly an artifact of
how he asked. Studies using other methods have not reliably reproduced the strict
separation. Even Herzberg's own data is messy on one point: he files salary under hygiene,
but it shows up on both sides of his chart, so "money never motivates" overstates it.
Assessment: also flag two dated bits, the 1968 language is very much of its era, and his
advice to keep employees *out* of redesigning their own jobs runs against everything modern
practice believes about involving people. Take the two-scale idea as a strong lens, not a
proven law.

**Evidence check (2026 review, verdict: mixed).** The strict two-scale separation is largely an artifact of Herzberg's interview method. King (1970, Psychological Bulletin) found no support for the theory's strong versions once other methods are used, and a 2010 meta-analysis (Judge et al.) found pay does modestly raise satisfaction, so "money never motivates" is too strong. What does hold up independently is narrower: that intrinsic job content relates to motivation and performance (Fried & Ferris, 1987).
