# Great teams are about personalities, not just skills

The big idea of this article is that a great team is not just a pile of skilled individuals,
it's the right *mix of personalities*. Dave Winsborough and Tomas Chamorro-Premuzic argue that
organizations obsess over each person's skills and job title while ignoring the psychological
roles people naturally play, which is often what actually decides whether a team works.

FACT: this is a 2017 HBR.org article. (Winsborough & Chamorro-Premuzic, "Great Teams Are About
Personalities, Not Just Skills.")

## Two roles, five types

Assessment: the authors say each person plays two roles: a *functional* role (their job and
skills) and a *psychological* role (the kind of person they are). Teams get staffed on the
first and hope the second works out. They name five psychological roles, and the *balance* of
them across a team is what matters.

Innovators (drive change). Pragmatists (challenge ideas). *Five psychological roles. A great team balances them. Diagram.*

- **Results-oriented:** organize and drive the work forward.
- **Relationship-focused:** build cohesion and read the room.
- **Process followers:** keep order, detail, and rules.
- **Innovators:** push change and spot problems early.
- **Pragmatists:** hard-headed challengers who test ideas.

FACT: the authors' cautionary examples cut both ways: a finance team with no relationship-
builders and almost no results-drivers stalled; a social-work team that was 86 percent
relationship-builders was too "saccharine" to get anything hard done. As they put it, focusing
too much on *getting along* leaves little energy for *getting ahead*.

## How much to trust this

Assessment: the useful, durable idea is that team *composition* matters, not just individual
talent, and that too much harmony can be as bad as too little. Thinking about the *balance* of
roles before you staff a team is a reasonable habit.

The honest caveats are significant here. FACT: this is a short opinion piece, not original
research; it cites others' studies loosely, and both authors run personality-assessment
businesses, so "assess the whole person" is also a sales pitch. It's framed as a rebuttal to
Google's psychological-safety findings and so downplays them rather than reconciling. Assessment:
take the "balance the mix" idea as a sensible lens, and be skeptical of anything that leads
toward buying a personality test.

**Evidence check (2026 review, verdict: mixed).** The best available evidence is only mixed: meta-analyses find the personality-composition effect is small (correlations around .10 to .24, small to modest on the usual scale, and near zero in lab studies), and more personality *diversity* actually lowered performance on some traits ([Peeters 2006](https://doi.org/10.1002/per.588); [Bell 2007](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.92.3.595)). The five-role "balance the mix" idea descends from Belbin, whose measure has weak reliability and whose "balanced teams win" claim is unproven, and Google's own 180-team study found composition mattered far less than team norms like the psychological safety this article argues against. Bottom line: think about the mix of roles when you staff a team, but fix norms and working conditions first, and do not buy a personality test on the strength of this piece.
