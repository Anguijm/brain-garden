# What great managers do

The big idea of this article is a clean split between managing and leading. Marcus Buckingham
argues that great *leaders* find what everyone has in common and rally people around it,
while great *managers* do the opposite: they find what is unique about each person and build
around that. In his phrase, average managers play checkers, where every piece is the same;
great managers play chess, where every piece moves differently.

FACT: this ran in Harvard Business Review in 2005 and draws on Gallup research, including a
survey of 80,000 managers. (Buckingham, "What Great Managers Do.")

## Chess, not checkers

Assessment: the whole article turns on this contrast. A checkers player moves identical
pieces the same way. A chess player has to know how each piece moves and place it where it
does the most good. FACT: Buckingham's claim is that great managing "is about release, not
transformation," you are not trying to fix people into a standard mold, you are arranging the
work so each person's natural strengths get used.

To play chess with your people, he says you have to learn three things about each one.

## Strengths, triggers, learning style

**Strengths.** FACT: Buckingham defines a strength carefully. It is not just something a
person is good at; it is an activity that *energizes* them, that they look forward to and get
better at over time. A weakness, likewise, is not just something they are bad at; it is
something that *drains* them. Assessment: the way to find someone's strengths is to watch
them, and to ask: "what was the best day you've had at work in the last three months?" Then
dig into what they were doing and why it felt good.

**Triggers.** A strength has to be switched on, and the switch is different for everyone.
FACT: the most powerful trigger by far is recognition, but people want it from different
audiences: some from their peers, some from the boss, some from experts in their field, some
from customers. Assessment: match the recognition to the person and it lands; give it in the
wrong form and it barely registers. (One profiled manager built custom awards; another
covered the walls with a top performer's sales charts because that person thrived on public,
measured recognition.)

**Learning style.** People take on a new skill in different ways. FACT: Buckingham names
three. *Analyzers* need to take a task apart and prepare before they do it, so give them
information and time. *Doers* learn by trying, so give them a real but simple task and let
them figure it out. *Watchers* learn by seeing the whole thing done, so have them shadow a
strong performer rather than sit in a classroom.

## Managing around a weakness

Assessment: because the focus is on strengths, weaknesses get *managed around* rather than
drilled. The article offers four ways: train the gap if it is just missing skill, pair the
person with someone strong where they are weak, give them a small trick or discipline to get
past it, or rearrange the job so the weakness stops mattering.

## How much to trust this

Assessment: the individualizing idea is the valuable core, and it is real: treating people as
interchangeable is a common management failure, and "know each person's strengths and deploy
them" is sound, humane advice. The best-day question is a genuinely useful tool.

Now the caveats. FACT: Buckingham co-created the StrengthsFinder tool and this article is
adapted from his own book, so the strong "build on strengths, don't fix weaknesses" line is
a philosophy he sells, not a neutral survey of the evidence. The company stories are
persuasive single anecdotes, not proof, and the three learning styles are presented as
settled science when learning-styles theories are actually contested. Assessment: one piece
of his advice is worth flagging as *bad*: he suggests always blaming a failure on "lack of
effort, even if only partly accurate," to protect the person's confidence. Telling people
something you know is untrue is a poor habit that will cost you trust. Keep the individualizing
core; skip that bit.

**Evidence check (2026 review, verdict: mixed).** The peer-reviewed record is uneven: the "learning styles" pillar (analyzers/doers/watchers) is contradicted by the research, not merely contested — Pashler et al. (2009) found no evidence that matching instruction to a learning style helps. And while building on strengths is genuinely beneficial, independent meta-analyses put the effect on performance at only about d = 0.28 (Virga et al., 2023), far more modest than the article implies, with much of the supportive engagement data being Gallup's own correlational work (Harter et al., 2002).
