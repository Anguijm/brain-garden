# The manager's job: folklore and fact

The big idea of this article is that the tidy textbook description of a manager, someone who
"plans, organizes, coordinates, and controls," is almost useless. It tells you nothing about
what a manager actually does hour to hour. Henry Mintzberg watched real managers closely and
found the job is fast, fragmented, and deeply human, not calm and systematic. Then he offered
a better map: ten roles a manager really plays.

FACT: the four classic words come from the French industrialist Henri Fayol in 1916, and
they had gone essentially unchallenged for decades when Mintzberg took them apart. The
article first ran in HBR in 1975, won a McKinsey Award, and was reprinted as a classic in
1990. (Mintzberg, "The Manager's Job: Folklore and Fact.")

## Four myths, four facts

Mintzberg pairs each comfortable myth with what observation actually shows.

| The folklore | The fact |
|---|---|
| The manager is a reflective, systematic planner | The work is relentless, brief, varied, and interrupted; managers are action-people, not ponderers |
| A good manager has no routine duties | The job is full of ritual, ceremony, and hands-on dealing that link the unit to the outside world |
| Senior managers want tidy, aggregated reports | They prefer phone calls, meetings, and even gossip, because fresh and verbal beats formal and late |
| Management is becoming a science | The real methods stay locked in managers' heads; the work runs on judgment and intuition |

FACT: the observations behind this are vivid. In Mintzberg's own study, half of managers'
activities lasted under nine minutes. A study of factory foremen clocked 583 separate
activities in an eight-hour shift, one every 48 seconds. Managers spent the large majority of
their time (roughly two-thirds to four-fifths across studies) talking rather than reading or
writing. Assessment: the picture is a person constantly pulled between what *might* be done
and what *must* be done, closer to a cubist painting than a neat org chart.

## The ten roles a manager really plays

Instead of four verbs, Mintzberg maps ten roles in three families. They build on each other:
your formal authority creates the people-facing roles, which feed you the information, which
lets you make the decisions.

**Interpersonal roles** (they come straight from your position):

- **Figurehead**: the ceremonial head, greeting visitors, signing documents, showing up.
- **Leader**: hiring, training, motivating, and getting the best from your people.
- **Liaison**: building a web of contacts *outside* your chain of command for information
  and favors.

**Informational roles** (you become the nerve center of your unit):

- **Monitor**: constantly scanning for information, asking around, picking up signals.
- **Disseminator**: passing information down to people who would not otherwise get it.
- **Spokesperson**: sending information out to the world (your boss, the board, the public).

**Decisional roles** (you turn information into action):

- **Entrepreneur**: voluntarily starting changes and improvement projects. FACT: managers
  juggled as many as 50 of these at once, at various stages.
- **Disturbance handler**: dealing with the crises you did not choose (the strike, the lost
  contract, the emergency).
- **Resource allocator**: deciding who gets what, including your own scarcest resource, your
  time.
- **Negotiator**: committing the unit's resources in real deals, which only you have the
  authority and the information to do.

## What to do with this

Assessment: the article is less a how-to than a mirror. Its practical payoff is
self-awareness, so Mintzberg ends with a set of honest questions worth asking yourself. A few
of the sharpest:

- Where do I actually get my information, and could someone else do some of that scanning?
- Do I hoard information because sharing is inconvenient, and does that force me to do work I
  could delegate?
- Am I too superficial? Do I flit between things too fast to think, and could I build in time
  to reflect?
- Do I control my own schedule, or just react? He notes that "free time is made, not found":
  you have to turn the things you value into obligations or they never happen.

The through-line: because a manager's real work is fragmented and invisible even to the
manager, the most useful thing you can do is study your own days honestly.

## How much to trust this

Assessment: the *diagnosis* is timeless and still rings true to anyone who has run a busy
desk: the job is interruption-driven, verbal, and judgment-based, not serene and systematic.
The ten-role map remains one of the clearest descriptions of managerial work ever written.
Use it as a checklist of hats you are (or are not) wearing.

The honest caveat is age. FACT: the research is from the early 1970s, with small samples (as
few as five chief executives in the core observation study), and its picture of information
predates email, mobile phones, the web, and analytics. Assessment: the *tools* have changed
completely, but the human shape of the work, fast, social, and improvised, has changed
surprisingly little. Read the specific statistics as historical snapshots and the ten roles
as the durable part.

**Evidence check (2026 review, verdict: mixed).** Later research gives a split verdict. The busy, fragmented, verbal picture of the job has been independently replicated: Kurke and Aldrich's 1983 study in *Management Science*, pointedly titled "Mintzberg was Right!", confirmed the same variety, brevity, and fragmentation. But the tidy ten-role map is the least empirically validated part; Colin Hales's [1986 critical review of the evidence](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-6486.1986.tb00936.x) in the *Journal of Management Studies* found that the work categories used across these studies overlap and resist clean, comparable separation. A 30-year comparison by [Tengblad (2006)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-6486.2006.00651.x), in the same journal, found managers' days were less fragmented than Mintzberg's famous statistics imply, so treat the sub-nine-minute figures as an upper bound. Bottom line: build your day around the reality of fragmented, verbal work (guard reflection time on the calendar) and use the ten roles as a loose self-audit checklist, not a precise job description.
