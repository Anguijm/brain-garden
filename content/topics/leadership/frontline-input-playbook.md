# Turning frontline input into action: a build-it guide

This is the how-to companion to **[Frontline workers know how to solve your
problems](frontline-problem-solving)**, which covers why this works and the evidence behind it.
This note is just the build. It is deliberately not tied to any brand-name method, because the
brand, the belts, the binder, and the jargon are the part that decays into a certificate on the
wall. Underneath all of that is a short list of mechanics that show up everywhere frontline
input actually gets used. Here they are in plain terms, with how you will know each one is
working and how each one dies.

The whole thing in one line: make problems easy to raise, impossible to ignore, and fast to
act on, with the authority to act sitting close to the work.

## Start with one signal nobody can argue with

Assessment: do not try to open every channel at once. Pick one measure everyone already agrees
matters and instrument its full loop first. Safety is the classic choice because no one argues
against it, but a specific rework category or a recurring delay works too. "Instrument the loop"
means five things happen, every time, no exceptions: it gets reported, it gets seen by someone
with rank, it gets a root cause, it gets an action with a name on it, and if it gets ignored the
person who raised it has a way to go over the head that ignored it. The point of starting with
one signal is that it builds the habit and the trust before you widen. When people see that
raising this one thing actually moves it, they start raising the rest.

You will know it is working when reports go up before they go down. A jump in reported problems
early on is the system working, not failing. It dies the first time someone who raised a real
problem gets treated as the problem.

## Put the day's problems where everyone can see them, with an owner and a date

Assessment: the single most useful object is a board in the shop, paper is fine, listing the
problems in front of the work right now. Three columns are enough: what is wrong, who owns it,
by when. Hold a five-minute stand-up at the board once a day, same time, everyone on their feet.
The one hard rule is that nothing goes on the board without a name and a date next to it, and
nothing stays on it past its date without a reason. That rule is what separates this from a
gripe session: a complaint with no owner and no date is just noise, and the board makes that
visible to everyone standing there.

You will know it is working when the board is other people's problems getting closed, not just
yours getting added. It dies when it becomes a status report you read to the team instead of a
working list the team drives.

## Give a problem a known path upward, by name and by time

Assessment: this is the mechanic that kills the suggestion box. When the shop cannot fix
something itself, it does not vanish and it does not wait for a quarterly review. It goes to a
named person one level up who can actually decide, within a set clock, say one or two days. That
person's job is to decide or to say plainly why not, not to sit on it. Everyone should be able
to point at the wall and say who a stuck problem goes to and how long they have. The power of
this is that an idea needing a manager's budget or a constraint travels a known road instead of
dying in silence, and silence is what teaches people to stop bothering.

You will know it is working when frontline people can name where a stuck problem goes and roughly
how fast. It dies when "escalate" means "email a group and hope."

## Go stand where the work happens

Assessment: managers see the real obstacle by watching the job, not by reading a report about
it. Go to the deck, regularly and briefly, and ask one question: what makes this harder than it
needs to be? Then shut up and watch. Most of the toil in any operation is invisible from a
conference room: the walk to get a tool, the wait for a tag-out, the workaround for a form that
is wrong. You are not there to inspect people, you are there to see the friction they have
stopped mentioning because nobody ever fixed it.

You will know it is working when you come back with problems you did not know you had. It dies
when the visit becomes an audit and people clean up for your arrival.

## Give people real authority to stop and flag

Assessment: a voice is not the same as authority. The strongest version of this gives a worker
the standing to stop and pull help when something is going wrong, and a guarantee that help
actually comes, not a reprimand. It does not have to be a physical cord. It has to be a real,
known signal that a job about to go wrong, or a safety problem, gets a fast response from someone
who can act, with no penalty for the person who pulled it. Authority close to the work is the
difference between people flagging problems early and people covering them until they explode.

You will know it is working when people stop small problems early and out loud. It dies the
first time someone gets punished for stopping the line on a false alarm.

## Make the default answer "yes, try it," and close the loop fast

Assessment: the one number that keeps ideas coming is how many of them get acted on. Operations
that sustain frontline input for decades run adoption rates most managers would find shocking,
because the default answer to a small, reversible idea is "yes, try it," decided fast and close
to the work. You do not need a committee for a mechanic's idea about kit staging. For the ideas
you cannot say yes to, give the real constraint out loud, because a straight "no, and here is
why" keeps people engaged and a silent non-answer does not. Speed and honesty are the whole
game: a fast yes or a fast honest no both beat a slow maybe.

You will know it is working when people keep bringing ideas without being asked. It dies when
most ideas get a slow maybe, because a slow maybe teaches people that raising anything is a waste
of breath.

## Run every change as a small bet, not a rollout

Assessment: understand the problem before you solve it, then treat the fix as an experiment, not
a policy. One shop, one bounded change, a number you expect it to move, and a date to check. If
it works, spread it. If it does not, you learned it cheap and nobody has to defend a bad rollout.
This also protects you from the most common failure, which is copying one visible practice from
somewhere else and expecting it to work alone. The gains in the evidence come from the whole
system running together over time, not from a single trick bolted on.

You will know it is working when changes have a number and a date attached before they start. It
dies when "pilot" means "roll it out everywhere and call it a pilot."

## What kills the whole thing

Assessment, and worth reading before you start, because a dead system is worse than none. Four
things kill it. Going dark: you ask, then nothing visibly changes, and you have now taught people
that input is pointless. Punishing the messenger: the person who raised the hard truth gets
treated as the malcontent, and the honest signal goes quiet. Turning it into a program: the
moment it grows a binder, a brand, and a certificate, it stops being about the work. And no
authority or time near the deck: if the people close to the work cannot decide anything and no
manager has ten minutes for the board, none of the mechanics above can run. If you cannot fund
those, do not start.

## Start small

Assessment: pick one shop, one signal, one board, one named escalation path, and run it for a
quarter before you widen anything. Stand at the board yourself. Say a fast yes to a few small
ideas and show the before and after in person. The goal of the first quarter is not results, it
is proof to the floor that raising something actually moves it. Everything else is built on that
one belief, and you can only earn it by acting fast on small things in plain view.

## How much to trust this

Assessment: this is a distillation, not a citation. Every mechanic here is drawn from operations
that have run frontline input at scale for years and from the evidence gathered in the companion
note: the daily board and escalation from manufacturing daily-management practice, the
single-signal loop from Alcoa's safety turnaround, the high adoption rate from Toyota's decades-
long suggestion system, and the understand-first, experiment-not-rollout spine from the Harvard
Business Review case. The honest caveat is the same one that runs through all of it: the gains
show up when the whole system runs together and is sustained, not from any single mechanic used
alone. The evidence, the numbers, and the sources are in
**[Frontline workers know how to solve your problems](frontline-problem-solving)**.
