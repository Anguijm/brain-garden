# Who's got the monkey?

The big idea of this article is a vivid answer to a common puzzle: why are managers always
out of time while their people are always out of work? William Oncken and Donald Wass say it
is because the "next move" on a problem, what they call the monkey, keeps leaping off the
subordinate's back onto the manager's. Say "let me think about it" and you have just adopted
the monkey. Do that all day and you drown, while your people wait on you.

FACT: this first ran in Harvard Business Review in 1974 and was reprinted as a classic in
1999 with a commentary by Stephen Covey. (Oncken & Wass, "Management Time: Who's Got the
Monkey?")

## How the monkey jumps

Assessment: the monkey is not the whole problem, it is specifically *the next action*. When a
subordinate stops you in the hall with "we've got a problem," the monkey is on their back. The
moment you say "let me look into it and get back to you," it hops onto yours, and now, oddly,
you are working for them: they get to cheerfully ask "how's it coming?"

FACT: Oncken's arithmetic is the punchline. Four people, each allowed to toss you three
monkeys a day, gives you 60 monkeys a week, more than anyone can carry. You end up hiding
behind a closed door on the weekend to catch up while, the story goes, your staff plays golf.
Assessment: the trap is that the harder you work to catch up, the more monkeys your people
hand you, so the more you fall behind.

## Keeping the monkey where it belongs

The fix is to make sure every monkey leaves your office on the *subordinate's* back, with an
agreed next move and a time to check in.

Rungs 3 to 5 rise through: recommend then act; act, but tell me right away; and act on your own and report routinely.](img/initiative-ladder.png)
*The five levels of initiative. Push people to work at levels 3 to 5. Diagram.*

FACT: Oncken names five levels of initiative, from lowest to highest: (1) wait to be told,
(2) ask what to do, (3) recommend, then act, (4) act, but report right away, (5) act on your
own and report routinely. His rule is that a professional should never operate at levels 1 or
2, so your job is to push every person and every problem up to level 3 or higher.

His half-serious "rules for the care and feeding of monkeys" make it stick:

- **Feed them or shoot them.** Every monkey gets acted on or killed off, never left to
  starve (which just wastes your time on postmortems later).
- **Keep the population within your capacity.** People will hand you exactly as many monkeys
  as you have time to feed, so cap it.
- **Feed them by appointment, not by ambush** in the hallway.
- **Feed them face to face or by phone, never by memo** (a memo puts the next move back on
  you).
- **Every monkey gets a next move and a next check-in time.** Never let it go vague.

Assessment: Covey's 1999 commentary is worth keeping alongside it. He warns that "just give
the problem back" can sound cold and controlling. The real goal is not to dodge your people's
problems but to *grow* their ability and trust to handle their own, which takes coaching, not
just a firm shove.

## How much to trust this

Assessment: as a mental model, the monkey is genuinely useful and sticky, and most managers
recognize themselves in it instantly. Watching for "whose next move is this?" is a real,
practical discipline that protects your time (the same time your own [manager's
job](the-managers-job) says is your scarcest resource).

The honest caveats are about tone and era. FACT: the piece is from 1974, and its
boss-and-subordinate framing is dated and hierarchical. Assessment: taken too literally, "your
problem will never become my problem" can turn into an excuse not to help, which is exactly
what Covey pushes back on. Use it as a guard against *reverse delegation*, not as a licence to
abandon people. Paired with real coaching, it is one of the most practical ideas here.

**Evidence check (2026 review, verdict: mixed).** The "monkey" itself is a memorable metaphor, not a tested finding: "reverse delegation" has essentially no peer-reviewed research behind it, and the closest evidence (meta-analyses of "empowering leadership") shows only a modest link to actual performance (roughly ρ=.31 in Kim et al., 2018). Pushing work back down is also double-edged: Cheong et al. (2016) found it can raise subordinates' stress and hurt performance, so treat this as a useful time-management prompt, not proof that offloading every problem improves outcomes.
