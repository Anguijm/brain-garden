# The feedback fallacy

The big idea of this article is that the modern gospel of feedback, that people grow best
when you frequently and candidly tell them what they are doing wrong, is mostly backwards.
Marcus Buckingham and Ashley Goodall argue that telling someone your judgment of their
performance does not help them improve, and piling on criticism actively gets in the way.
People get better when someone who knows them points at what is *working*.

FACT: this ran in Harvard Business Review in 2019 and pushes back directly on the
"radical transparency" and harsh-feedback cultures made famous by companies like Bridgewater
and Netflix. (Buckingham & Goodall, "The Feedback Fallacy.")

## Three beliefs about feedback that don't hold up

The article takes apart three assumptions underneath most feedback advice.

![Diagram: three rows, each pairing a common belief with the correction. Row one, the belief that other people see your weaknesses clearly, versus the fact that raters are unreliable and more than half of any rating reflects the rater, not you. Row two, the belief that learning means filling in gaps, versus the fact that people grow most in their areas of strength and criticism triggers a threat response. Row three, the belief that excellence is universal and can be copied, versus the fact that excellence is personal and can't be reverse-engineered from failure.](img/feedback-fallacy.png)
*The three feedback myths, and what the authors say is actually true. Diagram.*

**Myth 1: other people are a reliable source of truth about you.** FACT: forty years of
research on ratings shows the opposite. When you rate someone on a quality like "business
acumen," more than half of your score reflects *you*, the rater, not them. This is called
the idiosyncratic rater effect, and no training fixes it. Assessment: so the honest version
of feedback is not "here is where you stand," which you cannot actually know, but "here is
how you landed *with me*." You are a reliable source of your own reactions, and nothing else.

**Myth 2: learning means filling in what's missing.** FACT: the authors argue people grow
most where they are already strong, not where they are weak, and they cite brain research
that criticism triggers a "fight or flight" response that narrows thinking, while attention
to what is going well opens it up. Assessment: pointing at someone's gaps does not teach
them; it mostly makes them defensive.

**Myth 3: excellence is universal, so you can define it and transfer it.** FACT: excellence
is personal and a little mysterious. The two best free-throw shooters in basketball history
used completely different techniques (one shot underhand). And studying failure only teaches
you about failure, not about what great looks like. Assessment: a generic "ideal" model,
copied onto everyone, produces adequacy, not excellence.

## What to do instead

Assessment: the replacement is not "never say anything hard." It is to change what you pay
attention to and how you talk about it.

- **Spotlight outcomes as they happen.** When something genuinely works (a customer relaxes,
  a tense meeting turns), stop and say "*That*. Yes, that." You are helping the person notice
  a pattern already in them so they can repeat it.
- **Describe your reaction, don't render a verdict.** Instead of "that presentation was
  weak," try "here is where you lost me." Instead of "good job," try "here are three things
  that worked for me, what were you thinking when you did them?"
- **When someone wants help, look at what already works.** Ask "what are three things working
  for you right now?", then "when you solved something like this before, what did you do?"
  Assume they hold the answer and help them see it.

FACT: one caveat the authors grant, and it matters: plain instruction still helps where the
right steps are genuinely known in advance, like a checklist or how to give an injection.
The argument is against *evaluative* feedback, not against teaching a clear procedure.

## How much to trust this

Assessment: the strongest, best-supported piece is the rater problem. That "we are
unreliable judges of each other" rests on a large, real body of measurement research, and it
is a genuinely useful humility check: be skeptical of confident ratings of people, including
your own. That idea connects straight to this garden's habit of not trusting a confident
claim just because it sounds authoritative.

Be more careful with the rest. FACT: both authors are leaders of the "strengths" movement
(Buckingham co-created the StrengthsFinder tool), and this article previews their book, so
the one-sided "strengths, never weaknesses" framing doubles as marketing. The neuroscience
(you grow most where you are strongest) is a strong, arguably oversimplified reading of the
brain, and the famous fMRI study is described without a citation. Assessment: keep the rater
humility and the "spotlight what works" habit, which are valuable, but do not swing all the
way to "never correct anyone." The authors themselves don't go that far.
