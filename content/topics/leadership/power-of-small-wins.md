# The power of small wins

The big idea of this article is that the most powerful everyday motivator is not recognition,
incentives, or pressure, it is simply *making progress* in work that matters. Teresa Amabile
and Steven Kramer call it the "progress principle," and their striking finding is that most
managers have no idea it is the biggest lever they hold.

FACT: this ran in Harvard Business Review in 2011, based on a large daily-diary study.
(Amabile & Kramer, "The Power of Small Wins.")

## The progress principle

Assessment: the argument runs through what the authors call **inner work life**, the mix of
emotions, motivation, and perceptions a person carries through a workday. Good inner work
life makes people more creative, productive, and committed. And the single biggest thing that
lifts it is the feeling of forward movement, even a small step.

FACT: in a study of 238 people across 26 project teams (nearly 12,000 daily diary entries),
progress showed up on 76 percent of people's *best* days, while setbacks dominated their
*worst* days. Even minor wins mattered: over a quarter of the events that were trivial to the
project had a major effect on how people *felt*.

FACT: yet when 669 managers ranked five tools for motivating people, only 5 percent put
"support for progress" first. Most ranked it dead last, and put recognition on top.
Assessment: managers are aiming at the wrong lever.

## What fuels progress, and what blocks it

Assessment: the authors sort a manager's daily actions into four kinds, two that help and two
that hurt.

Nourishers (help the person: respect, encouragement). Inhibitors (hurt the work: interference, no resources). Toxins (hurt the person: disrespect, discouragement).](img/catalysts-nourishers.png)
*What fuels daily progress and inner work life, and what poisons it. Diagram.*

- **Catalysts** support the *work*: clear goals, autonomy, enough time and resources, help
  when asked, and learning from problems instead of punishing them.
- **Nourishers** support the *person*: respect, recognition, encouragement, a sense of
  belonging.
- **Inhibitors** and **toxins** are their opposites: interference and starved resources on
  the work side, disrespect and discouragement on the person side.

FACT: one more crucial finding, setbacks hit harder than wins help. A bad event has a bigger
negative effect on inner work life than a good event has a positive one. Assessment: so
clearing obstacles and avoiding toxic moments matters even more than celebrating.

## What to do with this

Assessment: the practical program is small and daily. Make sure people see how their work
matters. Then, each day, add catalysts and nourishers and clear away inhibitors and toxins.
The authors suggest ending the day by noting one or two wins and setbacks and planning
tomorrow's single most helpful move. The golden rule: "check in" (act as a resource) rather
than "check up" (micromanage).

## How much to trust this

Assessment: the core idea, that steady progress in meaningful work is the deepest everyday
motivator, is one of the more useful and humane ideas in this library, and the "clear the
path, then mark small wins" habit is easy to act on. It fits Herzberg's point that real drive
comes from the work itself, and adds the daily mechanics Herzberg lacked.

The honest caveats are modest. FACT: the authors are explicit that their diary data show
*correlation*, not proof of cause, and the sample is 238 people in creative knowledge-work
teams, so it may not transfer cleanly to routine, low-autonomy jobs (they admit progress feels
"elusive" in dull work). Assessment: treat the study figures as the reported findings and the
manager advice as well-grounded interpretation, not tested law. The direction is very
believable.

**Evidence check (2026 review, verdict: mixed).** Independent research backs the direction but not the superlative: a [2015 meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10902-013-9493-0) (Klug & Maier, 85 studies in *Journal of Happiness Studies*) finds successful goal striving correlates with well-being only moderately (ρ≈.43), and a [40-year meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0035661) (Cerasoli et al., 2014, *Psychological Bulletin*) shows intrinsic motivation and extrinsic incentives *jointly* drive performance, so "progress is the single biggest lever and recognition/incentives are the wrong target" is an overstatement, not a settled ranking. The specific diary figures come from one unreplicated correlational study, though the separate "setbacks outweigh wins" point is well established (Baumeister et al., 2001, "Bad Is Stronger Than Good", *Review of General Psychology*).
