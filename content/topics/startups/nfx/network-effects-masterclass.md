# The Network Effects Masterclass, episode by episode

Season one of NFX's free founder course is about network effects: the way some products get more valuable to each
user as more people use them. Plain English first: the firm argues this is the main source of lasting protection for
digital businesses, and that "does it have network effects, yes or no" is too crude a question. The ten episodes, on
[nfx.com](https://www.nfx.com/masterclass/network-effects) behind a free login, were read from their transcripts.

## 1. Why network effects are mission critical (13 minutes)
FACT: [James Currier says](https://www.nfx.com/masterclass/network-effects/network-effects-mission-critical) that if a
startup will take all your energy, build one with network effects, since they are the main source of lasting
defensibility, and defines the core idea as each new user making the product more valuable for every other user.

## 2. The underlying properties of network theory (27 minutes)
FACT: [Currier says](https://www.nfx.com/masterclass/network-effects/network-theory-properties) networks differ in
structure, density, identity, how alike the participants are and how value grows, and each difference changes how you
build. He insists on keeping network effects (retention and defensibility) apart from viral effects (free customer
acquisition).

## 3. Mapping out the 16 network effects (23 minutes)
FACT: [Currier arranges](https://www.nfx.com/masterclass/network-effects/mapping-the-16-network-effects) 16 types on
a map from strongest at the center to weakest at the outer edge. In his order: physical (phone wires, cable),
protocol (a published standard such as email or Bitcoin), personal utility (a messaging app people cannot leave),
personal (a friend graph), market network (all sides of a deal on one platform), two-sided marketplace, platform (a
development environment others build on), asymptotic marketplace (value grows fast, then flattens), expertise
(workers learn a tool and employers choose the tool they know), data, tech performance, language (the brand becomes the
verb), belief, tribal, bandwagon, and hub and spoke (a central feed that occasionally amplifies a winner). The advice
is to build one effect on a small cluster of users, then deliberately layer a second on top.

## 4. The four pillars of defensibility (17 minutes)
FACT: [Pete Flint names](https://www.nfx.com/masterclass/network-effects/4-pillars-of-defensibility) four digital
defensibilities: network effects, brand, embedding (the product woven into the customer's workflow) and scale, with
intellectual property as an extra in science and biology. Early-stage startups should lead with network effects and add
the others over time. (The course page's blurb lists a slightly different set.)

## 5. Network bonding theory (21 minutes)
FACT: [Currier argues](https://www.nfx.com/masterclass/network-effects/network-bonding-theory) that software is getting
easy, so the hard part is bonding people to your network, and that a company is itself a network. Co-founders, early
employees, investors, customers and press are all paid to join, implicitly and on a declining curve. Founders should make
that explicit, measure which people matter most, and pay in the right currency, because rival networks will try to
poach them.

## 6. Case studies (21 minutes)
FACT: [Currier and Flint use the 16-type map](https://www.nfx.com/masterclass/network-effects/network-effects-case-studies)
on Trulia, Facebook, Bitcoin and Uber, and say real companies win by stacking effects over time, with one reinforcing
the next. The map is used to explain why Facebook is stronger, and Uber weaker, than their size suggests. (The course
page promises a biotech case that is in episode 10 instead.)

## 7. Network problems demand network solutions (16 minutes)
FACT: [Currier says](https://www.nfx.com/masterclass/network-effects/new-social-contract) networks always produce
power laws, where a few participants get most of the wealth, status and attention, and the internet makes the curve
steeper and more visible. A founder of a network-effect business will create such a curve among its users and workers, so
should design a new social contract with them.

## 8. Your life is driven by network effects (15 minutes)
FACT: [Currier applies](https://www.nfx.com/masterclass/network-effects/your-life-is-driven-by-network-effects) the
same math to personal life: the information your network puts in front of you drives your decisions, so choose your
networks consciously at a handful of life crossroads. It is a personal episode, not a startup one.

## 9. Gaming (17 minutes)
FACT: [Gigi Levy-Weiss says](https://www.nfx.com/masterclass/network-effects/network-effects-in-gaming) the top games
and game platforms stack several network effects, which is why leaders stay on the charts for 10 to 15 years, and uses
Steam to show how a plain marketplace becomes nearly unassailable by adding layers.

## 10. TechBio (9 minutes)
FACT: [Omri Amirav-Drory says](https://www.nfx.com/masterclass/network-effects/network-effects-in-techbio) that in
biology defensibility starts with owning intellectual property and the freedom to operate, not users. Once that exists,
a platform technology that spawns many products gains a platform network effect, and regulatory approval, usually seen
as a burden, becomes a moat.

## Where the course disagrees with itself

Assessment: episode 7 argues for letting participants leave and use rivals, while episodes 2 and 5 treat that
multi-homing as a weakness a rival can attack. The course does not reconcile them. The recurring practical advice is
to attack where an incumbent's network is weak (it has stopped growing in value, users use several rivals at once, it
takes a high cut, quality has declined) and to stack more effects than the rival.
