# Wedges, timing and idea screens

Three tools from NFX for deciding whether an idea has somewhere to start and whether the moment is right. A "wedge" is
a narrow starting market that gives an early unfair advantage. The timing tests ask why the idea works now and not
five years ago. The idea screens come from one long talk to students.

## Twelve wedges

FACT: in [NFX's wedge video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RzX3dqZx89o), the speaker says almost every large company
started with an initial "killer wedge": a high-velocity market where a simple product works, sometimes a niche within a
niche. The twelve, as listed there:

1. Viral network: a dense, highly connected group that spreads a product fast. College campuses are the classic case,
   and Facebook started at Harvard.
2. Instant liquidity: giving money or shortening cash cycles, carefully underwritten, which also generates data.
3. Rapid feedback: a minimum viable product used by many customers who tolerate change, so you can improve and ship again.
4. Complementary customer: start with a segment next to an incumbent's, such as selling into markets it has not
   served. Booking.com and Airtable are named.
5. 10x return on investment: a product about ten times better than the nearest alternative, a must-have rather than a
   nice-to-have. Starlink for rural internet and Robinhood for trading are named.
6. Underserved needs: a small uncompetitive niche where you build a delightful experience, then expand. Airbnb is the
   classic example.
7. Premium launch: a visible, high-margin product for wealthy buyers that funds a mass-market version. Tesla's
   Roadster is the example.
8. Compliance: simplify something the customer cannot afford to get wrong, such as taxes, legal or security.
9. Repurposed asset: earn money from something sitting idle, such as clothes in a closet or spare time. Poshmark,
   TaskRabbit, DoorDash and Amazon Web Services are named.
10. Frictionless integration: remove the friction from a complex process. Plaid is named.
11. Status: start where reputation attracts others. Facebook at Harvard, Snapchat at Stanford and LinkedIn are named.
12. Transparency: give access to information people are curious about, then work out how to earn from it.

Assessment: the wedge names are the useful part. The captions of this video mix in clips of other speakers and some
examples are unclear, so use the list as a menu of questions, not a catalog of facts about those companies.

## Timing: three things that have to meet

FACT: in [a talk at Stanford's business school](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hwnh-HtByaY), Pete Flint says good
timing has three characteristics, ideally all three and at least two. The talk does not give the three a name.
- An enabling technology that was not around before (today mostly AI).
- An economic reason, such as something expensive becoming cheap or capital becoming scarce.
- Cultural acceptance, such as people trusting driver ratings in place of a taxi brand.
He applies this to Zoom, Uber and Airbnb. He also says being too early is almost the same as being wrong.

FACT: in [the idea talk to Stanford students](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=My02IkUg7Ug), James Currier says real
timing comes from rapid technology platform shifts that make something newly possible, not from slow cultural shifts,
which take a decade and are visible to every competitor. For regulation he says to get in after a rule changes, not
before, and that a technology found in a lab without a market rarely works unless it is a platform.

## The technology-window cycle

FACT: in [an NFX video on technology windows](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NtBIsBhrSnM), the speaker says every
technology has a window in which its well-known companies get created, and that the cycle has six stages: a hobbyist
moment, a status moment, knowledge spreading, competition rushing in, incumbents arriving with network effects or scale,
and the window closing, at which point building an important company is about "90 percent harder". The only dated
example given is railroads, roughly 1830 to 1870 in the United States. He places AI in the knowledge-spreading and
competition stages and says the window starts closing at some point in the next 4 to 12 years.

Assessment: he gives no method for telling which stage a market is in, so the cycle is better as a question ("who has
already arrived, and with what advantage?") than as a forecast.

## Screens from the idea talk

FACT: in the same Stanford talk, Currier gives a rough split of ideas: about 65 percent mediocre, about 25 percent
uncertain, about 10 percent "fertile soil", meaning a good team that works hard for two years should sell for \$10
million or more. He says this is his impression, not data. His screens for fertile soil are "simple, scalable and
similar" (copying a lot, not all), a network effect at the core, a list of every risk, and arithmetic on price times
frequency times customers. His own gaming example: a game with 100 million users at four cents a day worked for a rival;
his with 50,000 users at nearly four dollars a day also worked; one that fell outside both boundaries did not.
