# Copy a lot, but not all

The usual worry about a business idea is "someone is already doing this." NFX's partners give several answers, and
they do not fully agree. Read together they make a useful set of challenges to put to any idea: how much is borrowed,
what exactly is different, and why the leader cannot simply follow.

## Answer 1: borrow a working model and change two or three things

FACT: [James Currier told Stanford students](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=My02IkUg7Ug) that a totally new idea
draws praise but rarely sales, because people probably do not need something that has no precedent, and that a pure
clone draws legal action and a bad reputation. The target he names is "copy a lot, but not all": take something already
working and make two or three obvious changes.

FACT: in the same talk he argues that crowded markets are safer than empty ones. Empty markets carry more demand
risk, and he says the people who enter crowded ones tend to do better. His line, verbatim: "Get in the red ocean, get
some revenue, get some customers." He tells a portfolio company that had failed twice in empty markets to pick a
business it admires and add new energy, because it already knows someone will buy it, how to price it and how to sell it.

FACT: his examples in that talk are Lending Club copying Prosper while changing two things (it bundled the loans and
spoke to a regulator before launch), Google copying InfoSeek, Airbnb copying VRBO, and Facebook copying Friendster.
He adds that in several of these the real edge was the team rather than the product.

## Answer 2: do not chase differentiation at the start

FACT: in [the Speed Talk](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2tEETESsz4s), Currier says not to worry about being unique
early. Copy what works and you will become unique through your own customers and circumstances. His proof is a story
about a rival who built the same product from the same code: the two ended up in very different places. He also says
the winners among fast followers were often not the inventors, citing Lyft over Sidecar and Uber over Lyft.

Assessment: this is the loosest of the answers and the one most open to abuse. It works when speed to the market is the
scarce thing. It does not help when the incumbent can match your speed.

## Answer 3: use a business model the leader cannot copy

FACT: in [a conversation with Jeff Lawson](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=U_eQK9UR8ao), strategy author Hamilton
Helmer describes "counter-positioning": a challenger adopts a better business model that the incumbent cannot copy
without seriously damaging its own business. He calls it his favorite of his seven powers, and gives In-N-Out against
McDonald's, the iPhone against Nokia, and a traditional carmaker facing Tesla.

FACT: in the same conversation Helmer says network effects, though they help a product take off, are much rarer as a
lasting source of power, because more than one company can benefit from the same effect (he uses Lyft alongside Uber).
Helmer's seven powers are network economies, scale economies, counter-positioning, switching costs, branding,
cornered resource and process power. He says product-market fit is not enough on its own to last.

## Answer 4: attack the incumbent's revenue, and be 100 times better

FACT: in [a talk at Stanford's business school](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hwnh-HtByaY), Pete Flint says AI
helps both incumbents and challengers. His five openings for challengers are: exploit a conflict with the incumbent's
business model (his example: answer engines against a search business paid by clicks); pick slow, encumbered industries
where speed is the advantage; be roughly 100 times better, not 10 percent better; use AI on large piles of unstructured
data; and find a breakthrough user experience. He also says disruptors often start as something that looks like a toy.

## Answer 5: be the only one, not the best one

FACT: in [a conversation with technology writer Kevin Kelly](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vincGfoF8FA), Kelly says
competing to be the best at a defined game is zero-sum, while work that nobody has a name for has no category rival.
If people struggle to say what you do, he says, you may be on the right path. He adds that finding the particular mix
of talents that makes this possible usually takes years of a meandering career.

## Answer 6: enter an occupied space and win on one loop

FACT: in [a talk with Selina Tobaccowala](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=f5g_ny3QyZo), the founder behind Evite and
later a fitness startup describes researching people who had tried an idea before, since most will talk for fifteen to
thirty minutes, and understanding why they failed. She credits Evite's growth to testing one viral loop, turning an
invitee into a creator, and says to build testing into the product from the start.

## Where they disagree

Assessment: three tensions are worth putting to a founder directly.
- Answers 1 and 2 say copy first; answers 3, 4 and 5 say differentiate by structure from day one. The honest question
  is which one the idea has the time and money for.
- Answer 1 prefers a crowded market; answer 5 says to leave a defined category. They fit only if "crowded" means a
  proven demand and "the only" means a combination of strengths the leader does not have.
- Answer 3 tests whether the leader can follow. Answers 1 and 2 assume that it will, and rely on speed instead.
