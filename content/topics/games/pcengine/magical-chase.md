# Magical Chase (TurboGrafx-16)

**Developer:** Quest | **Publisher:** Palsoft / Turbo Technologies | **Year:** 1991

---

## Introduction

Magical Chase is a horizontally-scrolling "cute-em-up" — a shooter with pastel art, a young witch on a broomstick, and none of the grimness the genre usually carries. Ripple, an apprentice witch, has accidentally released six demons from a book and must put them back.

Two things make it notable. It plays beautifully: the option system is unusually flexible, the difficulty is well judged, and Quest (later of *Tactics Ogre*) clearly cared. And it is **one of the most expensive games ever released on the system** — the North American print run was tiny and physical copies routinely sell for four figures. This is a game most people can only realistically play through emulation, which is exactly the use case.

---

## Controls

- **D-Pad:** Move.
- **Button II:** Shoot. Hold for continuous fire.
- **Button I:** Rotate your options (the two floating stars).
- **Run:** Pause.

### The option system, which is the game

Two star-shaped options orbit Ripple. **Button I locks or rotates their position**, letting you aim your supporting fire independently of your movement.

That means you can fly left while shooting right, or park your options above you while dodging below. Nearly every difficult section is a positioning problem solved by placing the options correctly, and players who never touch Button I find the game far harder than it is.

---

## Shop and upgrades

Between stages you spend collected crystals in a shop. This is a real decision, not a formality.

- **Shot upgrades** — increase your main fire's power and spread.
- **Option upgrades** — make the stars stronger and change their fire pattern.
- **Bombs** — screen-clearing, limited.
- **Extra lives** — expensive and usually worth it.
- **Shields** — one-hit protection.

**Buy option upgrades early.** The stars end up doing much of your damage, and upgrading them compounds with the positioning skill the game is teaching.

---

## Stage notes

Five stages plus the finale, each ending in a boss.

**Stage 1** — a gentle introduction across the countryside. Learn to rotate the options here where it costs nothing.

**Stage 2** — the cave. Tighter spaces, terrain that kills on contact. Options parked ahead of you clear the path.

**Stage 3** — vertical movement and enemies from above and below. Rotating options continuously is the intended solution.

**Stage 4** — the difficulty step. Dense enemy waves and a boss with a genuinely tricky pattern.

**Stage 5 and the finale** — everything at once, and the demons returned to the book.

---

## Strategy

**Rotate constantly.** The instinct is to lock the options forward and treat them as extra guns. The game is designed around moving them; most bosses have a safe position that only works if your fire is pointing somewhere your body is not.

**Do not hoard crystals.** Saving for a big purchase across several shops usually leaves you under-powered for the stage in between. Buy incrementally.

**Learn the boss openings.** Each boss telegraphs, and each has a window where sustained fire is free. The fights are short once you know them.

**It is a fair game.** Unlike much of the genre, Magical Chase rarely kills you with something unavoidable. If a death felt cheap, there was usually an option position that would have prevented it.

## Emulation Notes

Runs perfectly on Batocera's PC Engine core, and the pastel art is one of the best showcases for integer scaling at 4K on this box — the colour work is the point of the game's presentation.

**Rewind is enabled** on this system and is well suited here, since progress carries between stages through the shop economy and a late death is expensive.

Worth repeating the practical note: original North American cartridges are among the rarest and most valuable on any system. This is emulation doing the thing emulation is for.
