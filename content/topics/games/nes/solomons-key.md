# Solomon's Key (NES)

**Developer:** Tecmo | **Publisher:** Tecmo | **Year:** 1987

---

## Introduction

Solomon's Key is a puzzle-platformer with one mechanic and fifty rooms built out of it: you can create and destroy a single block of stone in the tile directly diagonal-up from where you face. That is the entire toolkit. Everything else — reaching the key, reaching the door, avoiding the demons, collecting the hidden items — is a consequence of where you choose to put blocks.

It is also genuinely hard, in the way arcade ports of 1986 were hard. There is a timer, the fireball that lights your way burns down, and running out of either kills you.

The structure of each room is fixed: find the key, then reach the door. Everything else is optional.

---

## Controls

- **D-Pad:** Move and face. Which way you face determines where blocks appear.
- **A:** Create or destroy a block in the tile diagonally up-and-forward from Dana.
- **B:** Cast a fireball, if you have collected the spell. Kills demons.
- **Up:** Climb, when standing under a block you can mount.

### The block rule, precisely

Facing right, pressing A affects the tile **one right and one up** from Dana's head. Facing left, one left and one up. If that tile is empty, a block appears. If it holds a destructible block, it vanishes.

That means you build staircases by alternating: make a block, climb onto it, make the next. And it means you can trap yourself permanently by walling off the only route — the game will happily let you do this, and the timer is the only thing that ends the room.

---

## What is in a room

**The key** — must be collected before the door opens.

**The door** — the exit, in a fixed position.

**Fairies** — extra points and, hidden in some rooms, extra lives.

**Bells** — summon a fairy when collected.

**Jars and bottles** — points, extra time, and occasionally an extra life.

**Hidden items** — many rooms contain items revealed only by creating or destroying a block in a specific empty tile. There is no visual clue. This is the game's least fair aspect and the reason maps exist.

**Demons** — Panel Monsters, Ghosts, Gargoyles. Most cannot be killed without the fireball spell; all can be crushed by creating a block in the tile they occupy, which is the reliable answer.

---

## Strategy

**Crushing beats fighting.** Creating a block on top of an enemy kills it outright. This works on nearly everything and costs nothing but position. Learning to time it against a moving demon is more useful than the fireball.

**The timer is the real enemy.** Rooms are not usually hard to solve; they are hard to solve quickly. Hesitating over a staircase costs seconds you need later. On a first run through a room, expect to die to the clock and treat it as reconnaissance.

**Build up, not across.** Dana cannot jump far. Vertical staircases are the standard traversal, and most rooms are solved by building a column and walking off the top.

**Do not destroy your own route.** Because A both creates and destroys, facing the wrong way at the wrong moment removes the block you are about to stand on. When in doubt, stop, face deliberately, then press.

**Fifty rooms, plus hidden ones.** Constellation rooms are reached by finding hidden items in specific stages; they are not required to finish.

## Emulation Notes

Runs perfectly on FCEUmm. This is one of the games on this box where **rewind** is most valuable: a single mistimed block press can make a room unsolvable, and without rewind the only remedy is to stand there and wait for the timer to kill you. Being able to unwind three seconds is the difference between a puzzle game and a memory test.

Save states are worth using between rooms. The original has no continues past a point, and the difficulty was calibrated for an arcade cabinet's coin slot rather than a living room.
