# Tecmo Super Bowl (NES)

**Developer:** Tecmo | **Publisher:** Tecmo | **Year:** 1991

---

## Introduction

Tecmo Super Bowl arrived in August 1991 and immediately became the standard by which every other sports video game was judged for the next decade. The original Tecmo Bowl (1989) had used fictional teams with real players. This sequel went further: full 28-team NFL licensing, real player names, real playbooks drawn from each team's actual 1990 season tendencies, and a full Season Mode that tracked statistics, injuries, and standings across a simulated 16-game schedule.

The game captured the NFL at a specific moment — the twilight of the Montana era, the rise of Jim Kelly's no-huddle offense, the Bo Jackson phenomenon at its peak, and the Giants dynasty anchored by Lawrence Taylor. Players who lived through that era will recognize everything. Players who didn't will discover why certain names still carry weight thirty-five years later.

Tecmo Super Bowl is not a simulation. Blocking assignments are simplified, routes are predetermined, and the gap between a great player and a weak one is enormous — far larger than in real football. Bo Jackson on a clear path to the end zone does not get tackled. Jerry Rice with a step on any corner does not get covered. That imbalance is half the game's appeal. Learning which players are truly broken, and how to put them in positions to be broken, is most of what separates good Tecmo players from great ones.

---

## Game Modes

**Pre-Season:** Exhibition games. Any teams, no consequences, no statistics tracked. Use this to learn matchups before committing to Season Mode.

**Season Mode:** A full 16-game NFL season with all 28 teams. You control one team; the CPU simulates all other games. Stats accumulate across the season — rushing yards, passing yards, touchdowns, sacks, interceptions. After 16 weeks, the top teams from each conference enter the playoffs. Win the playoffs and you reach the Super Bowl. This is the main mode. It takes several hours to complete a full season.

**Pro Bowl:** Play as the NFC or AFC All-Stars. Good for experimenting with elite players outside their actual teams, but stats don't carry weight the same way.

**Super Bowl:** Jump directly to the championship. Useful for practicing the end-game pressure or testing specific team matchups without a full season.

## Controls

### Offense (before snap)
- **A:** Snap the ball / select play
- **B:** No function pre-snap
- **D-Pad:** Navigate play selection screen

### Offense (after snap — running plays)
- **D-Pad:** Control the ball carrier
- **A:** Speed burst — tap rapidly to break arm tackles and shed defenders. This is not a sprint button in the traditional sense; it sends break-tackle inputs that the game resolves probabilistically based on your player's stats versus the tackler's. Mashing A when you're grabbed is worth doing on every single carry.
- **B:** No function while running (no stiff-arm, no spin)

### Offense (after snap — passing plays)
- **D-Pad:** Move the quarterback while in the pocket
- **A:** Throw — targets the flashing receiver (the one the game has designated open). You cannot freely choose your target. The game cycles through receivers and highlights the one it considers open; you throw to that receiver. Veteran players learn to read which receiver flashes and time their throw to the down-field option rather than the checkdown.
- **B:** No function during passing plays

### Defense (before snap)
- **A:** Select defensive play
- **D-Pad:** Navigate play selection

### Defense (after snap)
- **D-Pad:** Control your selected defender
- **A:** Dive tackle — lunge at the ball carrier
- **B:** No function

### Kicking
- **A (first press):** Begin kick power meter
- **A (second press):** Lock in power at the meter's current position
- The meter oscillates; press at or near the top for maximum distance. Punts and kickoffs use the same mechanic. Field goals add a direction component — the meter bouncing left or right affects accuracy.

## Player Attributes

Every player in Tecmo Super Bowl has five hidden attributes that govern how they perform. You never see these numbers in-game, but they explain everything you observe.

**Running Speed (RS):** How quickly the player reaches top speed from a standing start. High values mean you accelerate out of the backfield fast.

**Maximum Speed (MS):** Your player's top-end pace in the open field. This is the number that matters most for skill players. Bo Jackson has the highest Maximum Speed of any rusher in the game: 75 out of 75. Jerry Rice has 69 as a receiver — highest among wideouts.

**Hitting Power (HP):** Determines how hard the player hits in blocking and tackling situations. Very high Hitting Power (88+) allows linemen to create lanes; very high defensive HP allows players like Lawrence Taylor to shed blocks almost instantly. Christian Okoye (Chiefs) has the maximum HP among running backs at 94 — he will run over defenders who should stop him.

**Ball Control (BC):** For offensive skill players, this governs how often they fumble under contact and how cleanly they catch passes thrown their way. Both Bo Jackson and Jerry Rice have Ball Control of 81 — the maximum. They almost never fumble or drop catchable balls.

**Reception (REC):** Specific to pass catchers. Determines how reliably they convert the catch animation into a completion. Jerry Rice has Reception 81. A player with Reception 19 will drop catchable balls regularly.

The gap between elite players and average ones is not marginal. A team with Jerry Rice (BC 81, REC 81, MS 69) at receiver and a team with a generic WR2 (BC 44, REC 44, MS 50) at receiver are not playing the same position.

## Team Rankings

Tecmo Super Bowl has 28 teams covering the full 1990 NFL season rosters. Here is how they stack up.

### Tier 1 — Elite

**San Francisco 49ers**
Joe Montana at quarterback (the best passing rating in the game), Jerry Rice at receiver (the best receiver in the game, period), and a balanced offensive line. The 49ers were widely considered for banning in competitive play among friends because the Montana-to-Rice connection functions as a cheat code. Montana reads the field accurately on passing plays and Rice catches everything thrown anywhere near him. The 49ers' weakness is a defense that lacks a truly dominant individual — their secondary and linebacker corps are strong but not the transcendent force their offense is. If you want to win, play the 49ers. If you want to be invited back, maybe don't.

**New York Giants**
Lawrence Taylor alone would put this team on the list. He is the best defensive player in the game. Phil Simms at quarterback is serviceable, Ottis Anderson provides a competent ground game, and Mark Bavaro is a capable tight end — but you are here for the defense. Taylor's Hitting Power is maximized and his Maximum Speed is the highest of any linebacker in the game. On defense, you control Taylor directly. You will wreck opposing backfields in ways that make runs unviable. Many experienced players consider the Giants the single best team in the game precisely because Taylor transforms every defensive snap.

**Buffalo Bills**
The most balanced team. Jim Kelly runs the no-huddle offense effectively, Thurman Thomas is a legitimate dual-threat back (good rushing stats and reliable as a receiver), Andre Reed gives you a top-five receiver, and Bruce Smith anchors a pass rush that terrorizes CPU offenses. The Bills don't have a single player as individually broken as Rice or Jackson, but they have four legitimately elite players on the same roster. In a long season, that depth matters.

### Tier 2 — Strong

**Los Angeles Raiders**
Bo Jackson. That is the entire case. Jackson's Maximum Speed is capped at the highest value in the game, his Ball Control is 81, and his Hitting Power is the highest among runners. When Jackson reaches the second level with a clear path, he will score. Defenders who should catch him don't. On paper, he is not the "best" running back by composite rating — his Receiving stat is low, limiting his pass-catching value. But as a pure ball carrier on running plays, nothing in the game stops him the way the numbers suggest he should be stopped. The Raiders' passing game is weak; their defense is average. You have one plan and it either works or it doesn't.

**Cincinnati Bengals**
Boomer Esiason is among the most accurate passers in the game. James Brooks is a capable runner. David Fulcher is the top-rated safety and makes the Bengals' defense legitimately difficult to pass against. The Bengals rank in most tier lists just below the Bills because they lack a receiver as dominant as Reed or Rice, but they are a legitimate threat in Season Mode.

**Houston Oilers**
Warren Moon is the second-best quarterback in the game. Drew Hill is the second-best receiver. The Oilers' run defense is their weak point, but if you let them pass freely they will score. Avoid playing man coverage against Moon; his accuracy and Hill's speed exploit it.

**Chicago Bears**
Mike Singletary and Richard Dent anchor a defense that can shut down most offenses when you play their run-stopping alignments. Neal Anderson provides a solid ground game. The Bears are not an elite offense but their defense makes them competitive in every game.

### Tier 3 — Competitive

Minnesota Vikings, Kansas City Chiefs (Christian Okoye is a physical freak at running back), Philadelphia Eagles (Reggie White is the best defensive end in the game), Washington Redskins, Miami Dolphins.

### Tier 4 — Below Average

Detroit Lions are here despite Barry Sanders — he is the second-best running back in the game, but the Lions' supporting cast in 1991 is so thin that the team cannot hold up in a 16-game season.

### Tier 5 — Avoid

Atlanta Falcons, Tampa Bay Buccaneers, New England Patriots, Indianapolis Colts. These teams lack the individual talent to compete at the highest level. Play them in Pre-Season to learn their weaknesses; do not play a Season Mode with them unless you want a challenge run.

## Offensive Strategy

### The Four Offensive Plays

Every team has exactly four offensive plays: two running plays and two passing plays. The exact play types vary by team, but the categories are consistent.

**Run Left / Run Right:** Sweeps around the outside. Your running back takes the handoff and attacks the edge. These are your home-run plays — when they work, they go the distance. When they fail, they lose two yards. The success rate depends heavily on your runner's Maximum Speed and the speed of the defensive end the CPU assigns to pursue.

**Up the Middle:** A dive into the interior. Lower ceiling than the sweeps (rarely breaks for big gains), but more reliable short yardage. Use this on 3rd and 1, or when you need to bleed clock in the fourth quarter. Tap A to maximize every carry.

**Short Pass / Long Pass:** The distinction matters. The short pass hits receivers on shorter routes with quicker development; the long pass targets deeper routes that take longer to develop. The CPU defender's coverage assignment is the critical variable — if the CPU picks the play you're running, your receiver gets tackled immediately. If the CPU picks the wrong play, your receiver is completely uncovered.

### The Play-Matching System

This is the core mechanic of Tecmo Super Bowl and it is simple once you understand it. Each offensive snap involves one offensive play selection and one defensive play selection. The game compares them:

- If the defense picks the correct offensive play (run when you ran, pass when you passed, and on passing plays, correctly identified short vs. long): the defense wins the play. Receivers get covered, running lanes collapse.
- If the defense picks incorrectly: the offense wins the play. On a mismatched run, your back gets into the open field. On a mismatched pass, your receiver is uncovered.

Against human opponents, this creates a genuine guessing game. Against the CPU, the CPU has internal tendencies that can be read and exploited. Watch what defensive play the CPU calls on each snap (it is briefly visible). You will notice patterns — the CPU over-adjusts to what you ran last, tends to call run defense on first down, pass defense on third and long. Once you identify the pattern, call the opposite.

### Exploiting Your Best Players

If you have Jerry Rice, every play selection that gets him the ball is correct. On passing plays, Rice is your target. Move the quarterback to extend the play, let Rice run his route, throw when he flashes.

If you have Bo Jackson, winning the run play matchup means a touchdown. Jackson with a clear lane does not get caught. Do not try to set up his carries with misdirection — just win the play matchup and hand off.

### The Two-Minute Drill

If you need to stop the clock, select any running play, snap the ball, and immediately run your quarterback out of bounds or into a dead zone. This spikes the clock effectively. You lose the down but stop time. Alternatively, any incomplete pass stops the clock — call your most reliable short pass on second down to spike, then run your actual play on third.

## Defensive Strategy

### Reading the CPU Offense

The CPU offensive play selection is not random — it is pattern-based and readable. After two or three drives against any CPU team, you will know their tendencies:

- Single-back formations generally indicate a running play
- Two-receiver splits often precede passing plays
- The CPU tends to run on first and ten, pass on second and long

Once you identify a tendency, call the matching defensive play. A matched defensive call against the CPU frequently produces a tackle for loss on runs or an interception on passes.

### Controlling Your Defender

Pick your fastest defender and control them directly. Lawrence Taylor is the obvious choice for the Giants. For other teams, find your fastest linebacker or defensive end. On run plays, attack the gap where the ball is headed. On pass plays, move toward the most dangerous receiver as soon as the snap fires.

### The Interception Setup

When you correctly call the matching pass defense (short or long), the CPU receiver gets hit immediately. If your controlled defender is near the ball, the interception animation triggers. You cannot guarantee interceptions — the game resolves this probabilistically — but positioning your fastest player near the CPU's best receiver on plays you correctly read dramatically increases turnover frequency.

### Stopping Bo Jackson

There is no clean answer. Jackson's Maximum Speed means he outpaces almost every defensive player in the game on a mismatched run play. Your only reliable options:

1. Correctly identify his run plays and call the matching defense. Jackson on a matched play does not get a lane and is tackled near the line of scrimmage.
2. Use a defender with very high Hitting Power — Christian Okoye types on defense — to make the tackle animation resolve faster when Jackson is caught.
3. Accept that you will give up big runs occasionally and score enough on offense to overcome it.

## Kicking Game

**Field Goals:** The power meter oscillates. Press A at the top for maximum distance. A fully powered kick goes approximately 55 yards reliably with a strong-legged kicker; shorter with a weak-legged one. Nick Lowery (Chiefs) is the highest-rated kicker. Accuracy is affected by the left-right oscillation of the meter — aim for center when the needle is centered. Kicks from the hash mark have a slight angle penalty.

**Kickoffs:** Use full power. Kick deep and force the CPU to drive the length of the field. Squib kicks are not available; every kickoff can be returned.

**Punts:** Full power punts give you maximum field position. The CPU returner's speed determines how dangerous returns are. Against teams with fast returners, kick to the sideline when possible by angling your kick direction slightly on the meter.

**Onside Kicks:** Select the onside kick option when prompted. CPU teams rarely recover them — their field position logic does not optimize for the situation. Use this to recover possession after a late deficit rather than gambling it early.

## Season Mode

### Before Each Game

Check your roster's condition ratings before kickoff. The game tracks each player's condition independently. A player listed in poor condition has reduced effective stats — lower speed, lower ball control, lower Hitting Power. Rest them if possible (the season schedule does not give you substitution options mid-game, but knowing who is compromised tells you how much to rely on them). Your star players' condition deteriorates faster because they touch the ball more.

### Managing the Schedule

You play 16 games in your conference and division schedule. The playoff structure mirrors the actual NFL: division winners plus wild cards. Win your division to guarantee a playoff spot. The CPU simulates all other games and those results are based on the teams' actual ratings — teams like the 49ers and Giants will win at high rates even against CPU opponents.

### Playoff Approach

By the playoffs, you should have a clear read on your team's strengths and weaknesses. If you have reached the playoffs with Bo Jackson, the strategy is unchanged: win run matchups, score often, live with defensive limitations. If you have reached the playoffs with the Giants, play the Lawrence Taylor defense aggressively — shade him to the side of the field where the CPU's best receiver lines up.

### Super Bowl

The Super Bowl is a single game with no margin for error. If you have played 16 regular season games and two rounds of playoffs, your reads on your own offensive and defensive play tendencies should be sharp. Trust them. The CPU opponent in the Super Bowl tends to have the highest-rated team not already eliminated — expect the 49ers, Bills, Giants, or Raiders if you did not control them yourself.

## Secrets and Known Exploits

**The Passing Window:** On passing plays, the game assigns a "target receiver" who flashes — this is who you throw to. Experienced players know that the game cycles briefly through receivers before settling on the open one. Throwing slightly late gives the deep receiver more time to separate, sometimes turning a short gain into a longer one. This requires feel and is specific to each team's receiver routes.

**Mash A on Carries:** Every carry, every time. When a defender makes contact, the game runs a break-tackle calculation based on your running back's attributes versus the tackler's. Mashing A submits break-tackle inputs rapidly. On players like Bo Jackson (Ball Control 81), this resolves in your favor at high rates. On lower-rated backs, it still helps — you will occasionally break free from defenders who should stop you.

**CPU Play Prediction:** After watching four or five of the CPU's plays in a row, call the opposite of whatever they just ran. The CPU has a strong recency-correction tendency — it shifts away from whatever you just defended successfully. If you stopped three runs, the CPU will pass. Call pass defense.

**Onside Kick Recovery Rate:** CPU teams recover onside kicks at a very low rate — well below the real-world average. In a one-score game with under two minutes remaining, the onside kick is a viable gamble. If you recover, you can potentially tie or take the lead without giving the CPU another possession.

**Bo Jackson with the Raiders:** If you play the Raiders, run Jackson on every first and second down regardless of the play matching risk. The expected value is so high on a clean lane that you absorb the matchup losses. When he breaks through, he scores. He scores more than often enough to justify the strategy.
