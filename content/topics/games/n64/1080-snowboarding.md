# 1080° Snowboarding (N64)

**Developer:** Nintendo EAD | **Publisher:** Nintendo | **Year:** 1998

---

## Introduction

1080° Snowboarding came out before the Tony Hawk series defined what an extreme sports game was, and it took a different route. Tony Hawk is a trick game with skating attached. 1080° is a *riding* game with tricks attached, and its priority is that the board feels like it is on snow — carving, edge grip, weight transfer, and the specific sensation of losing an edge and washing out.

That makes it less immediately generous than the arcade snowboarding games that followed. You cannot spam tricks. Landing badly costs you, hard, and the game expects you to learn to ride before it lets you show off.

It is also one of the best-looking N64 games, and the sound design — the board on packed powder, the scrape on ice — carried a surprising amount of the experience.

---

## Controls

- **Control Stick:** Steer. Also lean — forward for speed, back to slow and to set up a jump.
- **A:** Jump / ollie. Charge it by holding as you approach a lip.
- **B:** Grab. Combined with a stick direction, B is a specific trick.
- **Z:** Crouch / tuck. Reduces drag on straights and stabilises landings.
- **R:** Brake and carve hard.
- **C-buttons:** Rotation and spin direction during tricks.

### Riding, Not Tricking

The core skill is edge control. Holding the stick over commits the board to an edge; releasing flattens it. On a hard turn you want to carve — commit the edge and let the board arc — rather than steer, which scrubs speed.

**Landing is a skill in its own right.** You must be roughly straight and roughly level when you touch down. Landing sideways or nose-down washes you out and costs several seconds. The game gives you a moment of air control to straighten up before impact, and using it is the difference between a run that flows and a run of crashes. This is the thing that frustrates newcomers and it is entirely intentional.

## Riders

Five, each with distinct weight, speed, edge and trick statistics.

| Rider | Character |
|-------|-----------|
| Akari Hayami | Light, agile, best trick control, weakest in a straight line |
| Kensuke Kimachi | Balanced all-rounder; the sensible starting pick |
| Ricky Winterborn | Trick specialist, twitchy |
| Dion Blaster | Heavy, fast, poor turning |
| Rob Haywood | Heavy, stable, strong on rough terrain |

Boards are chosen separately and change the balance again — some favour speed, some carving, some tricks.

Start with **Kensuke** on a balanced board until you can hold a clean line, then move to a rider whose bias suits how you want to ride.

## Modes

**Match Race.** Head to head against a rival down a course. Beat all rivals to progress. This is the main career.

**Time Attack.** Solo against the clock.

**Trick Attack.** Score as many trick points as possible on a run. The scoring rewards variety — repeating the same trick devalues it — and rewards clean landings heavily.

**Contest.** Judged runs.

**Training.** The half-pipe and practice slopes. Use them.

**Two-player split screen.** Present and competent.

---

## The Courses

**Crystal Lake.** The introduction. Wide, forgiving, good for learning to carve.

**Crystal Peak.** Steeper and narrower.

**Golden Forest.** Trees, which are solid and which end runs. Line choice matters here more than speed.

**Mountain Village.** Structures and rails, tighter than anything before it.

**Air Make.** The trick course, built as a sequence of jumps.

**Dragon Cave.** Underground, dark, tight, and awkward.

**Deadly Fall.** The hardest course in the game and the reason the game has a reputation. Extremely steep, narrow, and full of drops where an imperfect landing ends the run. It is a genuine test of edge control and it is unlocked rather than available from the start.

## Tricks

Tricks are B plus a direction, with rotation added by the C-buttons and the stick. The names — Method Air, Indy, Stalefish, Roast Beef, Nose Grab, Tail Grab, Melon — are real snowboarding tricks rather than invented ones, and the game's spins go up to the 1080-degree triple rotation it is named after.

Scoring rewards:

- **Amplitude.** Higher jumps score more. Charge the ollie into the lip.
- **Variety.** The same trick repeated is worth progressively less.
- **Clean landings.** A trick you do not land scores nothing at all, and the game is strict about what counts as landed.

The 1080 itself requires the biggest jump on the biggest course with a full charge and a committed spin. Landing one is the game's actual final challenge.

---

## Playing Better

**Tuck on the straights.** Holding Z reduces drag noticeably and is free speed most players ignore.

**Carve, do not steer.** Committing an edge holds speed through a turn; sawing the stick left and right sheds it.

**Set up your landing in the air.** You have air control. Use it to straighten out before you touch down rather than to add one more half-rotation.

**In Match Race, take the clean line, not the fast line.** The rivals are beatable on consistency. A run with no washouts beats a faster run with two.

## Emulation Notes

1080° Snowboarding runs well under Mupen64Plus on Batocera and is not especially demanding.

The control caveat matters more than usual here, because the game reads the analogue stick's *magnitude* to determine how hard you are carving, and a modern thumbstick with a shorter throw makes fine edge control difficult. If the board feels like it snaps from flat to full-edge with nothing in between, increase the deadzone slightly and reduce sensitivity until you can find the middle. This game is unusually sensitive to getting that right.

The ROM is labelled **(JU)** in GoodN64 sets rather than (U) — the Japanese and North American releases share a cartridge with multi-language support, so there is no separate US-only dump to look for.
