# Lode Runner Twin: Justy & Liberty's Great Adventure (SNES)

**Developer:** Hudson Soft | **Publisher:** Hudson Soft | **Year:** 1994 (Japan only)

> **Library note:** No US SNES release exists for any Lode Runner title. This is the Japan cartridge "Lode Runner Twin" (internal header: `LODE RUNNER TWIN`, destination code 0x00 = Japan), documented exception per library rules.

---

## Introduction

Lode Runner Twin is the definitive 16-bit version of Broderbund's 1983 original. Hudson Soft had been the primary licensor for console Lode Runner since the Famicom days, and by 1994 they understood the formula as well as anyone. What they produced is not a reinvention — the core mechanic of digging holes, trapping enemies, and collecting gold before reaching the exit ladder is untouched — but a refinement. The sprite animation is smooth, the stage editor is present, the two-player mode works properly, and the sound design is a clear step up from anything on NES.

The "Twin" in the title refers to two playable characters: Justy, the standard male hero, and Liberty, a female character who plays identically in the main stages. Both can be selected before starting a game. In co-op and versus modes, each player picks their character and the differences in sprite design let you track two characters on screen without confusion.

The game ships with 50 main stages (more than the NES's 150 feels at first glance, though the SNES stages are larger and denser), plus the stage editor for creating and saving custom levels.

## Controls

- **D-Pad:** Move left and right. Press up or down at a ladder to climb.
- **Y:** Drill left. Digs a hole one tile to your left.
- **B:** Drill right. Digs a hole one tile to your right.
- **X / A:** No function in main gameplay.
- **Start:** Pause.
- **Select:** Suicide (restart the current stage). Use it when stuck in a position with no escape.

There is no jump. Movement is grid-based. Fall distance is unlimited; landing from any height is safe unless you land on an enemy or fall out of the screen.

## Core Mechanics

The underlying system is identical to the original Lode Runner. See the [NES Lode Runner guide](topics/games/nes/lode-runner) for the full mechanic breakdown. What follows covers the differences and additions specific to this version.

### Stage Scale

SNES stages are larger than NES stages and make heavier use of vertical space. The wider screen lets Hudson design stages with branching routes and multi-path solutions. Some stages that look like they have one answer have two.

### Enemy Behavior

Enemies in Lode Runner Twin are marginally smarter than their NES counterparts. They prioritize chasing the player more actively, will use ladders to cut off your route, and in later stages can dig holes themselves to escape traps. The pattern is the same as the NES game, but faster and more persistent. On the first dozen stages this is barely noticeable. By stage 30 you will be aware of it.

Enemies here also come in more than one type in some stages. Different sprites indicate different behaviors — some dig, some do not. Watch how enemies move in the first few seconds of a stage to identify which category they fall into before committing to a plan that assumes they are passive.

### Two-Player Mode

Two players simultaneously occupy the same stage. Co-op mode is cooperative: both players collect gold and either can reach the exit ladder to clear the stage. Versus mode is competitive: the player who collects more gold wins the stage. In both modes, each player can drill and can inadvertently trap their partner by filling a hole too early. Versus mode becomes vicious quickly — drilling a hole under your opponent while they are traversing a rope is a legitimate and deeply satisfying strategy.

### Stage Editor

Accessed from the main menu. You can place floor tiles (brick and concrete), ladders, ropes, gold, and enemy spawn points. Stages save to battery-backed SRAM. The editor works with a cursor controlled by the D-Pad; Y and B drill/place depending on context.

## Strategy

The fundamentals from the NES game apply completely: read the level before moving, control enemy position with holes, use the refill cycle deliberately, and drill every unmarked floor tile if gold is missing. The SNES version adds scale and speed but not new mechanics.

### Managing Two-Path Stages

When a stage has multiple routes to the exit ladder, pick the one that minimizes the time you spend exposed to enemies, not the one that collects gold fastest. Gold collection order matters less than position safety. A route that has a rope above the gold and a diggable floor below it is almost always better than a route that is all floor with enemies approaching.

### Enemies That Dig

If an enemy is digging out of a hole you created, you need to create a second hole adjacent to the first to push their exit toward a harmless position, or abandon that trap and route around them. You cannot out-dig an enemy that has learned to dig — the refill timing is the same for both holes.

### The Stage Editor as Learning Tool

If a stage is frustrating you, recreate the relevant section in the editor and experiment without the fail state. Understanding why a particular tile arrangement has only one solution is faster when you can modify the arrangement and see what changes.

## Stage Progression

Stages 1–10 are tutorials for players who have not played Lode Runner before. Stages 11–25 introduce digging enemies and two-path layouts. Stages 26–40 require multi-step trap sequences where you need to position two or more enemies with specific hole timing before the route to the gold opens. Stages 41–50 are generally the hardest, with tighter timing windows and fewer obvious landing zones.

The difficulty does not increase linearly — stage 35 is sometimes harder than stage 45 depending on what mechanics it uses. If a stage seems impossible, try another then return. The block of knowledge you need is sometimes in a different stage.
