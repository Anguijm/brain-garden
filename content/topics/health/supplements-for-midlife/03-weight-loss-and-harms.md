# The weight-loss aisle, and what can hurt

This is the chapter that matters most for the "not hurt" half of your question. The
weight-loss aisle is where the biggest promises and the biggest risks live, so it is worth
being blunt: almost nothing there works, and several things there can genuinely harm you.

## The "fat burner" aisle, honestly

**Berberine** ("nature's Ozempic," the ads say). FACT: a 2022 meta-analysis in *Frontiers in
Nutrition* reported that berberine lowered fasting blood glucose by 7.74 mg/dL (95 percent
confidence interval 4.70 to 10.79), HbA1c by 0.45 percentage points, and body weight by
0.84 kg. (Frontiers in Nutrition, 2022.) Assessment: the "Ozempic" framing is nonsense. The
actual drug drives 12 to 15 percent body-weight loss; berberine's 0.84 kg is a rounding
error. It is closer to a weak version of the diabetes drug metformin than to a weight-loss
drug. (An earlier version of this note gave the glucose effect as "roughly 15 mg/dL" and
HbA1c as "about 0.6 points," roughly double the real figures. Corrected here against the
paper.)

The real reason to be careful with berberine is drug interactions, but the specific one
matters. FACT: in human liver microsomes, berberine was a *selective*, quasi-irreversible
inhibitor of the drug-clearing enzyme CYP2D6, and it reduced the metabolic clearance of the
beta-blocker nebivolol; the same study found it did not markedly inhibit CYP1A2, 2C9, 2C19,
or 3A4. (Pharmacology study, 2020.) Assessment: CYP2D6 clears a long list of everyday
medicines, including many beta-blockers, several antidepressants, and some opioids, so
berberine is not a free "harmless natural supplement" to stack onto a medication list without
a pharmacist reading it first. But note what the evidence does *not* say: statins are cleared
mainly by CYP3A4, which this study found berberine leaves alone. (An earlier version of this
note claimed berberine "blocks the liver enzymes that clear about half of all prescription
drugs" and could "dangerously raise the levels of statins." The cited source contradicts
both, and the grounding gate blocked it. The statin warning was wrong and is gone.)

**Green tea extract.** FACT: the Cochrane review concluded that green tea preparations
"induce a small, statistically non-significant weight loss" that "is not likely to be
clinically important," a mean difference of 0.04 kg in the non-Japanese trials. (Cochrane.)
FACT: NIH LiverTox records more than 100 cases of clinically apparent liver injury attributed
to green tea extract, notes that doses equivalent to 800 mg of EGCG or more daily can be
associated with significant liver-enzyme elevations, and describes the most prominent
regulatory action as the withdrawal of the weight-loss product Exolise from Spain and France
in 2003. (NIH LiverTox.) Assessment: drinking green tea is fine; the "fat burner" extract
capsules are trivial benefit for a real liver risk. Skip them. (An earlier version said the
EU "capped and warned about" the extract. No cited source says that, so it is gone; the
Exolise withdrawal and the 800 mg EGCG threshold are what the record actually shows.)

**Glucomannan** (a soluble fiber). FACT: pooling eight randomized trials, Onakpoya and
colleagues found a mean weight difference of 0.22 kg (95 percent confidence interval -0.62 to
0.19), and concluded the evidence "does not show that glucomannan intake generates
statistically significant weight loss." (Systematic review, DARE record.) FACT: as a viscous
soluble fiber it does slow gastric emptying by forming a bulky gel, which increases satiety.
(Reviews of viscous fiber.) Assessment: so the mechanism is real, but it does not show up as
weight lost. It is the most harmless thing in the aisle and may
help as a hunger aid before meals, but take it with plenty of water (dry, it can swell and
choke you). Psyllium from the last chapter does much the same job and more. (An earlier
version claimed "around 1 kg at best." The pooled figure is 0.22 kg and not statistically
significant.)

**The blunt verdict.** Assessment: no over-the-counter fat burner meaningfully drives weight
loss. Across the whole category the honest number is 1 to 2 kg at most, often statistically
shaky and swamped by side effects. Your money is better spent on food, a coach, or, if it is
medically warranted, a real prescription from a doctor.

## What can actually hurt you

This is the list to simply avoid. FACT-backed harms, grouped by how much trouble they cause.

- **Stimulant "thermogenic" blends.** These stack several stimulants at once, typically
  caffeine plus something like yohimbine or synephrine. FACT: for yohimbine specifically, the
  poison-control review below logged racing heart in 43 percent of cases and high blood
  pressure in 25 percent. (Poison-control review.) Assessment: stacking stimulants that each
  raise heart rate and blood pressure is the concern, though no source here measured these
  particular combinations, so treat the stacking risk as reasoning rather than a measured
  finding. The higher-risk profile for these is a middle-aged person carrying extra weight and
  exercising hard, more so if blood pressure already runs high. A "proprietary blend" label
  also hides how much stimulant you are getting. Avoid them.
- **Yohimbine.** FACT: a review of poison-control calls identified 238 yohimbine cases, in
  which gastrointestinal distress appeared in 46 percent, racing heart in 43 percent, anxiety
  or agitation in 33 percent, and high blood pressure in 25 percent; yohimbine exposures were
  more likely than average to need management at a health-care facility (odds ratio 2.35).
  (Poison-control review.) Assessment: genuinely risky for anyone with heart or blood-pressure
  issues, and label doses are unreliable. Hard no. (An earlier version added "134
  hospitalizations." That number is in no cited source and has been removed; the 238 cases and
  the symptom percentages are what the review reports.)
- **DMAA.** FACT: DMAA (1,3-dimethylamylamine) is an amphetamine-like stimulant that the FDA
  declared illegal for use in dietary supplements in April 2013, and products containing it
  have been linked to liver injury, cardiac arrest, stroke, brain hemorrhage, and death
  following physical exertion. It hides on labels as
  "geranium extract," "Pelargonium graveolens," or "methylhexanamine." (DoD Operation
  Supplement Safety.)
- **DMHA.** FACT: DMHA is a *separate* stimulant, most often labeled "octodrine" or
  "2-aminoisoheptane." The FDA treats it as a new dietary ingredient lacking evidence of safe
  use, meaning supplements containing it are adulterated, and the cardiovascular concern so
  far rests on animal studies rather than the human death reports that DMAA carries. (DoD
  Operation Supplement Safety.) FACT: both DMAA and DMHA are on the DoD Prohibited Dietary
  Supplement Ingredients list. (Same source.) Assessment: if any of those names is on a label,
  put it down. (An earlier version merged these two into one entry and listed "octodrine" as a
  DMAA alias. Octodrine is DMHA. The two have different legal footing and different evidence
  behind them, so they are separated here.)
- **SARMs, prohormones, and "testosterone boosters."** FACT: the FDA states that SARMs "are
  considered unapproved drugs" that "cannot be legally marketed in the U.S. as a dietary
  supplement or drug at this time," and lists their reported harms as including increased risk
  of heart attack or stroke, liver injury and acute liver failure, psychosis, infertility, and
  testicular shrinkage. (FDA consumer update.) FACT: separately, NIH LiverTox records that anabolic androgenic steroids
  cause cholestasis, peliosis hepatis, and hepatic tumors, and that such steroids "have been
  found in some nutritional supplements available over-the-counter or via the internet,"
  including documented cholestatic injury from the bodybuilding product Superdrol
  (methasteron). (NIH LiverTox.) FACT: "testosterone boosters" mostly do not work; when
  Clemesha and colleagues evaluated 50 of them in *World Journal of Men's Health* (2020),
  only 24.8 percent had published data showing any increase in testosterone, 10.1 percent
  had data showing a decrease, and no data at all existed for 61.5 percent. (Clemesha 2020.) Assessment: the boosters are a waste of money, and
  the SARMs and prohormones are the opposite of "help not hurt." If you have real
  low-testosterone symptoms, that is a blood test and a doctor, not a store shelf. (An earlier
  version attributed the prohormone liver-injury link to the FDA. No cited source did that, so
  the prohormone claim is now carried by LiverTox on androgenic steroids in supplements, which
  does.)
- **Mega-doses of ordinary vitamins and minerals.** FACT: more is not better here, and it can
  be toxic. High-dose vitamin A damages the liver. A large trial found that 400 IU of vitamin
  E a day raised prostate-cancer risk about 17 percent in healthy men. Never take iron without
  a proven deficiency, since it is dangerous if you have an undiagnosed iron-overload
  condition. High-dose niacin harms the liver, and too much zinc causes copper deficiency.
  (NIH LiverTox; SELECT trial; NIH ODS.) Assessment: stay within the amounts on a normal
  label. A basic multivitamin is a ceiling, not a starting point to build on.

## Because the industry is barely regulated

FACT: the FDA does not check supplements for safety or quality before they are sold. Worse,
products get spiked with hidden drugs. One review found hundreds of adulterated supplements,
and in FDA marketplace testing, every one of 26 sampled Amazon products contained an
undeclared drug ingredient. (FDA; JAMA Network Open.) Assessment: the practical defense is
simple, buy only products carrying a **NSF Certified for Sport**, **Informed Sport**, or
**USP Verified** seal, which means the contents were actually tested against the label. Treat
the weight-loss and "fat burner" aisles as guilty until proven otherwise. (A seal proves the
product is clean, not that it works.)

## When to see a doctor, not a supplement store

Assessment: the single most useful thing you can do is not buy a supplement, it is get a
simple blood panel and a checkup. FACT: for a man your age and weight, that reasonably
includes blood pressure, a fasting lipid (cholesterol) panel, fasting glucose or HbA1c, and
liver and kidney panels, with vitamin D added, and testosterone only if you have symptoms.
(US Preventive Services Task Force.) Those numbers tell you what actually needs fixing, and
which of the supplements here are safe for you.

And go to a doctor promptly, not a store, if you get chest pain, a racing or irregular
heartbeat, or shortness of breath during exercise, or if your blood pressure stays high. And
if you feel unusually tired or nauseated, or notice yellowing skin or dark urine after
starting a supplement, stop it and get your liver checked.

## The honest bottom line

Assessment: pulling the whole section together, the money-and-effort order for you is clear
and a little boring: **lift weights, eat enough protein, sleep well, and stay in a calorie
deficit.** On top of that, a short cheap stack (creatine, protein powder, vitamin D if you
are low, psyllium) is genuinely worth it, a few things are worth a careful try, and the
loud, expensive, exciting-sounding products are the ones to walk past. You cannot supplement
your way out of the basics, but you can add a few real helpers without hurting yourself, as
long as you get your bloodwork, check your medications, and buy tested products.

This is general information, not medical advice; talk to your doctor or pharmacist before
starting supplements or a new exercise program, especially with any health conditions or
medications.
