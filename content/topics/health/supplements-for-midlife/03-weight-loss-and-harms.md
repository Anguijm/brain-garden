# The weight-loss aisle, and what can hurt

This is the chapter that matters most for the "not hurt" half of your question. The
weight-loss aisle is where the biggest promises and the biggest risks live, so it is worth
being blunt: almost nothing there works, and several things there can genuinely harm you.

## The "fat burner" aisle, honestly

**Berberine** ("nature's Ozempic," the ads say). FACT: it has real but small effects on
blood sugar (fasting glucose down roughly 15 mg/dL, HbA1c down about 0.6 points) and a tiny
effect on weight, around 1 kg. (Meta-analyses.) Assessment: the "Ozempic" framing is
nonsense. The actual drug drives 12 to 15 percent body-weight loss; berberine's ~1 kg is a
rounding error. It is closer to a weak version of the diabetes drug metformin than to a
weight-loss drug.

FACT: and here is the real reason to be careful, berberine blocks the liver enzymes (the CYP
family) that clear about half of all prescription drugs, so it can dangerously raise the
levels of statins, and add to blood-pressure and diabetes medications. (Pharmacology
reviews.) Assessment: for a 48-year-old who might be on a statin or a blood-pressure pill,
berberine is exactly the wrong "harmless natural supplement" to add without a pharmacist
checking your list first.

**Green tea extract.** FACT: the weight effect is essentially zero (a Cochrane review found
no meaningful weight loss), and concentrated extract carries a real, if uncommon, risk of
liver injury, which is why the EU capped and warned about it. (Cochrane; NIH LiverTox;
EFSA.) Assessment: drinking green tea is fine; the "fat burner" extract capsules are trivial
benefit for a real liver risk. Skip them.

**Glucomannan** (a soluble fiber). FACT: modest and mixed, around 1 kg at best, but it does
genuinely curb appetite by swelling in your stomach. (Reviews.) Assessment: the most
harmless thing in the aisle, useful as a hunger aid before meals, but take it with plenty of
water (dry, it can swell and choke you). Psyllium from the last chapter does much the same
job and more.

**The blunt verdict.** Assessment: no over-the-counter fat burner meaningfully drives weight
loss. Across the whole category the honest number is 1 to 2 kg at most, often statistically
shaky and swamped by side effects. Your money is better spent on food, a coach, or, if it is
medically warranted, a real prescription from a doctor.

## What can actually hurt you

This is the list to simply avoid. FACT-backed harms, grouped by how much trouble they cause.

- **Stimulant "thermogenic" blends.** FACT: these stack stimulants (caffeine plus things like
  yohimbine and synephrine) that raise blood pressure and heart rate and can trigger
  palpitations, anxiety, and irregular heartbeats. (NSF found banned stimulants in weight-
  loss supplements.) Assessment: the highest-risk person for these is exactly you, middle-
  aged, overweight, likely higher blood pressure, and exercising. A "proprietary blend" label
  even hides how much stimulant you are getting. Avoid them.
- **Yohimbine.** FACT: documented harms include high blood pressure, racing heart, anxiety,
  and in bad cases heart attacks and dangerous rhythms; one poison-control review logged 238
  bad reactions and 134 hospitalizations, and the doses on labels are wildly unreliable.
  (Poison-control review; DoD safety office.) Assessment: genuinely dangerous for anyone with
  heart or blood-pressure issues. Hard no.
- **DMAA and DMHA.** FACT: these are amphetamine-like stimulants the FDA has declared illegal
  and unsafe after cardiovascular events and deaths, yet they still turn up in gray-market
  pre-workouts under odd names like "octodrine" or "geranium extract." (FDA; DoD.) Assessment:
  if you see those on a label, put it down.
- **SARMs, prohormones, and "testosterone boosters."** FACT: SARMs and prohormones are
  unapproved drugs (not legal supplements) that the FDA links to liver injury and higher
  heart-attack and stroke risk. And "testosterone boosters" mostly do not work, one analysis
  of 50 products found only about a quarter contained anything shown to raise testosterone at
  all. (FDA; product analysis.) Assessment: the boosters are a waste of money, and the
  SARMs/prohormones are the opposite of "help not hurt." If you have real low-testosterone
  symptoms, that is a blood test and a doctor, not a store shelf.
- **Mega-doses of ordinary vitamins and minerals.** FACT: more is not better here, and it can
  be toxic. High-dose vitamin A damages the liver. A large trial found that 400 IU of vitamin
  E a day raised prostate-cancer risk about 17 percent in healthy men. Never take iron without
  a proven deficiency, since it is dangerous if you have an undiagnosed iron-overload
  condition. High-dose niacin harms the liver, and too much zinc causes copper deficiency.
  (NIH LiverTox; SELECT trial; NIH ODS.) Assessment: stay within the amounts on a normal
  label. A basic multivitamin is a ceiling, not a starting point to build on.

## Because the industry is barely regulated

FACT: the FDA does not check supplements for safety or quality before they are sold. Worse,
products get spiked with hidden drugs. One review found hundreds of adulterated supplements,
and in FDA marketplace testing, every one of 26 sampled Amazon products contained an
undeclared drug ingredient. (FDA; JAMA Network Open.) Assessment: the practical defense is
simple, buy only products carrying a **NSF Certified for Sport**, **Informed Sport**, or
**USP Verified** seal, which means the contents were actually tested against the label. Treat
the weight-loss and "fat burner" aisles as guilty until proven otherwise. (A seal proves the
product is clean, not that it works.)

## When to see a doctor, not a supplement store

Assessment: the single most useful thing you can do is not buy a supplement, it is get a
simple blood panel and a checkup. FACT: for a man your age and weight, that reasonably
includes blood pressure, a fasting lipid (cholesterol) panel, fasting glucose or HbA1c, and
liver and kidney panels, with vitamin D added, and testosterone only if you have symptoms.
(US Preventive Services Task Force.) Those numbers tell you what actually needs fixing, and
which of the supplements here are safe for you.

And go to a doctor promptly, not a store, if you get chest pain, a racing or irregular
heartbeat, or shortness of breath during exercise, or if your blood pressure stays high. And
if you feel unusually tired or nauseated, or notice yellowing skin or dark urine after
starting a supplement, stop it and get your liver checked.

## The honest bottom line

Assessment: pulling the whole section together, the money-and-effort order for you is clear
and a little boring: **lift weights, eat enough protein, sleep well, and stay in a calorie
deficit.** On top of that, a short cheap stack (creatine, protein powder, vitamin D if you
are low, psyllium) is genuinely worth it, a few things are worth a careful try, and the
loud, expensive, exciting-sounding products are the ones to walk past. You cannot supplement
your way out of the basics, but you can add a few real helpers without hurting yourself, as
long as you get your bloodwork, check your medications, and buy tested products.

This is general information, not medical advice; talk to your doctor or pharmacist before
starting supplements or a new exercise program, especially with any health conditions or
medications.
