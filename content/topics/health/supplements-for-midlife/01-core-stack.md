# The core stack that actually works

If you buy nothing else, this is the short list with real evidence behind it for someone
your age who is training. All of it is cheap, and most of it is very safe. The effects are
still modest, so treat these as small helpers on top of the training and diet, not
replacements for them.

| Supplement | Dose | Best for | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Creatine monohydrate | 3-5 g/day | strength, muscle, maybe memory | top pick; cheapest and safest |
| Protein powder (whey) | to hit your target | keeping muscle while losing fat | it is food, not a drug |
| Vitamin D3 | 1,000-2,000+ IU/day | correcting a deficiency | test your blood level first |
| Psyllium fiber | ~7-10 g/day, with water | cholesterol, blood sugar, appetite | quietly great value |
| Magnesium (glycinate) | 200-400 mg/day | blood pressure, if diet is low | modest; skip if kidney disease |
| Omega-3 (EPA+DHA) | 1-2 g/day | high triglycerides, no fish | situational, not a must |

## Creatine, the top pick

FACT: creatine helps your muscles do a little more work each session, which adds up to more
strength and muscle over a training block. In a pooled analysis of older adults doing
resistance training, creatine added about **1.4 kg of lean tissue** over placebo, plus small
strength gains. (Chilibeck 2017 meta-analysis.) It is the most studied and most reliable
legal training supplement there is. (ISSN position stand.)

Assessment: for you, it is the easy first pick, cheap, safe, and it makes the training you
are already doing pay off a bit more. There may be a bonus, too. FACT: a 2024 review found
creatine modestly improved memory, with the effect possibly larger when you are stressed or
short on sleep. (Frontiers in Nutrition 2024.) Do not buy it for your brain, but it is a
plausible extra.

FACT: the old "creatine hurts your kidneys" worry is a misunderstanding. It slightly raises
a blood marker called creatinine because that is its breakdown product, not because the
kidneys are damaged; decades of research show no harm to healthy kidneys at normal doses.
(Antonio 2021.) Assessment: two honest notes. If you have diagnosed kidney disease, clear it
with your doctor and mention creatine before any kidney blood test. And expect a quick 1 to
2 kg jump on the scale in the first weeks, that is water pulled into the muscle, not fat, so
judge progress by the mirror and your waist, not the scale.

Dose: 3 to 5 grams a day of plain creatine monohydrate, any time. Skip the fancy "HCl" or
"buffered" versions; monohydrate is the studied, cheapest form.

## Protein powder, a tool not a magic ingredient

Assessment: protein powder is just food, a convenient way to hit a daily protein target you
would otherwise miss. Its whole value is helping you get enough protein, which matters a lot
for you: FACT: eating more protein while losing weight protects the muscle you would
otherwise lose, and protein is the most filling nutrient, which makes a calorie deficit
easier to stick to. (ESPEN meta-analysis 2024; ISSN.) Keeping muscle while you drop fat is
one of the highest-value things you can do at 48.

FACT/Assessment: how much protein is where the marketing misleads. General guidance is about
1.6 grams per kilogram of body weight, higher while dieting. But for an overweight person,
using your full scale weight overshoots, because fat tissue needs little protein. The
practical fix: base your target on your **goal weight**, not your current weight. A man who
weighs 110 kg but whose healthy target is around 80 kg is better aiming for roughly 130 to
160 grams of protein a day (built around the 80 kg) than the 200-plus grams the raw formula
would demand. Whey makes hitting that realistic without extra calories.

Safety: fine for healthy kidneys (the "protein wrecks your kidneys" claim does not apply
without existing kidney disease). If dairy bothers you, use whey **isolate** or a plant
protein.

## Vitamin D3, if you are low

Assessment: vitamin D only helps if you are actually deficient, and it does little for
someone already topped up. FACT: deficiency is common, and more common in people carrying
extra weight, because vitamin D gets diluted into fat tissue. (NIH Office of Dietary
Supplements.) So you have a better-than-average chance of being low.

FACT: be realistic about what it does. Correcting a true deficiency is worthwhile and can
help muscle and immunity. But a large trial of 2,000 IU a day found **no** reduction in
heart disease or cancer, so do not take it expecting those. (VITAL trial.) The move is to
test your blood level (the test is called 25-OH-D) and dose to the number.

Dose: usually 1,000 to 2,000 IU a day to maintain, more for a short while if you are
genuinely low. FACT: the safe daily upper limit is 4,000 IU; real toxicity takes chronic
mega-doses. (NIH ODS.) Skip the giant monthly "boluses."

## Psyllium fiber, quietly one of the best buys

FACT: psyllium is a cheap soluble fiber that does three useful things for you at once. It
lowers LDL cholesterol by around 7 percent, lowers blood sugar and HbA1c (most in people
with prediabetes or metabolic issues), and blunts appetite to help a calorie deficit.
(Meta-analyses of psyllium on lipids and glycemic control; the FDA even allows a
heart-health claim for it.) Assessment: for an overweight man eating a typical low-fiber
diet, that is a lot of value from something that costs almost nothing.

Safety, two real rules: FACT: always take it with a full glass of water, because a
gel-forming fiber taken dry can cause choking or a blockage. And separate it from your
medications by a couple of hours, since it can slow their absorption. Start with a small
dose and build up to avoid gas.

## Magnesium, if your diet is light on it

FACT: magnesium is an essential mineral many people fall short on, and supplementing lowers
blood pressure a small amount overall, with a bigger effect in people who are already high
or truly deficient (up to about 8 mmHg systolic in those groups). (Meta-analysis of 38
trials.) Assessment: worthwhile mainly if you do not eat many greens, nuts, and beans, or if
your blood pressure is creeping up. The popular sleep benefit exists but the evidence is
weak, so do not count on it.

Dose: 200 to 400 mg of elemental magnesium a day; choose **glycinate** (gentle) or citrate,
not oxide (mostly a laxative). Safety: the usual side effect is loose stools. FACT:
important, if you have kidney disease, do not supplement magnesium without medical
supervision, since failing kidneys cannot clear the excess. (NIH ODS.)

## Omega-3, situational

FACT: fish oil (the fats EPA and DHA) reliably lowers triglycerides, so it is worth it if
your bloodwork shows those are high, or if you just do not eat fish. But be clear-eyed about
the heart claims: a large Cochrane review found ordinary fish-oil capsules have little or no
effect on heart attacks or deaths in the general population. (Cochrane 2020.) The famous
heart benefit came from a high-dose prescription drug in high-risk patients, not a drugstore
softgel.

Dose: about 1 to 2 grams a day of combined EPA plus DHA, and read the label, a "1,000 mg
fish oil" pill often has only ~300 mg of the actual omega-3s. FACT: one real caution, a
2021 meta-analysis of cardiovascular-outcome trials (Gencer, *Circulation*) found that
fish-oil supplements raised the risk of a heart-rhythm problem called atrial fibrillation,
and the rise was larger in trials dosing above 1 gram a day (hazard ratio 1.49) than at or
below it (1.12), a statistically significant dose relationship, so do not overdo it. Eating
fatty fish twice a week is a fine substitute.

## What about HMB and leucine?

You may notice this stack leaves out two things a "muscle" supplement pushes hard, standalone
**leucine** and **HMB**, like the Apex product in the
[muscle-and-aging note](topics/health/muscle-protein-synthesis-aging/). That is on purpose.

Assessment: leucine is the amino acid that flips on muscle-building, but you already get
plenty of it from protein, and whey is especially rich in it. FACT: leucine taken on its own,
across 17 trials, produced no meaningful gain in muscle or strength. (Leucine meta-analysis,
*Frontiers in Nutrition* 2022.) So hitting your protein target does the job; a separate
leucine pill adds little.

Assessment: HMB is made from leucine and mostly slows muscle *breakdown*. FACT: its evidence
is modest and mostly about sparing muscle during illness or bed rest, and much of the benefit
disappears once you are training and eating enough protein. (Deutz 2013 on bed rest; HMB in
sarcopenia meta-analysis.) There is a thin case for it while dieting, since it is anti-catabolic, but resistance
training plus enough protein already protect your muscle in a deficit, and the studied dose is
3 grams (the product had only 1). Assessment: for a healthy, training, well-fed 48-year-old,
whey plus creatine plus enough protein covers what leucine and HMB are sold to do, more cheaply
and with better evidence. Save HMB for a genuine catabolic situation, like serious illness or
bed rest, at the real 3-gram dose. Neither one raises uric acid, if gout is a concern (see the
[gout chapter](05-gout-and-uric-acid)).
