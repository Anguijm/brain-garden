# Eating through a UC medication pause

Written for a specific situation: a woman in her late thirties with ulcerative colitis who is
stopping her UC drug for two to three months, while continuing treatment for breast cancer she
is in remission from. Cruciferous vegetables — broccoli, cabbage and their relatives — have
reliably set her off in the past.

This is **dietary information, not a treatment plan**. Diet does not replace the drug that is
being paused, and the plan for that pause belongs to her gastroenterologist and oncologist
together. What follows is what the research actually supports, and where the two sets of dietary
advice she is now under pull in different directions.

**A note on the drug name.** The medicine was given as "Rimvac". No drug by that name appears in
the UC literature; the near-certain match is **Rinvoq (upadacitinib)**, a JAK inhibitor approved
for ulcerative colitis. That would also explain the pause: Rinvoq's FDA label carries a
malignancy warning and says to weigh benefit against risk "particularly in patients with a known
malignancy". Worth confirming the spelling, because if it is a different drug the timing advice
below may not fit.

## The one thing to understand before anything else

**FACT.** A low-FODMAP diet reliably reduces pain and bloating in people with UC in remission who
have IBS-like symptoms. In a randomised controlled study, eliminating FODMAPs for two weeks
significantly decreased pain and bloating scores. **And there is no evidence it reduces
intestinal inflammation.** Reviewers describe it as a short-term option for symptom control, with
an "absence of data demonstrating that it reduces intestinal inflammation".

**Assessment, and this is the important part.** Feeling better and being better are two different
things in UC, and during a medication pause that gap becomes dangerous. A diet that quiets
symptoms while inflammation climbs will hide exactly the signal she and her doctors need to see.

The practical consequence: **use diet to feel well, but do not use "I feel fine" as evidence the
pause is going safely.** That is what stool calprotectin testing is for, and it is worth asking
whether it should be checked more often than usual while she is off the drug.

## What the evidence actually supports

### The Mediterranean pattern has the best evidence, and it is not a restrictive diet

**FACT.** In a 12-week randomised controlled trial of 28 adults with quiescent UC, those assigned
a Mediterranean diet pattern were compared against people continuing their usual diet. At week
12, **20% of the Mediterranean group had faecal calprotectin above 100 μg/g, versus 75% of the
control group.** Faecal calprotectin is a stool marker of gut inflammation, so this is an
objective measure, not a symptom score.

**Assessment.** This is a small trial and it studied maintenance in people already in remission,
which is precisely the situation of a planned drug pause. It is the closest thing to a
"maintenance diet" the evidence offers, and it earns its place by being *additive* — it is mostly
about what to eat more of, which makes it sustainable in a way that elimination diets are not.

In practice that means: olive oil as the main fat, fish several times a week, legumes, nuts,
whole grains, plenty of vegetables and fruit, modest poultry and dairy, very little red or
processed meat.

### Ultra-processed food and emulsifiers are worth avoiding, with honest caveats

**FACT.** In a randomised placebo-controlled trial, adults with UC who took **carrageenan**
capsules were more likely to relapse over a year than those on a carrageenan-free diet. The trial
had **12 participants**, which is very small.

**FACT.** Carboxymethylcellulose (CMC) and polysorbate-80 — two common food emulsifiers — promote
intestinal inflammation in animal models, with CMC producing the more aggressive colitis, worst
in the colon and rectum.

**Assessment.** The mechanism is plausible and the direction is consistent, but the human evidence
is thin and the mouse work does not automatically transfer. This is a reasonable thing to act on
during a higher-risk window, and not a reason for alarm about a single ice cream. Reading labels
for carrageenan, CMC (E466) and polysorbate-80 costs nothing.

## Her specific trigger: it is the form, not the vegetable

**FACT.** Cruciferous vegetables are high in **insoluble fibre**, which stays intact through the
gut and can irritate an inflamed colon, worsening diarrhoea, gas and bloating during a flare.
They also contain **raffinose**, a complex sugar that ferments and produces gas. Broccoli is split
between the two: the florets are largely soluble fibre, the **stems** are the insoluble part.
**Cooking softens the fibre and breaks down some of the raffinose.**

**Assessment — this is the most immediately useful thing in this note.** "Broccoli and cabbage
upset me" is usually not a verdict on the vegetable. It is a verdict on *raw, stalky, skin-on,
large-portion* vegetables. The same plant behaves differently depending on four things:

| lever | harder on the gut | easier on the gut |
|---|---|---|
| **cooking** | raw | steamed, boiled or roasted until properly soft |
| **part** | stems, stalks, cores, skins | florets, tender leaves, peeled flesh |
| **texture** | whole pieces | blended into soup, puréed |
| **portion** | a large serving | a small serving alongside other food |

So instead of striking cruciferous vegetables off the list — which costs real nutritional value,
and cuts against the cancer-survivorship advice below — the experiment worth running is: well-cooked
broccoli **florets** only, no stems, small portion, ideally blended into a soup. If that is
tolerated, the problem was never broccoli.

**Assessment.** If cruciferous vegetables fail even cooked, puréed and small, the useful next step
is not further elimination but structured reintroduction with a dietitian, because the
alternative — a steadily shrinking list of safe foods — is a well-recognised trap in IBD and
carries its own costs.

## Where the two sets of advice meet

She is now under two dietary regimes at once. It is worth being explicit about where they agree
and where they conflict, because the conflict is narrower than it looks.

**FACT.** Guidance for cancer survivors, including the American Cancer Society's, recommends a
diet high in fibre, vegetables, fruit and whole grains, and limiting alcohol, processed meat,
ultra-processed food and sugar-sweetened drinks.

**They agree on almost everything.** Both point at the same Mediterranean-shaped plate: plants,
fish, olive oil, whole grains, legumes; away from processed meat and ultra-processed food.
Alcohol is discouraged by both — by cancer guidance on risk grounds, and because it is a common
UC symptom trigger.

**They conflict on one thing: fibre.** Survivorship advice says eat plenty of it. UC advice says
insoluble fibre irritates an inflamed colon.

**Assessment — the conflict is about form, not quantity.** She can meet both by getting fibre from
sources that are *soluble and soft* rather than *insoluble and coarse*: oats, peeled and cooked
fruit, well-cooked vegetables without skins or stalks, puréed legumes, bananas. That is a
substantial fibre intake in a form an inflamed colon tolerates. The instruction "eat more fibre"
and the instruction "avoid rough fibre" are only contradictory if fibre is treated as one thing.

**On soy, since it comes up.** **FACT.** Evidence indicates whole soy foods can be safely included
after a breast cancer diagnosis, including oestrogen-receptor-positive disease, at moderate
intakes of roughly one to three servings a day, and some data suggest lower recurrence risk.
**Assessment.** This matters here because tofu and soy milk are soft, low-residue protein sources
that suit a cautious gut, so ruling them out on outdated worry would remove genuinely useful
options.

## During the pause specifically

**Assessment throughout this section — there is no trial of "what to eat while your UC drug is
withdrawn", so this is reasoning from the evidence above rather than evidence about this
situation.**

- **Do not start the pause and a new diet on the same day.** If something changes, there will be
  no way to tell which change caused it. Settle the diet first, then pause the drug.
- **Keep a simple daily log** — stool frequency, blood present or not, urgency, pain, plus what
  was eaten. Two lines a day. During a pause, the trend matters more than any single day, and
  memory is unreliable once someone is worried.
- **Front-load the boring version.** The weeks just after stopping are the ones to be most
  conservative in: cooked over raw, peeled over skin-on, small and frequent over large meals.
  Loosen later if things stay quiet.
- **Keep protein up.** Both conditions favour it, appetite often falls when the gut is unhappy,
  and losing weight and muscle during a pause helps nothing. Fish, eggs, tofu, poultry, smooth
  nut butters, dairy if tolerated.
- **Watch for iron.** UC causes blood loss and is a common cause of iron deficiency; fatigue gets
  attributed to everything else. Worth having checked rather than guessed at.

## When diet is not the answer

Get medical advice promptly rather than adjusting food, if any of these appear: **blood in the
stool that is new or increasing, more than about six stools a day, waking at night to open the
bowels, fever, severe abdominal pain, or unintended weight loss.** These are flare signals, and a
flare during a planned drug pause is information the treating team needs quickly. No dietary
change substitutes for that call.

## What the evidence does not say

**Assessment, stated plainly because the internet claims otherwise.**

- No diet has been shown to *induce* remission in adult UC on its own.
- No diet prevents relapse when a maintenance drug is withdrawn. That is not a studied question.
- The Mediterranean trial above had 28 people; the carrageenan trial had 12. These are real
  findings, and they are small. Treat them as reasons for a sensible default, not as guarantees.
- Anything sold as a "colitis cure diet" is ahead of the evidence.
